SD-OCT is attractive because it eliminates the need for depth scanning, which in TD-OCT is performed usually by mechanical means. SD methods can be implemented in two formats: (i) spectrometer based (SB) or (ii) by using a tunable laser or a swept source (SS). Each method, SB-OCT or SS-OCT has its own merits and deficiencies. The depth resolution achieved depends on the bandwidth of the optical source in the TD-OCT and SB-OCT and on the tuning bandwidth in the SS-OCT.</p><p>A TD-OCT setup is equipped with an optical source and an interferometer, as shown schematically in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="fig02">Figure 2</xref> as a Michelson interferometer, where a <italic toggle="yes">Reference Mirror</italic> and an optical <italic toggle="yes">Splitter</italic> are used to produce a reference beam. A <italic toggle="yes">Microscopy Interface</italic> optics is employed to convey light from the <italic toggle="yes">Splitter</italic> to, and from the <italic toggle="yes">Object</italic> to be examined, up to a <italic toggle="yes">Processing Unit</italic>, that performs interference of light between the reference beam and the beam returned by the <italic toggle="yes">Object</italic>, as well as processing of the interference signal and its analysis. The path traversed by the object wave from the splitter to the object and back represents the object path length. The path traversed by the reference wave from the <italic toggle="yes">Splitter</italic> to the <italic toggle="yes">Reference Mirror</italic> and back represents the reference path length. An optical path difference (OPD) in the interferometer is defined as OPD =|object path length – reference path length|.</p><fig id="fig02" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 2</label><caption><p>TD-OCT setup.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="jmi0247-0209-f2.jpg"/></fig><sec><title>TD-OCT</title><p>As shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="fig02">Figure 2</xref>, a TD-OCT setup uses a <italic toggle="yes">Broadband optical source</italic> and the <italic toggle="yes">Processing Unit</italic> employs a <italic toggle="yes">Photodetector</italic>. The principle of operation is based on partial coherence interferometry, where the <italic toggle="yes">Photodetector</italic> senses variations in the interference result as long as the OPD is less than the coherence length, cl, of the <italic toggle="yes">Broadband source</italic>. Let us say that the <italic toggle="yes">Object</italic> is made from a structure of layers at different depths, as illustrated in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="fig03">Figure 3</xref>. Each layer returns a replica of the incoming wavetrain, delayed correspondingly. By scanning the reference path length through moving the <italic toggle="yes">Reference Mirror</italic>, the layer satisfying the coherence gate condition, OPD &lt; cl, can be selected. In <xref ref-type="fig" rid="fig03">Figure 3</xref>, the layer selected is that where its backscattered wavetrain is matched temporally by the reference wavetrain. Maxima of interference are obtained for each scatterer in depth that satisfies the condition OPD = 0. By scanning the OPD, a TD-OCT system outputs a reflectivity profile in depth, termed as an A-scan. So far, such a configuration is that of a low coherence interferometer widely used in sensing. OCT was invented by adding a transversal scanner to the object arm of a low coherence interferometer to scan the beam laterally over the object (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="b11">Huang <italic toggle="yes">et al.</italic>, 1991</xref>). By collecting adjacent A-scans for successive pixels along a transversal coordinate, a cross-section image is obtained, termed as a B-scan (similar to the terminology used in ultrasound imaging). This imaging method has become what is now known as longitudinal (or axial) TD-OCT. Another version of TD-OCT is <italic toggle="yes">en face</italic> OCT, based on one-dimensional reflectivity profiles (T-scans), collected by flying the spot transversally, while maintaining the axial coordinate fixed (<italic toggle="yes">Reference mirror</italic> at rest). In this case, a B-scan image is constructed from many T-scans repeated for successive pixels in depth, in other words, scanning fast laterally and slow axially using the <italic toggle="yes">Reference mirror</italic>.
