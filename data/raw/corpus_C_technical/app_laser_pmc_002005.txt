# PMC: low level laser therapy healing review — Overview

[<xref rid="b2-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there were more than 422 million adults with DM worldwide in 2014 [<xref rid="b2-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. This prevalence continues to increase and is expected to reach 642 million by 2040 [<xref rid="b3-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>]. One of the microvascular complications of DM is diabetic foot ulcer (DFU), which is associated with increased mortality from serious infections [<xref rid="b4-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. Epidemiological studies show that 20% of patients with DM have DFU, which, if left untreated, leads to amputation, reducing the functional status of patients [<xref rid="b4-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>].</p><p>Low-level laser therapy (LLLT) is one of the modalities that uses photons at non-thermal temperatures to alter biological activity. Currently, the main uses of LLLT are to reduce pain and inflammation, trigger tissue healing and regeneration, and prevent tissue damage [<xref rid="b5-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. Numerous studies have demonstrated how LLLT helps patients with DFU wounds heal [<xref rid="b6-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. LLLT is a minimally invasive, easy-to-use, and inexpensive therapeutic option for patients with DFU. In patients with DM, there is an imbalance in metalloprotease levels that can degrade the extracellular matrix, reduce tissue extensibility, and ultimately slow down wound healing [<xref rid="b7-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. LLLT is thought to trigger DFU wound healing by increasing the stability of extracellular structures and stimulating growth factors needed to facilitate wound healing [<xref rid="b7-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>].</p><p>The use of LLLT in patients with uncontrolled diabetes with chronic wounds has not been extensively studied. The current management of DFUs is debridement, wound dressing, and antibiotics [<xref rid="b8-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. Patients with uncontrolled diabetes may not respond to traditional treatments, which is a substantial risk factor for poor wound healing [<xref rid="b9-amjcaserep-25-e944106" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. There is a need for alternative treatments that can promote wound healing in these patients, and LLLT can have a role to play in this regard.</p><p>This case study intends to investigate the function of LLLT in the treatment of diabetic wounds in a patient with uncontrolled diabetes. A patient with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes who appeared with a right foot ulcer that had been persistent for a long time is described in the case study. Despite treatment with antibiotics and wound dressings, the ulcer did not improve, and the patient was referred for LLLT treatment. The case report describes the treatment protocol, the patient’s response to treatment, and the follow-up outcomes.</p><p>The results of this case report imply that LLLT can be a successful choice for treating chronic wounds in people with uncontrolled diabetes. The case report also highlights the need for further research to explore the potential of LLLT in improving wound healing and glycemic control in diabetic patients with chronic wounds.</p></sec><sec sec-type="cases"><title>Case Report</title><p>A 55-year-old male patient was referred to the physical medicine and rehabilitation clinic in Makassar, Indonesia with a history of type 2 DM, diabetic neuropathy, and DFU. The patient had DM for 2 years and was taking metformin 1500 mg/day. The patient had a grade II diabetic ulcer (Wagner grading system) on the first toe of the right foot for the last 3 months. The ulcer had been treated with conventional wound care, with non-adherent saline gauze dressing, and there was no improvement. At the time of the initial visit, the patient’s HbA1C level was 7.3%, fasting blood sugar level was 104 mg/dL, and 2-h postprandial blood sugar 247 mg/dL.</p><p>On physical examination, the patient’s weight was 65 kg, height was 167 cm, and body mass index was 23.31 kg/cm<sup>2</sup>, which is a normal value. There was an ulcer on the first toe of the right foot, measuring approximately 6×2 cm, with a depth of 3 mm. There were no signs of venous insufficiency (CRT &lt;2 s) and no signs of severe infection. The joint range of motion of the right toe was full, and the intrinsic muscle strength of the left and right feet was 5 out of 5. There was a decrease in tactile sensation in the left and right feet by approximately 30%.</p><p>The patient was then scheduled for routine wound care with non-adherent saline gauze dressing and administration of LLLT 3 times a week. Wound care was performed by cleaning the wound with normal saline (NaCl 0.9%) and topical antibiotics (framicetyne sulfate 1%). Then, 660 nm wavelength, continuous mode, and 4 J/cm<sup>2</sup> dosage of LLLT therapy was used.
