This approach aligns with global ethnobotanical practices, where similarity indices are valuable for assessing cross-cultural and ecological patterns in medicinal plant use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec40"><title>Indigenous knowledge transfer and medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups</title><p id="Par230">Independent t-tests revealed significant differences in medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups in the Karamara forest patches. Male participants demonstrated higher knowledge than females (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &lt; 0.05), reflecting gender-specific roles in resource use. Men typically engage in harvesting trees, shrubs, and multipurpose plants, increasing familiarity with species used for construction, fuel, and medicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>]. In contrast, females tend to specialize in herbs and domestic remedies, a pattern also documented in Nigeria, India, and China [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR82">82</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR83">83</xref>], <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>– [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>].</p><p id="Par231">Illiterate participants had significantly greater knowledge than literate individuals (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &lt; 0.05), suggesting that formal education may reduce engagement with traditional medicinal practices. Similar trends appear in Tanzania, Pakistan, and Latin America, where elders and less formally educated individuals retain deeper ethnobotanical knowledge due to reliance on traditional remedies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR96">96</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>].</p><p id="Par232">Key informants, including traditional healers and community elders, showed the highest knowledge, significantly exceeding general informants (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &lt; 0.05). Specialized experience, social roles, and prolonged exposure to local healthcare practices are strong predictors of ethnobotanical expertise. Globally, healers and elders function as custodians of medicinal plant knowledge, emphasizing their role in documentation, conservation, and sustainable use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR84">84</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par233">Age also strongly influenced knowledge. One-way ANOVA indicated a significant effect of age (F = 50.54, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &lt; 0.05), with older participants demonstrating the greatest expertise. Correlation (<italic toggle="yes">r</italic> = 0.653, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &lt; 0.001) and regression analysis (R² = 0.575) suggest that age accounts for over half the variation in knowledge, reflecting accumulation through prolonged experience and engagement with traditional healthcare. Similar age-related patterns are reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan, where elders serve as primary knowledge holders while younger generations show reduced familiarity due to urbanization and formal education [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>]. These trends are also observed in Europe and the Americas, highlighting knowledge erosion among youth in modernized contexts [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par234">Knowledge transfer in Karamara is predominantly vertical, passing within families to selected heirs, typically male children. This selective transmission preserves community-specific therapeutic practices and social prestige, a pattern noted in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>].
