Data analysis consisted of thematic coding where data was categorised according to its framework and linked to relevant themes.<xref rid="bib0130" ref-type="bibr"><sup>26</sup></xref> This was implemented by grouping words and sentences together that convey similar meanings and labelling each group with codes.<xref rid="bib0135" ref-type="bibr"><sup>27</sup></xref> Codes were allocated to exhaustive categories creating groups of content that share a commonality.<xref rid="bib0135" ref-type="bibr"><sup>27</sup></xref> One researcher (SG) coded all transcripts and then met with two other researchers (JW and DC) to discuss emerging themes and establish consensus. Coding allowed for segments of text to be interpreted in new ways<xref rid="bib0140" ref-type="bibr"><sup>28</sup></xref> and developed ‘clusters’ of meaning<xref rid="bib0130" ref-type="bibr"><sup>26</sup></xref> that represent the perceptions, beliefs and attitudes that practitioners have about adaptogens. The researcher (SG) removed any possible identifying information from the transcripts. Participants are identified by pseudonym initials to maintain confidentiality.</p><p id="par0120">Prior to conducting the study, the researchers conducted a literature review to identify the domains used to measure adaptogenic activity in clinical trials which is described elsewhere.<xref rid="bib0070" ref-type="bibr"><sup>14</sup></xref> Two additional key articles were identified during the literature review phase which outline the laboratory findings (i.e. molecular targets) on adaptogens.<xref rid="bib0025" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>, <xref rid="bib0050" ref-type="bibr">10</xref> The findings of this study were collated with those existing reviews in the form of a narrative synthesis informing the findings and discussion points.</p></sec><sec id="sec0035"><label>2.5</label><title>Ethics</title><p id="par0125">The project was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) through ECNH (approval number 20181217-SG-1), and was conducted according to the principles outlined in the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2007.<xref rid="bib0145" ref-type="bibr"><sup>29</sup></xref></p></sec></sec><sec id="sec0040"><label>3</label><title>Results</title><p id="par0130">Three FGs were conducted in Brisbane, Melbourne and Sydney. The groups contained between 4 and 7 people (17 total), lasting between 60 and 120 min. Three core themes were developed from the qualitative data, relating to practitioner understanding and use of adaptogenic herbal medicines. These themes were: <italic toggle="yes">ambiguous cultural origins with divergent perceptions on sources of knowledge about adaptogens; raising vitality and having a restorative effect,</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">intersystem activity.</italic> Within these three central themes a number of sub-themes are discussed.</p><sec id="sec0045"><label>3.1</label><title>Ambiguous cultural origins with divergent perceptions on sources of knowledge of adaptogens</title><p id="par0135">Although the majority of participants commented on the alignment of adaptogens with naturopathic and Western herbal medicine practice philosophy, and several viewed it as being analogous with historical tonic concepts in WHM, most viewed the concept as having been imported from other cultures or traditions. Ayurveda, Chinese medicine and Russian herbal medicine were listed as likely sources, or more modern concepts such as the General Adaptation Syndrome (developed by Hans Selye). As the quotes below illustrate, when the participants were questioned on where the knowledge of adaptogens has come from there was a consensus that – while adaptogens were now an intrinsic part of herbal medicine practice – they had originally come from cultural systems of medicine outside of Western herbal medicine:<disp-quote><p>“I think the concept is kind of like, I guess you could say Chinese or Ayurveda kind of concept but when you think about it there’s lots of Western herbal medicines that are also Adaptogens.” (MB)</p><p>“The traditional Ayurveda and TCM.” (AP)</p><p>‘I always think about the military that were in the middle of the desert in Siberia so I kind of think of that historical concept in terms of how it came to be in use.’ (LD)</p><p>“It started with the Russians actually.” (VM)</p></disp-quote></p><p id="par0140">There was little consensus of adaptogen’s traditional origins beyond the fact it originated in other cultures and that the herbs they most commonly referred to as adaptogens were herbs drawn from TCM and Ayurvedic traditions.
