Indeed, germination improved the phenolic compounds content by 49% and 44% in <italic toggle="yes">Sinapis alba</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Brassica nigra</italic> seeds, respectively, as well as in broccoli seeds [<xref rid="B36-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="B107-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">107</xref>]. Furthermore, asparagine, proline, and glutamic acid were much more abundant in broccoli seeds after germination [<xref rid="B36-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>]. Consequently, germination is a simple and effective approach to boost seed nutrients. Yet, the germination time reduced the broccoli seed total flavonoid content, probably resulting from the moisture accumulation [<xref rid="B79-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">79</xref>].</p><p>As shown in <xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref> and <xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t004" ref-type="table">Table 4</xref>, the phytochemical profile variation in different <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seed species is also dependent on the extraction solvent, the extraction methods, and the detection and quantification approaches. Sample preparation and extraction methods are crucial steps. Acetone, methanol, ethanol, ethyl acetate, and their mixtures with water are the most employed solvents in plant phenolic extraction [<xref rid="B11-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. Indeed, equal parts water and acetone has a better effect on the phenolic compounds extraction of mustard seed [<xref rid="B108-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">108</xref>]. Generally, the extraction of phenolic compounds is most effective using solvents with high polarity [<xref rid="B109-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">109</xref>]. Numerous conventional and advanced extraction techniques such as maceration, soxhlet, microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), and ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) are applied for the phytochemical screening of <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds. It was reported that hydrodistillation increased the canola oil extraction level, while, in Indian mustard seed, soxhlet extraction with petroleum ether raised the oil content to 37% with strong antioxidant potential [<xref rid="B104-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">104</xref>,<xref rid="B110-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">110</xref>]. Furthermore, it was reported that the inactivation of myrosinase by a heating process (30 min at 60 °C), followed by methanol/water extraction and solid-phase extraction (SPE), is the preferable strategy for obtaining high GLS yield. The myrosinase inactivation by a heating process and high methanol concentration prevented the GLS enzymatic hydrolysis [<xref rid="B111-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">111</xref>].</p><p>The most common analytical techniques applied in <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seed phytochemical profile analysis are GC and HPLC. A variable profile has been demonstrated within the <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> spp. GC and GC–MS are applied to identify the volatile organics directly or indirectly after derivatization. They have been used to characterize the GLS breakdown products and fatty acids [<xref rid="B78-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">78</xref>,<xref rid="B97-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">97</xref>]. However, some isothiocyanates, such as sulforaphane, are thermally unstable and, consequently, GC is inadequate for their evaluation [<xref rid="B36-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="B112-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">112</xref>]. Additionally, due to the non-volatility or thermal instability of some GLSs, GC is not appropriate for their direct analysis and, consequently, must be converted into volatile derivatives which may be not suitable with some GLS structures [<xref rid="B111-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">111</xref>]. HPLC is, thus, the preferred method with a desulfation step to reduce the polarity for the separation by reverse-phase chromatography (RPC) [<xref rid="B113-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">113</xref>]. Therefore, as shown in <xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t004" ref-type="table">Table 4</xref>, HPLC is the frequently used technique for GLS and amino-acid analysis in <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds [<xref rid="B36-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="B114-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">114</xref>]. However, the lack of standards of some compounds is a limiting factor of HPLC viability. Thus, to improve phytochemical detection and quantification, the combination of HPLC with the highly sensitive method MS (LC–MS) is needed as a rapid, selective and sensitive approach [<xref rid="B35-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>,<xref rid="B115-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">115</xref>].
