BY</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec><title>Background</title><p>Leprosy is a chronic infectious disease caused by <italic toggle="yes">Mycobacterium leprae</italic>, the only pathogenic bacteria able to infect peripheral nerves. About 30% of people with leprosy develop nerve damage. Neural impairment results in a set of sensitive, motor and autonomic disturbances, with ulcers originating primarily on the hands and feet. Neuropathic ulcers are one of the most common sequelae of leprosy, but little is known about their clinical and epidemiological aspects. They are very disabling to the patient and can result in deformity and/or amputation of the affected limb [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B1">1</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B2">2</xref>].</p><p>Brazil has the highest prevalence of leprosy cases in the world (3.21 cases per 10000 inhabitants in 2007), with the majority of these cases registered in the North and Middle-West Regions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B3">3</xref>]. The State of Pará, in the Amazon region, registered 4955 new leprosy cases in 2006, accounting for nearly 1% of all cases world-wide [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B4">4</xref>].</p><p>Approximately 19% of new cases in Pará have a grade 1 or 2 disability. In 2005, the cure rate for leprosy was 71%, which was a questionable result according to Brazilian Health Ministry [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B6">6</xref>]. Delayed diagnostics, lack of appropriate treatment and failure in leprosy reactions control contribute to the occurrence of nerve damage and neuropathic ulcers in these patients.</p><p>Different methods of treatment have been used in ulcer management, but the outcomes are frequently dissatisfactory, and many people must live with chronic wounds that result in high economic and social costs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B2">2</xref>].</p><p>Low level laser therapy (LLLT) has been used to accelerate wound healing since the late 1960 s, but its results are controversial [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B7">7</xref>]. One study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>] evaluated the use of LLLT in the treatment of leprosy ulcers with satisfactory results (66% were cured). However, in a systematic review published by Cochrane [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B9">9</xref>], the authors did not find evidence of wound healing improvement related to LLLT.</p><p>The main objectives of this study were to analyze clinical and epidemiological characteristics of patients with leprosy ulcers and to evaluate the effect of LLLT on wound healing in these patients.</p></sec><sec sec-type="methods"><title>Methods</title><p>This study was approved by the Center of Tropical Medicine Research Ethics Committee from the Federal University of Pará (protocol number 074/2006 - CEP/NMT).
