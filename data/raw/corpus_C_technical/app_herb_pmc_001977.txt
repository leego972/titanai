In <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>, accumulating evidence suggests that these metabolites—comprising both ubiquitous constituents (e.g., quercetin, caffeic acid) and unique lignin-derived derivatives—play a substantial role in the therapeutic effects traditionally associated with this species [<xref rid="B4-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="B17-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B21-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>].</p><p>In this section, only selected phenolic compounds with either confirmed structural identity or significant biological activity have been discussed in detail. This selective approach reflects the authors’ intention to emphasize pharmacologically relevant metabolites while avoiding speculative interpretation of tentative or potentially artifactual identifications.</p><p>In vitro studies have confirmed strong antioxidant activity in ethanol extracts from <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> roots and leaves, which correlates positively with their phenolic content. Nguyen et al. [<xref rid="B89-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">89</xref>] reported that root extracts exhibited potent radical-scavenging activity against DPPH (IC<sub>50</sub> = 96.14 µg·mL<sup>−1</sup>) and ABTS (IC<sub>50</sub> = 38.76 µg·mL<sup>−1</sup>), with total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) being strongly associated with antioxidant potential. Similar results were obtained for leaf extracts by Ly et al. [<xref rid="B20-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>], which showed pronounced free radical scavenging activity and inhibition of lipid peroxidation in TBARS assays.</p><p>Phenolic compounds isolated from lipophilic fractions, including 1′-hydroxyeugenol (<bold>31</bold>), sinapyl alcohol (<bold>39</bold>), and the dimeric 1,2-bis(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)ethylene (<bold>38</bold>), exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of nitric oxide (NO) production in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages [<xref rid="B17-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. These findings suggest that lipophilic phenolics may modulate key inflammatory signaling pathways. Its chemical structure, featuring both methoxy and hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring, confers high antioxidant capacity and facilitates redox regulation [<xref rid="B91-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">91</xref>].</p><p>Le Roy et al. [<xref rid="B91-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">91</xref>] showed that sinapyl alcohol is stored in plants as glucosides, which can be mobilized in response to environmental stimuli, providing protection against pathogens and UV radiation.</p><p>Further evidence supports the anti-inflammatory and antinociceptive properties of sinapyl alcohol in vivo. Oral administration significantly reduced edema and pain in formalin- and carrageenan-induced inflammation models, highlighting its therapeutic potential in inflammatory and pain-related disorders [<xref rid="B92-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">92</xref>]. Additionally, sinapyl alcohol exhibits moderate antimicrobial activity and regulates lignin biosynthesis by modulating cell wall differentiation, which may underlie its cytotoxic and neuroprotective potential [<xref rid="B93-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">93</xref>].</p><p>Ferulic acid derivatives isolated from <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> roots, including 8-O-4-dehydrodiferulic acid (<bold>29</bold>) and 8-O-4/8-O-4-dehydrotriferulic acid (<bold>30</bold>), demonstrated neuroprotective effects in oxidative stress models. Selvaraj et al. [<xref rid="B21-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>] attributed these effects to their antioxidant capacity and modulation of apoptotic and intracellular signaling pathways in neuronal cells.</p><p>Elicitation with jasmonic acid and other stressors enhanced phenolic biosynthesis and antioxidant potential in in vitro cultures of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> [<xref rid="B12-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. This observation suggests that elicitation strategies could be further optimized to boost pharmacologically valuable phenolic content in vitro.</p><p>Quercetin derivatives may also contribute to the antimicrobial activity of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>. A review by Mawea et al. [<xref rid="B94-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">94</xref>] highlighted the antibacterial efficacy of quercetin and quercitrin in other plant models, supporting the ethnomedicinal use of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> for treating infections and inflammatory skin conditions.</p><p>Among the most pharmacologically significant polyphenols in <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> is kaempferol-3-O-α-L-rhamnoside (afzelin) (<bold>20</bold>), isolated from the diethyl ether fraction of leaf extracts [<xref rid="B8-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>].
