The term adaptogen was used as a functional claim for certain botanicals and herbal medicinal products in Europe and the USA and the adaptogen concept is now a generally accepted concept (Samuelsson and Bohlin, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B62">2009</xref>). A number of clinical trials clearly demonstrated that adaptogens exert an anti-fatigue effect that increases mental work capacity against a background of stress and fatigue, particularly in tolerance to mental exhaustion and enhanced attention (Panossian and Wikman, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">2010</xref>). Studies on animals and isolated cells have revealed that adaptogens exhibit neuroprotective, anti-fatigue, antidepressive, anxiolytic, nootropic, and CNS stimulating and tonic effects (Panossian and Wagner, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B51">2005</xref>; Panossian and Wikman, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">2010</xref>). In contrast to conventional stimulants such as sympathomimetics (e.g., ephedrine, fenfluramine, phentermine, prolintane) and general tonics, adaptogens do not possess addiction, tolerance and abuse potentials, or impair mental function, or lead to psychotic symptoms with long term use (Panossian and Wikman, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">2010</xref>). Recent pharmacological studies of a number of adaptogens have provided a rationale for these effects also at the molecular level (Panossian and Wikman, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">2010</xref>). The stress–protective activity of adaptogens is associated with regulation of homeostasis <italic toggle="yes">via</italic> several mechanisms of action which are linked to the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the regulation of key mediators of the stress response, including cortisol, nitric oxide, stress-activated protein kinase c-Jun N-terminal protein kinase (JNK; Panossian et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B50">2007</xref>), forkhead box O (FoxO) transcription factor (DAF-16; Wiegant et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B74">2009</xref>), and molecular chaperones (Chiu and Ko, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B16">2004</xref>; Panossian and Wikman, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">2010</xref>). However, it still remains unclear what the primary upstream targets are in response to stimulation by adaptogens. In this study, we investigate whether heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) and Neuropeptide Y (NPY) might be one of the primary upstream targets of adaptogens in neuroglia cells.</p><p>Neuropeptide Y is a stress–responsive hormone widely distributed in the central and peripheral nervous system (Tatemoto et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B70">1982</xref>; Irwin, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B29">2008</xref>). In the brain the concentrations of NPY are significantly higher than other neuropeptides, and is found mainly in the limbic system, including the amygdala and the hypothalamus, which are areas of the brain involved in the regulation of emotional behaviors and stress response (Dumont et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">1993</xref>; Smialowska et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B67">2007</xref>). In the peripheral nervous system, NPY is concentrated in sympathetic nerve endings (Irwin, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B29">2008</xref>). Sympathoadrenal activation during the stress response results in NPY release from the sympathetic nerve endings either alone or with catecholamines (Morris et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">1986</xref>). NPY release follows stressors including strenuous exercise (Karamouzis et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B30">2002</xref>), panic disorders (Boulenger et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B11">1996</xref>), cold exposure (Kellogg, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B31">2006</xref>), and chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS; Fletcher et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">2010</xref>). The elevation of NPY in blood of CFS patients is associated with severity of stress, negative mood, and clinical symptoms (Fletcher et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">2010</xref>). On the other hand, psychological stress elevated plasma NPY in healthy subjects (Morgan et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B42">2001</xref>). In the periphery, sympathetic nerve- and platelet-derived NPY act in a stimulatory fashion; synergizing with glucocorticoids and catecholamines to potentiate the stress response, induce vasoconstriction and increase vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation. However, in the brain NPY acts as an anxiolytic and inhibits sympathetic activity which results in lowering blood pressure and heart rate (Morris et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">1986</xref>; Kuo et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B34">2007</xref>), and inhibiting the production of cortisol in human adrenal cells (Kempna et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B32">2010</xref>). NPY can regulate both immune cells and neuronal cells, e.g., NPY strongly inhibits NO synthesis through Y(1) receptor activation, which prevents IL-1β release and thus inhibits nuclear translocation of NF-κB in microglia (Ferreira et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B23">2010</xref>).
