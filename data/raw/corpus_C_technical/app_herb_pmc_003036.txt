During fieldwork, detailed notes and photographs documented each species’ habitat, growth form, and traditional uses. Specimens were transported to the Biology Department Laboratory, College of Natural and Computational Sciences, Jigjiga University, where they were air-dried, pressed, labeled, numbered, and initially identified to the family or, when possible, species level.</p><p id="Par40">Comprehensive taxonomic identification was conducted using authoritative references, including the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea and standard taxonomic keys. Specimens were verified at the National Herbarium (ETH), Addis Ababa University, by comparison with authenticated herbarium samples and published references [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>]. Scientific nomenclature followed the accepted standards of the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea, ensuring consistency and reliability.</p><p id="Par41">To resolve potential taxonomic ambiguities, digital resources were also employed, including PlantNet, Flora Finder, PlantSnap, Google Image Search, the African Plant Database, the World Checklist of Selected Plant Families, JSTOR Global Plants, and Plants of the World Online (Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>]. This integrative approach combining field observation, herbarium verification, and digital tools ensured precise identification and thorough documentation of MPs in the Karamara forest patches.</p><p id="Par42">All identifications were formally confirmed by Dr. Getu Alemayehu, and voucher specimens were deposited in the Mini Herbarium, Department of Biology, Jigjiga University (deposition number JJU004107). These specimens provide a permanent reference for future taxonomic studies, biodiversity assessments, and conservation planning, establishing a reliable foundation for ethnobotanical research and sustainable management of medicinal plant resources in the region.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec6"><title>Quantitative ethnobotanical analysis</title><p id="Par43">To evaluate the reliability, depth, and consensus of traditional medicinal plant knowledge in the Karamara forest patches, several established quantitative ethnobotanical indices were applied. These included the Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Fidelity Level (FL), Cultural Value Index, and Informant Consensus Factor (ICF).</p><p id="Par44">Participatory ranking methods, including preference ranking and direct matrix ranking, were employed to capture local perceptions of plant efficacy and prioritize species based on cultural and therapeutic importance. Additionally, similarity analyses using the Jaccard Similarity Index were conducted to compare species composition and usage patterns within the study area and with findings from other ethnobotanical studies. This integrative approach allowed for objective assessment of species significance, consensus among informants, and identification of culturally and ecologically valuable medicinal plants.</p><p id="Par45">The Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) was calculated for each medicinal plant species to evaluate its local prominence and cultural importance within the study community [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>].
