Regulatory Aspects of Herbal Anticancer Drugs</title><p>It is generally established that the drugs including the anticancer compounds require phase III clinical research trials for marketing permissions. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidelines require at least one controlled trial in Phase III with statistically significant results for the green signal to market them [<xref rid="B176-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">176</xref>]. Except for exceptional circumstances, all the drugs need to go through all the phases of trials according to the guidelines of international agencies such as the FDA and EMA. However, it has been observed that pharmaceutical companies deviate from the standard protocol and start testing new compounds on human subjects earlier than the defined timeline. The reason for such practices is to accelerate the approval of these compounds under the pressure of investors [<xref rid="B176-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">176</xref>]. This means that the drug is presented for approval with insufficient data on its quality, safety, and efficacy. </p><p>Although plant-based compounds have shown be less toxic compared to conventional synthetic compounds, there is growing evidence on the side effects of the unregulated use of these plants against different diseases. The problem is that there is insufficient data available regarding the quality, safety, and efficacy of herbal drugs. <italic toggle="yes">F. indica</italic>, for instance, has shown potent activity against breast cancer when tested in the MDA-MB-231 cell line [<xref rid="B44-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">F. indica</italic> is used traditionally to treat many disorders and people have even started the use of its herbal tea against breast cancer. However, the question remains that there are only a few reports available on the anticancer activity of the plant. Globally, the process of oncology drug development and marketing is regulated through the involvement of experts and an advisory process mediated by regulatory authorities [<xref rid="B177-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">177</xref>].</p><p>There are several regulatory framework models available for prescribing such drugs but there is a need for harmony among regulating agencies and improvement in the regulation process. For instance, the FDA has recently adopted the questions and answers guidelines of the International Council for Harmonization on the nonclinical evaluation of drugs intended to treat cancer. These guidelines include 41 questions and answers which provide additional information about anticancer drug development and are aimed at bringing harmonization in the process of anticancer drug development [<xref rid="B178-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">178</xref>]. It is, however, suggested that regulatory authorities, while bringing harmony with other agencies working for regulating anticancer herbal compounds, should increase the focus on combining information from traditional knowledge about that drug and the scientific studies on it [<xref rid="B179-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">179</xref>].</p><p>Moreover, it is evident that plants of the same species grown in different areas vary in their profile of medicinal compounds [<xref rid="B180-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">180</xref>]. This calls for the need to focus on the production of uniform and high-quality plants with a uniform metabolite profile that once tested is declared safe or unsafe once and for all. This might be achieved through the help of in vitro growth and biotechnological and genetic studies on these anticancer plants [<xref rid="B181-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">181</xref>,<xref rid="B182-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">182</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec6-biomolecules-10-00047"><title>6. Modern Trends in Traditional Medicine Informatics and Opportunities for Anticancer Plant Products</title><p>With the advancement of information technology and bioinformatics, there is an increasing trend to build resources and databases that report herbal formulations, active components of the herb, and related information. There are several efforts like Chinese Medicine Integrated Database (TCMID) [<xref rid="B183-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">183</xref>], Collective Molecular Activities of Useful Plants (CMAUP) [<xref rid="B184-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">184</xref>], SymMap [<xref rid="B185-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>], encyclopedia of traditional Chinese medicine (ETCM) [<xref rid="B186-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">186</xref>] etc. In addition, several researchers have developed strategies for in silico pharmacokinetic properties of molecules/drugs [<xref rid="B187-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">187</xref>,<xref rid="B188-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">188</xref>,<xref rid="B189-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">189</xref>,<xref rid="B190-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">190</xref>,<xref rid="B191-biomolecules-10-00047" ref-type="bibr">191</xref>].
