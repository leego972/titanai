the Andes Region</title><p>The Andean environment fosters biochemical diversity by influencing nutrient uptake, secondary metabolite production, and stress-response mechanisms in plants. Quinoa and lupin, for instance, are highly adapted to these extreme conditions, thriving in nutrient-poor soils and enduring frequent droughts and frost events [<xref rid="B4-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. The mineral composition of Andean soils, particularly their high nitrate (NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup>) content, plays a crucial role in shaping plant metabolism. Quinoa ecotypes, such as Socaire, exhibit superior nitrate uptake efficiency, leading to enhanced protein and amino acid accumulation, which directly impacts both growth and nutritional quality [<xref rid="B18-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>].</p><p>Similarly, <italic toggle="yes">L. mutabilis</italic> has evolved specialized root systems that allow for it to fix atmospheric nitrogen, improving soil fertility and making it an integral part of traditional Andean crop rotation systems [<xref rid="B28-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. This ability not only enhances its own nutritional composition, but also benefits subsequent crops, ensuring sustainable agricultural practices. Meanwhile, <italic toggle="yes">A. hybridus</italic>, a species with deep roots in Andean agriculture, exhibits exceptional adaptability to soil variability and microclimatic fluctuations, leading to increased phytochemical accumulation that enhances its antioxidant and antimicrobial properties [<xref rid="B29-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>].</p><p>Beyond soil composition, altitude significantly impacts phytochemical production. Studies on Andean blueberries (<italic toggle="yes">Vaccinium floribundum</italic>) have shown that higher elevations correlate with increased polyphenol and anthocyanin accumulation, boosting antioxidant activity [<xref rid="B30-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. This pattern is also reflected in <xref rid="nutrients-17-01749-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>, where higher altitudes are associated with the biosynthesis of complex antioxidant compounds such as anthocyanins, flavonols, and phenolic acids. A similar trend has been observed in quinoa, amaranth, and lupin, where high-altitude conditions enhance the biosynthesis of flavonoids, phenolic acids, and other protective secondary metabolites that contribute to their functional properties [<xref rid="B9-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec3dot2-nutrients-17-01749"><title>3.2. Adaptations of Andean Plants</title><p>To cope with the harsh Andean environment, <italic toggle="yes">C. quinoa</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. hybridus</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">L. mutabilis</italic> have evolved an array of secondary metabolites—flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and phenolic compounds—that not only provide protection against abiotic stressors (e.g., UV radiation, drought), but also enhance their nutritional and therapeutic potential. These metabolic responses are essential for their resilience and survival, reflecting sophisticated biochemical flexibility [<xref rid="B31-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>].</p><p>Quinoa, for example, produces elevated levels of phenolic compounds when exposed to high UV radiation, which enhances its antioxidant properties [<xref rid="B9-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. Likewise, <italic toggle="yes">L. mutabilis</italic> exhibits significant phenotypic variation in response to soil type, with plants grown in silty loam soil demonstrating higher protein and isoflavone content compared to those in sandy clay loam, directly affecting their nutritional and medicinal value [<xref rid="B32-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>].</p><p>Amaranth, another crop deeply rooted in Andean agriculture, is known for its high adaptability to drought-prone environments. The extreme temperature fluctuations at high altitudes stimulate the accumulation of bioactive compounds such as betalains and phenolic acids, which contribute to its anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects [<xref rid="B29-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>].</p><p>In addition to their abiotic stress tolerance, Andean plants must balance their biochemical investment between pollination and herbivory defense. Research on <italic toggle="yes">Haplopappus</italic> spp. demonstrates how floral volatile profiles vary with altitude to optimize insect attraction while simultaneously deterring herbivores [<xref rid="B33-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. This ecological strategy is visually represented in <xref rid="nutrients-17-01749-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>, where volatile and defensive compound production is associated with different elevation levels. Similarly, quinoa, amaranth, and lupin rely on these adaptive chemical strategies to maintain reproductive success and minimize biotic stress [<xref rid="B34-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec3dot3-nutrients-17-01749"><title>3.3.
