The presence of three active antioxidants, namely, (–)-epicatechin <bold>51</bold>, quercitrin <bold>4</bold>, and myricitrin <bold>11,</bold> was reported to be supportive of the activity of the leaf extract. Quercitrin <bold>4</bold> and myricitrin <bold>11</bold> were also found in the leaf extract of Egyptian <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> [<xref rid="B25-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. The potent antioxidant activity of the leaf extract reported by Elansary et al. [<xref rid="B13-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>] was extensively supported by many other flavonoids and polyphenols in the extract, as indicated in their HPLC analysis. </p><p>Youzbachi et al. [<xref rid="B48-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>] should also be mentioned for their investigation into the antioxidant of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> seeds collected in Tunisia. Their methanolic extract displayed scavenging capacity in the DPPH assay, with an average IC<sub>50</sub> value of 590 μM of Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC). The GC analysis of the methanolic extract revealed a high content of fatty acids, especially health-promoting fatty acids [reported in % of the total content (101.7 g/kg of dried weight (DW) of seeds], which included linoleic acid <bold>63</bold> (61.11–65.45), and oleic acid <bold>64</bold> (19.67–22.85). Smaller proportions of palmitic <bold>65</bold> (9.18–9.98), stearic <bold>66</bold> (1.49–1.82), vaccenic <bold>67</bold> (1.13–2.05), and palmitoleic <bold>68</bold> (0.34–0.58) acids were also quantified (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-f005" ref-type="fig">Figure 5</xref>). The phenolic content of the extract was reported to be 1.91 g of gallic acid <bold>25</bold> equivalent kg<sup>−1</sup> DW, while the total flavonoid content was shown to be in an average of 0.40 g of rutin <bold>6</bold> equivalent kg<sup>−1</sup> DW. In addition, the group used the Kjeldahl assay to estimate the protein content of the seeds. The assay revealed a high content of protein, with an average of 107.2 g kg<sup>−1</sup> on DW of seeds. This study did not conduct a detailed chemical analysis of the phenols and flavonoids to allow for further discussion. However, the estimated phenolic and flavonoid contents were evidently linked to the scavenging capacity observed in the seed extract. Notably, markedly high contents of linoleic acid <bold>63</bold> (61.11–65.45%) and oleic acid <bold>64</bold> (19.67–22.85%) were identified in the seed extract. These unsaturated fatty acids are known to benefit cardiovascular health [<xref rid="B49-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>].</p><p>The antioxidant activities (IC<sub>50</sub> values) listed in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref> are markedly variable, even from the same plant part. For instance, the extracts of flowers collected in different parts of the world, when applying different extraction conditions, have been shown to have different IC<sub>50</sub> values. The main contributor to this variability would be the different chemical compositions in each extract, as shown in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref> and the <xref rid="app1-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="app">Supplementary Table S1</xref>. Although one can observe a few common compounds existing across the extracts, the compositions are variable. This information reiterates that factors, including location, growing conditions, collection season, and extraction methods, could influence the composition of metabolites in the extracts and, hence, the outcome of activities. </p><p>Notably, three main groups of compounds were commonly identified (with variable concentrations) across actives extracts listed in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>.
