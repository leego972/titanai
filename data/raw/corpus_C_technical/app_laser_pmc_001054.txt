In optic neuritis eyes, medium-sized retinal vessel reduction correlates with ganglion cell loss, suggesting altered retinal metabolism.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup> Conversely, in non-optic neuritis eyes, there is loss of small vessels, which appears unrelated to ganglion cell degeneration, pointing to a compromised blood–retina barrier and inflammatory effects.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup> Furthermore, a post-mortem study by Green <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>. demonstrated abnormal distribution of tight junction proteins in some retinal vessels and an accumulation of HLA-DR–positive immune cells in the retinal layers and optic nerve head near vessels.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> These pathological findings complement <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> evidence, supporting a role for compartmentalized inflammation and vascular barrier dysfunction in MS-related retinal changes. Retrograde degeneration is also likely to contribute, as previous studies have shown effects on pRNFL/GCIPL thinning in non-optic neuritis eyes<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref></sup> although its impact on the retinal microvasculature remains less well defined.</p></sec><sec id="fcag007-s4.2"><title>Brain volume associations differ between optical coherence tomography angiography and optical coherence tomography metrics</title><p>Our findings reveal differential correlations between global brain volumes, primary visual pathway regions, and higher order visual structures with OCTA and OCT metrics. OCTA and mGCIPL thinning were reflective of cortical grey matter atrophy, whereas pRNFL thinning was associated with white matter atrophy and borderline significant correlations with deep grey matter atrophy. This may suggest different mechanisms at play; cortical neurodegeneration may stem from chronic hypoperfusion leading to retrograde degeneration and mGCIPL thinning<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref></sup> as well as an increased energy requirements in grey matter compared to white matter leading to relative hypoxia,<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref></sup> whilst pRNFL changes may be linked to axonal loss involving deep grey matter connections.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref></sup></p><p>Previous imaging studies support this hypothesis, demonstrating reduced cortical microvascular oxygenation and grey matter hypoperfusion in MS.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B8" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="fcag007-B41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref></sup> These patterns also align with experimental models showing that early hypoxia precede clinical symptoms.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B42" ref-type="bibr">42</xref></sup></p><p>We found no correlation between OCTA metrics with lesion volume and number, which is in keeping with previous smaller clinically isolated syndrome and relapsing remitting MS cohort studies suggesting that OCTA reflects neurodegeneration from chronic hypoperfusion rather than acute inflammation in demyelinating lesions.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B12" ref-type="bibr">12</xref></sup></p></sec><sec id="fcag007-s4.3"><title>Retinal microvascular dysfunction reflects a network of atrophic changes along the visual pathway</title><p>Further analysis identified an association between regional cortical grey matter atrophy and worse OCTA metrics, which possessed a predilection for higher-order visual cortices while OCT metrics were more strongly linked to early visual pathway structures.</p><list list-type="bullet"><list-item><p>
<bold>Primary visual cortex and visual association cortices:</bold> All OCTA and OCT metrics were associated with smaller volumes of the primary visual cortex, but OCTA uniquely reflected alterations in higher-order visual processing regions (occipital or non-occipital lobe), such as the cuneus, inferior occipital, occipital fusiform and inferior temporal gyri.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="fcag007-B43" ref-type="bibr">43</xref></sup></p></list-item><list-item><p>
<bold>Thalamus:</bold> Associations were found with all OCTA and OCT metrics and smaller thalamic volume, likely due to its vulnerability to disrupted connectivity, relay-centre function and subjectivity to both anterograde and retrograde degeneration at the synapse, as well as high metabolic activity and microglial density.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B44" ref-type="bibr">44-47</xref></sup></p></list-item><list-item><p>
<bold>Anterior visual pathway:</bold> pRNFL thinning was uniquely associated with chiasm volume loss, reinforcing the link between Wallerian degeneration and white matter atrophy in visual pathway degeneration.</p></list-item></list><p>This suggests that the vascular alterations detected by OCTA may extend beyond early visual pathway degeneration, and is potentially linked to global hypoperfusion, increased metabolic demand, and possible susceptibility of posterior circulation structures to ischaemia.<sup><xref rid="fcag007-B48" ref-type="bibr">48-51</xref></sup> These mechanisms are likely all contributing and interacting as we found that non-visual cortical regions in the frontal, parietal and temporal lobes were not associated with OCTA or OCT, suggesting that global hypoperfusion is not the sole mechanism.
