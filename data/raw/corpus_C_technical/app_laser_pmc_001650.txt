These techniques, known as SD-OCT and swept-source OCT (SS-OCT), are subsets of Fourier-domain detection.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b33" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b34" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b35" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b36" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b37" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b38" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b39" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b40" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b41" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b42" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b43" ref-type="bibr">43</xref></sup> Early OCT instruments used time-domain detection, a low-coherence light source and interferometer with a scanning reference arm. However, it is possible to detect light echoes in the Fourier domain measuring the interference spectrum with a spectrometer and high speed line scan camera.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>,<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b45" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b46" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b47" ref-type="bibr">47</xref></sup> Spectral-domain OCT was described by Fercher et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref></sup> and by Hausler<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b48" ref-type="bibr">48</xref></sup> in the mid-1990s. Spectral-domain OCT was also experimentally and theoretically investigated at MIT in 1991 and is described in patents dating from 1995 to 1996,<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b49" ref-type="bibr">49</xref></sup> but the results were not published in scientific literature.</p><p>The first SD-OCT retinal images were demonstrated in 2002 by Wojtkowski et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref></sup> from the Copernicus University (Toruń, Poland), working in collaboration with the Medical University of Vienna. There was initial skepticism about SD-OCT because the interference spectrum is sensitive to eye motion and we thought that micron scale motion might cause the fringe averaging and loss of signal. Spectral-domain OCT has the interesting limitation that it works only at high speed; low speed fails because eye motion causes fringe averaging. In 2003, three different research groups independently demonstrated that SD-OCT has a powerful sensitivity advantage over time-domain detection, because it essentially measures all echoes of light simultaneously.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b37" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b38" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref></sup> Spectral-domain OCT is also closely related to Fourier spectroscopy, which achieves enhanced sensitivity. Sensitivity is enhanced by the ratio of axial resolution to imaging depth. For most OCT systems, this is factor of 50 to 100×, enabling a corresponding increase in speed. Spectral-domain OCT drove a boom in OCT research and development.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b46" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b47" ref-type="bibr">47</xref></sup></p><p>High speed improved image quality and retinal coverage, a key step toward comprehensive volumetric imaging. In 2003, Jay Wei left Zeiss to found the startup company Optovue and introduced the first FDA-approved SD-OCT instrument in 2006. Spectral-domain OCT is currently the standard for ophthalmic instruments and imaging speeds range from 25,000 to 80,000 A-scans per second. The fundamental concepts of SD-OCT were in the public domain, allowing numerous companies to develop SD-OCT instruments (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f09">Fig. 9</xref>). Competition in the market has fostered innovation, but price and reimbursement pressures create challenges.</p><fig id="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f09" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Figure 9</label><caption><p>Photographs from the 2006 American Academy of Ophthalmology Annual Meeting.
