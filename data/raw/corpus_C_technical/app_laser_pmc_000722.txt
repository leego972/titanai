Participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any point, either by request or at the discretion of the investigator in the event of safety concerns.</p><p>Following eligibility screening, participants underwent a baseline skin biopsy 1 week prior to the first laser session. Skin samples were obtained using a 4 mm punch biopsy under local anesthesia with 1% lidocaine. We selected the “normal‐appearing” skin on the field‐cancerized area as the site for skin biopsies, with the aim of avoiding any AK lesion in the preauricular region.</p><p>After antisepsis with aqueous chlorhexidine, topical 4% lidocaine was applied for 30 min. Procedures were performed with a non‐ablative fractional 1927‐nm thulium laser device (Lavieen, WONTECH, South Korea). <italic toggle="yes">The parameters were: total energy of 20 mJ/spot</italic>, using a 1.6 cm diameter spot size, center‐to‐center spacing (pitch) of 0.7 mm, corresponding to a density of approximately 204 MTZ/cm². A 20% overlap was applied between pulses. One global pass was applied to the entire face, followed by an additional pass on keratotic areas. Each patient underwent four sessions at 1‐month intervals with identical parameters.<disp-quote content-type="quotation"><p>Post‐treatment care was standardized for all participants to ensure consistency, reproducibility, and optimal management of adverse effects. Patients were instructed to cleanse the treated area with a gentle, non‐irritating soap and to apply petrolatum‐based ointment for skin occlusion and barrier repair. In addition, a medium‐potency topical corticosteroid (betamethasone 0.1%) was prescribed twice daily for 7 consecutive days to reduce post‐procedural inflammation. Strict photoprotection was recommended throughout the treatment period and follow‐up, including the daily use of broad‐spectrum sunscreen and avoidance of direct sun exposure, aiming to minimize the risk of post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation.</p></disp-quote>
</p><p>Pain intensity was measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain experienced), and adverse events were systematically documented.</p><p>Clinical response to treatment was evaluated through standardized digital photography, a modified five‐point photoaging scale [<xref rid="lsm70119-bib-0035" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>, <xref rid="lsm70119-bib-0036" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>], and AK lesion counts, performed by two independent dermatologists. Assessments were conducted at the following time points: before the first treatment session (T0), before the second (T1), before the third (T2), before the fourth (T3), and 3 months after the final session (T4).</p><p>Clinical improvements were measured according to the modified five‐point photoaging scale described by Zane and Dover [<xref rid="lsm70119-bib-0035" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>, <xref rid="lsm70119-bib-0036" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>]. This scale evaluates six parameters of photoaged skin—global photoaging score, mottled pigmentation, facial erythema, fine surface lines, sallowness, and tactile roughness—allowing a standardized assessment of treatment outcomes.</p><p>Three months after the final session, a second biopsy was performed 0.5 cm from the first to minimize interference from the healing process.</p><p>Samples collected before and after treatment were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 3 µm slides for hematoxylin‐eosin staining and evaluated by two independent dermatopathologists.<disp-quote content-type="quotation"><p>Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate correlations between clinical and histological variables. Non‐parametric Wilcoxon tests were used for continuous data, and the McNemar test was applied for categorical comparisons. A significance level of 5% (p ≤ 0.05) was adopted. All analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 25.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).</p></disp-quote>
