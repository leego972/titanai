Gene expression profiles of SWT-treated MCF-7 cells were significantly matched with those of estradiol-treated MCF-7 cells in the CMap database, suggesting that SWT might have potential phytoestrogenic effects. Pathway enrichment analysis using differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of SWT-treated datasets revealed that <italic toggle="yes">Nrf2</italic>-regulated genes, antioxidant genes, and chemopreventive inducible genes were affected by SWT treatment, but not by estradiol or ferulic acid in MCF-7 cells. The potential role for phytoestrogen of SWT was further confirmed by whole genome microarray profiling and by estrogen-responsive element luciferase reporter assay in MCF-7 cells (<xref rid="ref92" ref-type="bibr">92</xref>), suggesting the potential of SWT as an estrogen receptor modulator. Another <italic toggle="yes">in silico</italic> analysis (<xref rid="ref93" ref-type="bibr">93</xref>) predicted SWT’s new targets, FOS, JUN, and CAPS3 based on SWT-induced transcriptome data and herb-target information obtained from TCMID database. These studies provide insights into the understanding of complex actions of SWT for gynecological diseases. Antioxidative and estradiol-regulating effects of SWT may help ameliorate ovarian follicular maldevelopment associated with ovulatory and menstrual disorders known to cause infertility (<xref rid="ref94" ref-type="bibr">94</xref>). Recently, protective effects of SWT on ovarian function and oocyte maturation against cyclophosphamide (CP, a chemotherapy drug)-induced chronic ovarian dysfunction and maternal aging have been demonstrated using transcriptome analysis in an <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> mouse model (<xref rid="ref95" ref-type="bibr">95</xref>, <xref rid="ref96" ref-type="bibr">96</xref>). DEGs by SWT treatment in CP-treated mice ovaries were involved in ovarian follicle development, binding of sperm to zona pellucida (Zp), and microtubule nucleation (<xref rid="ref95" ref-type="bibr">95</xref>). Among downregulated DEGs, oocyte-specific genes such as fertilization-related genes (<italic toggle="yes">Zp2</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Zp3</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Nlrp5</italic>), ovarian follicle development-related genes (<italic toggle="yes">Bmp15</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Oas1d</italic>), and oocyte maturation-related genes (<italic toggle="yes">Obox1</italic>) were restored to normal levels after SWT administration. Additionally, further enrichment analysis using TargetScan, a tool for sequence-based microRNA-target predictions, identified microRNA binding sites of DEGs. Three microRNAs (miR-200b-3p, miR-665-3p, and miR-667-3p) had binding sites on either <italic toggle="yes">Bmp15</italic> or <italic toggle="yes">Oas1</italic> mRNA and their expression levels were restored in SWT plus CP-treated mice. Kim and You have also investigated the influence of SWT on ovarian reserve and fertilization in aged mice (&gt; 40-week-old) (<xref rid="ref96" ref-type="bibr">96</xref>, <xref rid="ref97" ref-type="bibr">97</xref>). They performed functional enrichment analysis of transcriptome data obtained from ovulated ovaries and revealed that RAS signaling pathway-related genes were restored to normal levels as those in young mice after SWT administration to aged mice (<xref rid="ref96" ref-type="bibr">96</xref>). SWT administration also restored miR-223-3p, which could interact with embryo implantation-related genes (<italic toggle="yes">Hal</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Acp5</italic>) in the uterus of aged mice (<xref rid="ref97" ref-type="bibr">97</xref>).
