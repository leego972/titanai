ODD results from the deposition of proteinaceous material at the optic nerve head, which later becomes calcified. The diagnosis is based on the clinical examination of round yellow‐white refractile bodies on the surface of the optic disc, with disc swelling, hyper‐autofluorescence of the disc on fundus autofluorescence imaging, and hyper‐reflectivity on ultrasound B scan. An imaging modality that will help distinguish ODD from true disc swelling would be revolutionary; OCTA has this potential [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0121" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>]. There are OCT features described, such as using high definition enhanced depth imaging to see a central hyperechoic mass with shadowing behind the mass or position of the Bruch's membrane, that may help with the differentiation of ODD from a true oedema (Figure <xref rid="ceo14544-fig-0008" ref-type="fig">8</xref>) [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0122" ref-type="bibr">122</xref>]. Optic disc crowding compresses the RNFL, most commonly manifesting with an inferior‐nasal or inferior‐temporal visual field defect with central visual field sparing [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0123" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>]. It is thought that this crowding of the optic disc from drusen may compress the peripapillary nerve fibre layer and impair retinal blood flow to the peripapillary retina [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0124" ref-type="bibr">124</xref>]. Therefore, the OCTA in ODD shows peripapillary capillary drop out adjacent to optic disc drusen deposits; these vascular changes precede RNFL loss and clinical manifestation of VF deficits [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0121" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0124" ref-type="bibr">124</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0125" ref-type="bibr">125</xref>]. This is as opposed to genuine papilloedema, where no loss of peripapillary vascular density is demonstrated on OCTA compared to healthy discs; this is accompanied by large vessel dilation and tortuosity, shunt vessels and microaneurysms, as well as vessel obscuration from oedema (Figure <xref rid="ceo14544-fig-0009" ref-type="fig">9</xref>) [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0042" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0044" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0104" ref-type="bibr">104</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0121" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>]. However, arrest of axoplasmic transport mechanisms can cause secondary axonal degeneration and optic neuropathy in papilloedema, the biggest risk factor being the initial severity of papilledema given by the frisen grading clinically and/or RNFL thickness on OCT [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0126" ref-type="bibr">126</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0127" ref-type="bibr">127</xref>]. One could extrapolate from other optic neuropathies that one would expect to see capillary drop out in the RCP in such cases (Figure <xref rid="ceo14544-fig-0010" ref-type="fig">10</xref>). It is theorised that visible ODD will have a greater capillary drops out, to correspond to the greater RNFL thinning and VF deficit that is seen in this subtype, when compared to the buried ODD subtype [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0124" ref-type="bibr">124</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0128" ref-type="bibr">128</xref>].
