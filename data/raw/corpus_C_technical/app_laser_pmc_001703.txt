Phototherapy</title><p>Phototherapy, also known as photobiomodulation, low-level laser therapy (LLLT), involves the application of light (often laser light of a specific wavelength or a light emitting diode, LED) to stimulate cellular processes.</p><p>The effects of phototherapy are chemical and not thermal. Energy which is delivered to cells produces insignificant and minimal temperature changes, typically in the range of 0.1–0.5°C [<xref rid="B18" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. Cellular responses are the result of changes in photoacceptor molecules, or chromophores. Photoacceptors take part in cellular metabolism and are not connected to a light response, such as chlorophyll which is a photoreceptor [<xref rid="B19" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. Once the photon energy is absorbed, the photoacceptor assumes an electronically excited state [<xref rid="B19" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>], which in turn stimulates cellular metabolism [<xref rid="B20" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>, <xref rid="B21" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>] by activating or deactivating enzymes which alter other macromolecules such as DNA and RNA [<xref rid="B22" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>, <xref rid="B23" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>]. The energy which is absorbed by the photoacceptor can be transferred to other molecules causing chemical reactions in the surrounding tissue; this then gives rise to observable effects at a biological level [<xref rid="B19" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>, <xref rid="B24" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Photon energy is absorbed by the chromophores and there is an increase in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [<xref rid="B25" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>, <xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>] and cell membrane permeability, which leads to activation of secondary messengers which in turn activate a cascade of intracellular signals [<xref rid="B27" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>]. There is also an increase in mitochondrial membrane potential and proton gradient [<xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>].</p><p>The exact mechanisms of action following laser irradiation are not well understood, and a number of theories exist, the most studied and best understood being that of cytochrome-c oxidase (cyt <italic toggle="yes">a</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">a</italic>
<sub>3</sub>), the terminal enzyme in the eukaryotic mitochondrial respiratory chain (complex IV). Cytochrome c oxidase facilitates the transfer of electrons to molecular oxygen. The end product of this complex is the production of ATP. Cytochrome c oxidase has two heme moieties (heme <italic toggle="yes">a</italic> and heme <italic toggle="yes">a</italic>
<sub>3</sub>) and two redox-active copper sites (Cu<sub>A</sub> and Cu<sub>B</sub>), and these are the possible absorbing chromophores for visible red and near infrared (NIR) light [<xref rid="B19" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>, <xref rid="B28" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>, <xref rid="B29" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. When photon energy is absorbed by cytochrome c oxidase, there is a change in the mitochondrial redox state and/or pumping of protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane [<xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>] and an increase in ATP synthesis. There is also an increase in intracellular calcium ([Ca<sup>2+</sup>]i) which stimulates DNA and RNA synthesis [<xref rid="B24" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. It has been speculated by Karu [<xref rid="B28" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>] that photoirradiation may intensify the transfer of electrons within cytochrome c oxidase by making more electrons available. An increase in the transfer of electrons and protons accelerates oxidative metabolism which ultimately leads to increased ATP [<xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. Photoirradiation causes the reduction or oxidation of cytochrome c oxidase and is dependent on the initial redox status of the enzyme at the time of irradiation [<xref rid="B29" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Silveira and colleagues [<xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>, <xref rid="B30" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>] showed that LILI produced an increase in mitochondrial complexes I, II, III, and IV, as well as succinate dehydrogenase. Hu and colleagues [<xref rid="B31" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>] also found an increase in cytochrome c oxidase activity and concluded there was a cascade of reactions which altered cellular homeostasis. Houreld et al., [<xref rid="B32" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>] showed that irradiation of isolated mitochondria resulted in an increase in cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV) activity.
