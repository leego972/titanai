A thorough review and discussion of commercial OCT efforts is available elsewhere.<xref rid="r31" ref-type="bibr"><sup>31</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r33" ref-type="bibr"><sup>33</sup></xref></p><sec id="sec4.1"><label>4.1</label><title>Ophthalmology</title><p>Ophthalmology was one of the first research and application areas of OCT, since few other medical imaging modalities provided the needed resolution and noninvasive diagnostic capability to observe the retina and other ocular components. With the recent commercial release of software-based angiography processing in desktop ophthalmic systems, current research interest areas include viewing retinal cells beyond the rods and cones,<xref rid="r101" ref-type="bibr"><sup>101</sup></xref> wider fields of view,<xref rid="r102" ref-type="bibr"><sup>102</sup></xref> and high-speed imaging.<xref rid="r103" ref-type="bibr"><sup>103</sup></xref> Many different systems have been constructed toward achieving one or several of these aims by both research and commercial interests.</p><p>One excellent example of the evolution of a handheld design in principle and execution is demonstrated in a combined scanning laser ophthalmoscope (SLO)-OCT handheld retinal system. SLOs are typically used to collect high-speed reflectivity maps of the retina<xref rid="r104" ref-type="bibr"><sup>104</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r105" ref-type="bibr"><sup>105</sup></xref> and when used with fluorescein dyes can visualize vasculature.<xref rid="r106" ref-type="bibr"><sup>106</sup></xref> When combined with OCT, a fundus image can be quickly generated along with cross-sectional depth scans.<xref rid="r107" ref-type="bibr"><sup>107</sup></xref> Multiple reference designs, CAD drawings, and optical designs have been provided that demonstrate a cohesive effort to produce handheld OCT probes. The original design uses what appears to be a combination of off-the-shelf and custom fabricated aluminum parts to allow for a relatively compact unit.<xref rid="r108" ref-type="bibr"><sup>108</sup></xref> A second-generation version<xref rid="r109" ref-type="bibr"><sup>109</sup></xref> further reduced the form factor and increased the optical capabilities of the first. As shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="f13">Fig. 13</xref>, a third-generation version produced high-resolution images of the retina in a compact handheld,<xref rid="r110" ref-type="bibr"><sup>110</sup></xref> shown in pediatric subjects, who are typically more difficult to image than adults.
