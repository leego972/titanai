This selective transmission preserves community-specific therapeutic practices and social prestige, a pattern noted in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>]. Sharing with outsiders is infrequent and often contingent on compensation, reflecting the cultural and economic value of traditional medicinal knowledge. Similar restrictions occur in Sudan, other African regions, and South America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>]. Transmission occurs primarily orally, with occasional knowledge reported from dreams or divine inspiration, a mode common in Ethiopia, Asia, and Latin America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>].</p><p id="Par235">Medicinal plant collection practices are guided by cultural beliefs and healer-specific customs, including timing, method, and collector identity. Some informants prefer morning or afternoon harvests, while others follow gender-specific rules. These practices resonate globally, where lunar cycles, spiritual status, and social norms are believed to influence the potency of remedies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>].</p><p id="Par236">Thus, these findings emphasize the intricate linkages among ethnobotanical knowledge, cultural practices, and spiritual beliefs. The predominance of familial transfer, coupled with generational disengagement, highlights the urgent need for systematic documentation and community-based programs to safeguard traditional knowledge—a concern shared globally in both developing and developed contexts [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec41"><title>Threats and conservation of Indigenous knowledge associated with medicinal plants</title><p id="Par237">MPs in the Karamara forest patches face multiple threats, with agricultural expansion identified as the primary driver of species loss. Additional pressures include firewood collection, charcoal production, timber extraction, and construction activities. Similar patterns have been reported across Ethiopia, where population growth and the need for agricultural land significantly threaten both general flora and medicinal plant species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>]. In the Eastern Nuba Mountains of Sudan, overharvesting for timber, agricultural expansion, and overgrazing were also highlighted as principal threats to wild edible and medicinal trees [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR115">115</xref>]. Comparable pressures are documented in Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan, where habitat degradation, urbanization, and unsustainable harvesting drive declines in medicinal plant populations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>– [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>].</p><p id="Par238">Indigenous knowledge associated with MPs is primarily transmitted orally, often within family lines and restricted to selected heirs, typically male children. The death of elder healers, combined with modernization, formal education, and cultural shifts, accelerates the erosion of authentic ethnomedicinal knowledge [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>]. Secrecy in knowledge transmission, practiced to maintain social respect and ensure compensation, further limits dissemination beyond the family, posing additional challenges to intergenerational continuity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>].</p><p id="Par239">Conservation of MPs in the Karamara forest is largely informal. Most species are harvested from the wild, with few structured protection measures.
