Wounds treated with blue light showed a positive but not significant increase in blood vessel formation (73 ± 27).</p><p>Throughout the treatment, both wound size and wavelength affected the amount of light being absorbed and scattered by the surrounding tissue. In this experiment, the radiative power for each wavelength was nominalized but the absorbed and transmitted energies of the therapy are beyond an experimental evaluation. In lieu, <xref rid="ijms-24-05895-f008" ref-type="fig">Figure 8</xref> (left) shows the remaining fractional reflection of light intensity of early wounds as an estimate of the power not penetrating the wound or deeper tissue. As expected, red light was mostly reflected, as evident by the surface color, commensally indicating the least energy penetrating the animal. Since the therapy was set calibrated for a constant illumination power, this demonstrates, in turn, that the most energy is being deposited at the wound site for blue light therapy.</p><p>The volume, which, together with the energy, is deposited, also varies dependent on the wavelength. <xref rid="ijms-24-05895-f008" ref-type="fig">Figure 8</xref> (right) shows the significant increase in wound temperature at the end of the treatment. Shorter wavelengths result in larger surface heating, while penetrating longer wavelengths result in a core temperature rise.</p><p>To obtain better insight into the governing thermal mechanism, further analysis of the wound surface temperature dynamics during therapy was performed. <xref rid="ijms-24-05895-f009" ref-type="fig">Figure 9</xref> shows the continually increasing mean wound surface temperature when exposed to the light therapy. Noticeably, there is no change in wound surface temperature in the control group for the first 140 s of the sham treatment. Only thereafter has enough time passed for the internally built up thermal energy due to kinetic activity and induced stressors to be transported to the surface.</p><p>In order to correct for the light deposition of thermal energy, we need to correct for the inherent internal heating of the control group by treating it as the zero-external heating value. The dashed lines in <xref rid="ijms-24-05895-f008" ref-type="fig">Figure 8</xref> show that the corresponding control group corrected least squares exponential plateau regression fits to the individual temperature readings.
