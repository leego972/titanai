A later study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">137</xref>] confirmed these results with respect to in vitro γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA)<sub>A</sub>-benzodiazepine receptor binding activity of an ethanolic <italic toggle="yes">H. opposita</italic> leaf extract.</p><p id="Par91">Cited in 7 use applications across three body systems for an RI of 71%, the root paste of <italic toggle="yes">Sarcocephalus latifolius</italic> is applied to incisions, or taken orally, as analgesic 'for any pains’. This use application is corroborated by multiple ethnobotanical sources cited by Burkhill [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>]. More recently, a highly controversial 2013 paper [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR138">138</xref>] claimed to have isolated from the root ( ±)cis-2-[(dimethylamino) methyl]-1-(3 methoxyphenyl) cyclohexanol—a morphine analog commonly known by its international non-proprietary name, tramadol. A 2016 study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>] followed up on this controversial finding with a comprehensive study of <italic toggle="yes">S. latifolius</italic> which identified multiple antinociceptive compounds, most notably indoloquinolizidine alkaloids to which they attributed antiplasmodial and antibacterial, as well as analgesic activities.</p><p id="Par92">Beyond the purely medical conditions which can be cross-referenced to the clinical data, respondents cited indications for more clinically ambiguous conditions which cannot be identified with confidence, which presented interpretive challenges in terms of data triangulation, which are discussed in the limitations section below. Four taxa were cited with reference to use applications loosely corresponding to ‘debility’—interpreted by the author to reflect use of the specified extract as an immunostimulatory tonic. Among them, the ‘bulb’ (corm) of <italic toggle="yes">Gladiolus dalenii</italic> was said to be chewed ‘to treat any sickness,' while the root paste of <italic toggle="yes">Kigelia africana</italic> was indicated as treatment for ‘general body weakness’. Respondents likewise ascribed immunostimulatory activities in the leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Cyphostemma adenocaule</italic> and the fruit of <italic toggle="yes">Strychnos innocua</italic> (‘you cannot get sick after eating’).</p><p id="Par93">Perhaps related to these applications are the two taxa more specifically cited for treatment of anemia—the bulb extract of <italic toggle="yes">Gloriosa superba</italic> (notoriously toxic due to its high levels of the cytotoxic alkaloid colchicine), and the root extract of <italic toggle="yes">Grewia villosa</italic>. The antihemolytic activity of G. superba extracts has been evaluated in in vitro studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR140">140</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR141">141</xref>], while a 2010 study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR142">142</xref>] reported in vitro antioxidant and in vivo hepatoprotective activity γ-lactones isolated from a stem extract of a species identified as '<italic toggle="yes">Grewia tiliaefolia</italic>' (presumably <italic toggle="yes">G. tiliifolia</italic> A.Rich., syn. <italic toggle="yes">G. amicorum</italic> Steud.).</p><p id="Par94">In another ambiguous indication, the root paste of <italic toggle="yes">Rhynchosia hirta</italic> was reported to be burnt, the smoke inhaled by patient ‘to chase devils’—interpreted by the author as an antipsychotic treatment. While the ethnobotanical literature holds no corroboration of this usage, the presence of alkaloids and saponins in <italic toggle="yes">Rhyncosia</italic> species as been noted [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR143">143</xref>] noted, to which antipsychotic activities have been [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR144">144</xref>].</p><p id="Par95">Other notable results which beg a degree of cultural interpretation include the reported use of <italic toggle="yes">Synedrella nodiflora</italic> in treatment of a condition called ‘false tooth’ or <italic toggle="yes">ebino</italic>. Although classified here as a dental condition, in fact there is no clinical basis to support a cultural belief, widespread within the region, that the eruption of primary canine teeth causes fever and diarrhea, often leading to removal of the emerging tooth buds using unsterilized instruments and without anesthesia or antiseptic [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR145">145</xref>]. Several years prior to the study, the author witnessed one such extraction within the Lango sub-region, effected with a sharpened bicycle spoke.</p><p id="Par96">The belief in ‘false tooth’ has been explained by emergence of the primary canine teeth corresponding to the time of first exposure to a range of ailments including diarrhea, which may lead to dehydration which may make the tooth bud more pronounced in appearance [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR145">145</xref>].
