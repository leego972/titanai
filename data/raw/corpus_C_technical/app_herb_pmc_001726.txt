Antioxidant Mechanism of Identified Compounds in <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic></title><p>The structural features of the flavonoids and phenolic acids required for antioxidant activity have been extensively investigated [<xref rid="B39-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>,<xref rid="B46-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>,<xref rid="B47-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>,<xref rid="B50-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>,<xref rid="B51-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>,<xref rid="B52-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. An antioxidant compound must donate hydrogen or a single electron to a reactive free radical to form an inactive species. When forming a resonance-stabilised radical intermediate that is comparatively more stable than the free radical, it becomes reduced. The overall result is the termination of damaging oxidative chain reactions. An example of the antioxidant mechanism of how flavonoids such as quercetin <bold>3</bold> can terminate the radical chain reaction is shown in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-sch001" ref-type="fig">Scheme 1</xref> [<xref rid="B53-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>,<xref rid="B54-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>]. Quercetin <bold>3</bold> can donate a proton and an electron to a free radical (R<sup>●</sup>) from its phenolic (OH) groups to form resonance stabilised <bold>3a</bold>–<bold>3b</bold>. The intermediate <bold>3b</bold> then reacts with a radical (O<sub>2</sub><sup>−●</sup>) to form <bold>3c</bold>, which can undergo ring epoxidation to provide <bold>3d</bold>. The decarboxylation of <bold>3d</bold> results in the formation of the stable benzoic derivative <bold>3e</bold>. The overall process leads to the termination of the damaging radical chain reactions.</p></sec><sec id="sec3dot1dot2-molecules-28-04396"><title>3.1.2. SAR of Antioxidants Compounds Identified in <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic></title><p>For flavonoids, the structure features required for antioxidant activity depend on the number and positions of hydroxyl (OH) groups, as well as other substituents and the glycosylation of the flavonoid nucleus [<xref rid="B39-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>]. Three structural features are essential determinants of the radical-scavenging activity of flavonoids: (i) the <italic toggle="yes">ortho</italic>-dihydroxy structure in the B-ring, (ii) the C2=C3 double bond in conjugation through the flavonoid nucleus, and (ii) the 4-oxo function in the C-ring. Flavonoids form complexes with the metal ions using the 3- or 5-hydroxyl and 4-keto substituents or hydroxyl groups in the <italic toggle="yes">ortho</italic> position in the B-ring, thus resulting in improved antioxidant efficacy. Studies of rare earth metal-chelated flavonol complexes confirmed that, in stable complex structures, the metal cations were found to chelate to 3-hydroxy and 4-keto sites [<xref rid="B55-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>,<xref rid="B56-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B57-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>]. Flavone glycosylation of the flavonoids at the 3- or 7-position diminishes their activity compared to the corresponding aglycones [<xref rid="B39-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>,<xref rid="B52-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. A systematic analysis of the SAR of flavonoid requirements for antioxidant activity is shown in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-f006" ref-type="fig">Figure 6</xref>.</p><p>Hydroxycinnamic acids were shown to have in vitro antioxidant activities against human low-density lipid (LDL) oxidation. The activities of these compounds were ranked as caffeic acid <bold>35</bold> &gt; ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> &gt; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold>. The presence of the <italic toggle="yes">ortho</italic>-dihydroxy group in the phenolic ring (as in caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>) was suggested for the antioxidant activity of hydroxycinnamic acids toward LDL in vitro oxidation [<xref rid="B58-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>]. The radical scavenging activity of these hydroxycinnamic acids follows a similar mechanism to the flavonoids based on their ability to donate hydroxyl hydrogen and/or an electron to form resonance-stabilised intermediates.
