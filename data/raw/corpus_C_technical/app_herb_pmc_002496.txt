These activities are often linked to <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic>’s high levels of flavonoids, tannins, and phenolic acids (Backiam et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">2023</xref>). Additionally, <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> contains highly aromatic essential oil compounds, including camphor, eugenol, chavicol, eucalyptol, and many others (Abraham et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">2023</xref>; Pandey et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">2014</xref>; Yamani et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">2016</xref>).</p><p id="Par31">However, there are varied reports about <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic>’s cytotoxic effects. As a food and herbal product, <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> is considered safe to consume, but some studies report cytotoxic effects against many human cancer cell lines. Harsha et al. (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">2020</xref>) reported an IC<sub>50</sub> range of 30–50 µg/ml of <italic toggle="yes">O. tenuiflorum</italic> extracts against the leukemic cell lines K562 in an MTT assay (Harsha et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">2020</xref>), and Lam et al. (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">2017</xref>) determined that <italic toggle="yes">O. tenuiflorum</italic> has an IC<sub>50</sub> less than 100 µg/mL against MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer lines (Lam et al. <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">2017</xref>). There are also reports that <italic toggle="yes">O. gratissimum</italic> inhibits breast cancer cell proliferation and pomolic acid isolated from <italic toggle="yes">O. gratissimum</italic> is as effective at inhibiting MCF-7 cells as a reference drug (Nangia-Makker et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">2007</xref>; Nganteng et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">2022</xref>). Other species of <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic>, like <italic toggle="yes">O. canum</italic>, have cytotoxic effects against MCF-7 breast cancer cells (IC<sub>50</sub> = 60 µg/ml) (Tamil Selvi et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">2015</xref>). Interestingly, Dolghi et al. (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">2021</xref>) reported that at concentrations lower than 75 µg/ml <italic toggle="yes">O. basilicum</italic> extracts have a stimulatory effect on HT-29 human colorectal cancer cell growth (Dolghi et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">2021</xref>).</p><p id="Par32">For this study, we evaluated the effects of three <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> species, <italic toggle="yes">O. tenuiflorum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">O. gratissimum</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">O. basilicum</italic> against the HT-29 human colorectal cell line. 25 of the 40 materials were grown in a greenhouse at The Pennsylvania State University under controlled conditions, and the remaining materials were ordered online as bulk herbs from various herbal product suppliers. All materials were extracted in 80% methanol in water with 0.1% formic acid prior to MTT cytotoxicity evaluations and untargeted UPLC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>).</p><p id="Par33">

</p><sec id="Sec13"><title>Ocimum materials display a range of cytotoxic effects against HT-29 cells</title><p id="Par34">There was a range of activities amongst the 40 evaluated <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> materials against human HT-29 colorectal cells (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>A). We determined the IC<sub>50</sub> of each extract via an MTT colorimetric assay, which measures cell viability after incubation with a botanical extract or compound of interest. We combined HT-29 cells with extracts of 0–200 µg/ml with 18 replicates of each <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> material in a 96-well plate. This range of concentrations allowed an accurate calculation of each extract’s IC<sub>50</sub>, which is reported as the average and standard deviation of three separate plating replicates.</p><p id="Par35">
