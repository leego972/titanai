Results</title><sec id="sec2dot1-ijms-26-05247"><title>2.1. Bioactivity and Antioxidant Potentials of Kh</title><p>The bioactivity as say was conducted via MTT and DPPH antioxidant assays. The plant crude extract reduced the viability of colon carcinoma cell lines (<italic toggle="yes">Caco-2</italic> and HT-29 cells) at a low-microgram concentration with IC50 values of 10 µg/mL and 17 µg/mL, respectively (<xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>A). Similarly, the plant crude extract shows a high antioxidant potential via the DPPH assay. <xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>B shows the IC50 value of crude Kh at a low-microgram concentration.</p></sec><sec id="sec2dot2-ijms-26-05247"><title>2.2. Kh Induced Dual Apoptotic and Autophagic Flux Degradation Inhibition in Caco-2 Cells</title><p>The cell cycle fraction analysis demonstrates a dose-dependent arrest of <italic toggle="yes">Caco-2</italic> cells at the G1 phase with increasing concentrations of Kh extract (<xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f002" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>). At lower concentrations, the normal transition of cells through the cell cycle was evidenced. The increase in concentration at 100 µg/mL resulted in a shift from the G2/M phase to G0/G1 arrest. This is indicative that Kh may inhibit progression through the G2/M checkpoint, potentially by modulating key regulators such as cyclin B1 and Cdk1, which are essential for G2/M transition. In addition, the G0/G1 arrest could be due to the damaged cellular components supposed to be removed via autophagy. Reports have established autophagy as a cellular process that promotes cell survival by facilitating the removal of damaged proteins during cell cycle transitions, especially during stress. The failure of autophagy flux degradation is integral to cell cycle arrest and consequent senescence or apoptotic induction.</p><p>Further examination of the mechanism of action via cellular phenotyping assay with immunofluorescence assay shows that nuclei of the treated cells show signs of DNA fragmentation compared to the control (red arrows in <xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f003" ref-type="fig">Figure 3</xref>). A dose-dependent degradation of tubulin was also observed (yellow arrows in <xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f003" ref-type="fig">Figure 3</xref>), and conversely, p62 (known for autophagosome degradation) shows increased autophagosome puncta at both concentration levels (blue arrows in <xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f003" ref-type="fig">Figure 3</xref>). p62 is a selective autophagy receptor, and its degradation occurs during active autophagy induction. The increased p62 puncta suggest inhibition of autophagic flux degradation.</p><p>Interestingly, a dose-dependent increase in late apoptosis was detected via Annexin V-FITC staining (<xref rid="ijms-26-05247-f004" ref-type="fig">Figure 4</xref>). Corroborating the earlier data of cell cycle fraction analysis arrest with autophagy flux degradation inhibition suggests the extract induced increased cellular stress that caused the shifting of earlier G2/M arrest observed at a lower concentration level to the G1 arrest at a higher concentration level and consequently cell cycle exit and apoptosis induction.</p></sec><sec id="sec2dot3-ijms-26-05247"><title>2.3.
