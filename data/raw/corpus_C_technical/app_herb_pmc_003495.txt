2026</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1"><title>Background</title><p id="Par5"> Plants have symbolized one of the most long-term basics of healthcare in human history. Archaeological and historical evidence demonstrates that plant-based healing practices were already well established in ancient China between 5000 and 4000 BC and in the Middle East and North Africa by around 1600 BC [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. Even with the rapid development of biomedical systems, MPs continue to play an essential role in global health, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where nearly 80% of the population still depends on traditional medicine for primary healthcare [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>].</p><p id="Par6">Ethnobotanical knowledge is not simply a collection of remedies; it is deeply interwoven with cultural identity, belief systems, and long-term relationships between communities and their surrounding ecosystems. Such knowledge is typically transmitted orally through generations and reflects locally adapted strategies for maintaining health and resilience. However, accelerating socio-economic transformations, environmental degradation, and climate-related pressures increasingly threaten both medicinal plant resources and the cultural knowledge associated with them. These challenges highlight the urgent need for systematic documentation of indigenous ethnomedicinal practices before they disappear [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>].</p><p id="Par7">Africa is widely recognized for its exceptional biological and cultural diversity, and traditional medicine remains the backbone of healthcare in many rural areas across the continent [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>]. In Somaliland, limited access to modern health facilities has resulted in widespread reliance on medicinal plants. More than half of the population, and over 95% of pastoral communities, depend on traditional remedies for disease prevention and treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>]. Somali ethnomedicine integrates herbal therapies with spiritual and magico-religious elements, reflecting a holistic understanding of illness and well-being [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>]. Despite its continued importance, much of this knowledge remains undocumented and is rapidly eroding as elder healers pass away without fully transmitting their expertise to younger generations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>].</p><p id="Par8">The Horn of Africa is considered a global biodiversity hotspot, yet Somaliland’s flora remains relatively understudied despite its richness and ecological importance [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>].
