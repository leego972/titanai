Traditional and Agronomic Uses of <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> Seeds</title><p><italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds possess enormous bioactive compounds associated with a wide range of biological properties. Thanks to these virtues, <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> plant seeds have been naturalized and adapted for use in agronomy and medicine [<xref rid="B15-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B22-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>]. Indeed, due to their high concentration of glucosinolates, seeds have been traditionally used as a spicy food condiment (Dijon mustard in France) [<xref rid="B23-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>,<xref rid="B24-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. Moreover, nonfood applications for seeds have become more popular, especially in the cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries. In traditional and modern medicine, mustard seeds are employed in various folk remedies as an appetizer, aperitif, digestive stimulant, laxative, expectorant, and antiseptic agent to treat gastrointestinal, respiratory, and skin diseases, as well as arthritis, foot aches, rheumatism, and lumbago [<xref rid="B15-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B25-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. In addition, because seeds are rich in proteins, they form a vital component of the food supply for pigs, poultry, and other types of livestock, in addition to aquaculture [<xref rid="B26-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>,<xref rid="B27-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>]. It has also been proven that <italic toggle="yes">S. alba</italic> seed meal is able to suppress weeds, while <italic toggle="yes">B. juncea seed</italic> meal is employed as a broad-spectrum pesticide against fungi, insects, and nematodes [<xref rid="B28-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Moreover, seeds play a crucial role in the soil enrichment and fertilization process due to the favorable C/N ratio, the high nitrogen-rich protein content (20–45%), and the presence of essential nutrients including phosphorus (1%), potassium (1%), calcium (1%), magnesium (0.5%), sulfur (0.5 to 2%), zinc (100 mg/kg of total dry matter), manganese (100 mg/kg of total dry matter), and copper (10 mg/kg of total dry matter) [<xref rid="B29-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. Similarly, soil amendment with 10 D intermediate doses (30 t/ha) of <italic toggle="yes">B. carinata</italic> seed meal improved soil fertility by enriching total organic carbon, humified carbon, and phosphorus without adverse effects on microorganisms [<xref rid="B30-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. With 87% coagulation activity, an active coagulant napin protein isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds (<italic toggle="yes">B. nigra</italic>) was described as a helpful method to treat pond water turbidity [<xref rid="B31-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec3-molecules-27-06008"><title>3. Edible Oil Profile</title><p>Vegetable oils rich in essential unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) are an indispensable element of the human diet and are often suggested by nutritionists [<xref rid="B32-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> plants are well known for their seeds’ richness in edible oil, with an average content in <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. juncea</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">B. rapa</italic> ranging from 45% to 50%. The oil content variability is attributable to genetic variances in <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> species, environmental conditions, and agricultural practices [<xref rid="B33-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. Among all the <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> species, <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic>, rapeseed, is one of the most important sources of edible oil.
