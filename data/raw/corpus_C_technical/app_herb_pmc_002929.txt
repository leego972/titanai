# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

Talmud (Gittin 69b), “<italic toggle="yes">Illava</italic>” is mentioned as a medicinal plant. It is held to be Aloe vera by most of the old Jewish commentaries [4, I:150].</p><p id="Par36">“<italic toggle="yes">Oren</italic>”—<italic toggle="yes">Pinus</italic> or <italic toggle="yes">Laurus nobilis</italic>? Amar [8:158] takes “<italic toggle="yes">Oren</italic>” (Isa. 44:14-15) to be <italic toggle="yes">Pinus halepensis</italic> Mill., under “A high level of the identification reliability but not sure.” Cedar in Akkadian is “<italic toggle="yes">Erenu</italic>” [112: 181-182]. In Campbell–Thompson’s view [20:282], “<italic toggle="yes">erini</italic>” or “<italic toggle="yes">erinu</italic>” is used as a general term for the coniferous tree, a view not accepted today. In the Talmud (Gittin 69b), there is a remedy called <italic toggle="yes">Atarafa d</italic>’<italic toggle="yes">ara</italic>’<italic toggle="yes">a</italic>” (אטרפא) against stomach worms; “<italic toggle="yes">tarfa</italic>” means a leaf and “<italic toggle="yes">de</italic>’<italic toggle="yes">ara</italic>’<italic toggle="yes">a</italic>” is translated by A. Steinsaltz [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR67">67</xref>] (in his commentary to Gittin 69b) as <italic toggle="yes">Laurus nobilis</italic>, based on the name of this species in other Semitic languages as “<italic toggle="yes">ar.</italic>”</p><p id="Par37">Campbell–Thompson [20:298] mentions “ēru” (which he identifies as <italic toggle="yes">L. nobilis</italic>) against “anus troubles”; this identification cannot be confirmed or denied because of the lack of sufficient cuneiform evidence. Therefore, Feliks’s [6:92] and Zohary’s [7:120] acceptance of “<italic toggle="yes">Oren</italic>” as <italic toggle="yes">Laurus</italic> has no solid evidence. It is worth mentioning that, as a rule that Campbell–Thompson’s [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>] identification is based mainly on Aramaic and Hebrew terms. So, following Campbell–Thompson, to clarify the Biblical plant terms, this might end in a vicious circle!</p><p id="Par38"><italic toggle="yes">Ceratonia siliqua</italic>. Despite the debate over whether <italic toggle="yes">Ceratonia</italic> is mentioned in the Bible [3, I: 393-407, 4:72-73; 113:passim, 114: passim], the plant clearly was widely distributed in the Holy Land as an indigenous species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">115</xref>]. The few archaeological findings of <italic toggle="yes">Ceratonia</italic> as: phytolites [116:1259]; wood [113:85; 117:112]; seed and fruits [118:101; 37:4] as well as pollen [119:12,18] indicate its presence in the Holy Land in the Bible period and earlier. All of the authorities agree that it was present here naturally, even if it is not mentioned directly in the Old Testament [113, 114, 116, 117 and references therein]. There is a debate [120: specimen No. 41; 4: 72-73; 7:63 and reference therein] if the “Locust” cited in Matthew 3:4 and the “pods” of Luke 15:16 are really <italic toggle="yes">Ceratonia</italic><bold>.</bold> The many Jewish post-Biblical references indicate its high importance as a food plant in the Holy Land [121:203-204; 3, II: 393-407]. Indeed, the presence of the carob in the Holy Land during the Bible period is quite certain; it is a common medicinal plant in the region [rev. 122].</p><p id="Par39">“<italic toggle="yes">Brosh</italic>”–<italic toggle="yes">Cupressus/Juniperus</italic>: Amar [8:159-161] discusses in detail the different Jewish historical suggestions for the identification of “<italic toggle="yes">brosh</italic>,” and summarizes: “it seems that we are speaking on an aggregate name for both genera <italic toggle="yes">Cupressu</italic>s and especially <italic toggle="yes">Juniperus</italic>.” Notably, Löw (<italic toggle="yes">Cupressus</italic>—[3, II:26-33] <italic toggle="yes">Juniperus</italic>—[3, II: 33-38]) and Felix [6: 79-80] have the same view. According to Zohary [7:106], “It probably refers to <italic toggle="yes">Abies cilicica</italic> (Antoine &amp; Kotchy) Carriére. Today, there is general agreement that the Akkadian word “<italic toggle="yes">burashu</italic>” denotes Juniper [112: 180-181). The Akkadian term for cypress is “s<italic toggle="yes">hurmenu</italic>” [112: 184]. Some of the previous ideas on this issue [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR122">123</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR124">125</xref>] are not accepted today.</p><p id="Par40">“<italic toggle="yes">Kikkayyon</italic>”–<italic toggle="yes">Ricinus</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">Lagenaria</italic>: Amar [8: 178-179] following [126: 352-354] suggests that the identification of the Hebrew “<italic toggle="yes">Kikkayyon</italic>” as <italic toggle="yes">Ricinus communis</italic> is of a “high but not sure level of identification's reliability” and that it could also be <italic toggle="yes">Lagenaria vulgaris</italic> Ser. ( = <italic toggle="yes">L. siceraria</italic> (Molina) Standl.). Although <italic toggle="yes">Lagenaria</italic> is known as a medicinal plant [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR126">127</xref>], it is not as common a medicinal plant as <italic toggle="yes">Ricinus</italic>.
