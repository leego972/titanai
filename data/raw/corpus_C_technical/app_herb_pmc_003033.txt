The area lies between 9°21′–9°23′ N latitude and 42°42′–42°43′ E longitude, at elevations of 1,921–2,255 m above sea level, representing a dryland highland–lowland transition zone with marked seasonal rainfall variability and recurrent droughts. Rugged terrain and rocky substrates have allowed fragments of natural vegetation to persist, despite widespread land-use change in the surrounding landscape [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>].</p><p id="Par16">Ecologically, the Karamara forest patches represent a mosaic of dry evergreen Afromontane remnants that gradually transition into woodland and shrubland formations. The vegetation is largely composed of drought-tolerant woody species, shrubs, climbers, and herbaceous plants that are well adapted to shallow soils and pronounced seasonal water stress. Floristically, the area is dominated by plant families such as Fabaceae, Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Lamiaceae, and Burseraceae, reflecting adaptation to semi-arid environmental conditions.</p><p id="Par17">The most widespread vegetation type in Karamara Forest is <italic toggle="yes">Acacia–Commiphora</italic> woodland and bushland, characterized by resilient tree and shrub species, particularly <italic toggle="yes">Acacia</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora</italic>. These taxa are well suited to the region’s semi-arid climate and play a key role in maintaining ecosystem functions. The woodland supports a rich diversity of ethnomedicinal plants that are widely used by local communities for healthcare and livelihood needs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par18">Extensive shrubland areas occur throughout the forest, especially in sites influenced by grazing and other human activities. These habitats are dominated by woody shrubs and small trees and provide important resources such as fuelwood, fodder, and medicinal plants. Despite their relatively open structure, shrublands contribute substantially to the overall biodiversity of the area.</p><p id="Par19">Grasslands with scattered trees are also present in open patches and seasonally grazed zones. These areas are dominated by herbaceous species and are particularly important for livestock grazing, especially during the rainy season when forage availability is high.</p><p id="Par20">Vegetation associated with riverine and seasonal stream habitats is found along temporary watercourses and in low-lying areas. These sites support relatively dense and diverse plant growth, including moisture-dependent herbs, shrubs, and trees that are less common in the surrounding dryland habitats. Thus, the vegetation of Karamara Forest reflects the combined influence of semi-arid climatic conditions, grazing pressure, and human use, while retaining considerable ecological and ethnobotanical significance.</p><p id="Par21">Many species serve multipurpose roles, providing medicinal, nutritional, and material resources. However, ongoing pressures such as agricultural expansion, overgrazing, firewood collection, and selective harvesting of roots and bark have led to fragmentation and ecological degradation, making the patches conservation-sensitive and several medicinally important species increasingly threatened [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par22">The local population is predominantly Somali, organized into subclans sharing language, culture, and Islamic faith. Customary institutions guide social interactions, resource access, and land management. Livelihoods are mainly agro-pastoral, combining crop cultivation with livestock rearing, supplemented by small-scale trading, charcoal production, and fuelwood collection. This dependence on forest resources directly shapes the use and management of medicinal plants.</p><p id="Par23">Healthcare in the region relies heavily on traditional medicine due to limited access to biomedical services, which are constrained by distance, cost, and personnel shortages. MPs harvested from the Karamara forest serve as primary remedies for a wide range of human ailments and are occasionally used alongside biomedical treatments when available. Ethnomedicinal knowledge is transmitted orally, primarily through elders, traditional healers, and knowledgeable family members, with apprenticeship and observational learning central to its maintenance. Knowledge transfer typically follows social norms and familial lines, though it is increasingly challenged by formal education, urbanization, and reduced engagement of younger generations with forest resources.</p><p id="Par24">By linking ecological characteristics with sociocultural reliance, the Karamara forest patches represent a critical biocultural landscape.
