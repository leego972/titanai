Assessment of the Antimicrobial Activity of Medicinal Plants Used in the Treatment of Human Diarrheal Cases in Rwanda</title><p>Six articles tested the antimicrobial activities of medicinal plants against diarrhea-causing agents. Twenty (20) medicinal plants were tested for antibacterial activity against <italic toggle="yes">Shigella</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Salmonella</italic> species and anti-diarrheal activity in mice. Of the 20 medicinal plants, 65% exhibited anti-diarrheal activities, with <italic toggle="yes">Myrica kandtiana</italic> inhibiting the growth of several bacteria [<xref rid="B44-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>]. However, another study reported that all seven medicinal plants tested were not effective against <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Salmonella</italic> [<xref rid="B48-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>]. Furthermore, different studies reported the anti-diarrheal activities of <italic toggle="yes">Bidens pilosa</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Tetradenia riparia</italic> [<xref rid="B46-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">Chenopodium ugandae</italic> [<xref rid="B56-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>]. Mugiraneza et al. [<xref rid="B56-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>] reported the in vitro activity of <italic toggle="yes">Chenopodium ugandae</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Erythrina abyssinica</italic> against <italic toggle="yes">Salmonella</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Shigella</italic> species. Other medicinal plants reported to have antimicrobial activities were <italic toggle="yes">Acacia sieberiana, Vernonia amygdalina,</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vernonia aemulans</italic> (antiviral); <italic toggle="yes">Clerodendrum myricoides</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia hirta</italic> (antibacterial and antiviral); <italic toggle="yes">Hibiscus surattensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Tetradenia riparia</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Thunbergia alata</italic> (antibacterial). Cos et al. [<xref rid="B51-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>] reported the antiviral and antibacterial activities of <italic toggle="yes">Tithonia diversifolia</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Chenopodium ugandae</italic>. Strong antifungal activity was reported for <italic toggle="yes">Clematis hirsuta</italic> against <italic toggle="yes">Candida albicans</italic> [<xref rid="B51-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec2dot4-antibiotics-10-01231"><title>2.4. Ethnopharmacology and Phytochemistry of Anti-Diarrheal Plants in Rwanda</title><p>Drug manufacturing using medicinal plants requires ethnopharmacological and phytochemistry studies that help in the validation process [<xref rid="B57-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>]. One of the extensively studied plants is <italic toggle="yes">Tetradenia riparia</italic>, for which some compounds (umuravumbolide and deacetylumuravumbolide) are named using root words of the Kinyarwanda language, and it is available in home gardens [<xref rid="B58-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>]. Recently, the anti-helminthic activity of <italic toggle="yes">T. riparia</italic> was found to be associated with 8(14),15-sandaracopimaradiene-7α,18-diol [<xref rid="B59-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>]. Furthermore, saponins and tannins from <italic toggle="yes">Tetradenia riparia</italic> have anti-helminthic activity [<xref rid="B45-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>,<xref rid="B59-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>].</p><p>The antibacterial activity of <italic toggle="yes">Vernonia amygdalina</italic> has been associated with sesquiterpene lactones (vernodalinol, vernolepin, vernomygdin, hydroxyvernolide, vernolide, and vernodalol) [<xref rid="B60-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia hirta</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic> are rich in quercitrin, which was found to stop diarrhea in mice, while <italic toggle="yes">Mangifera indica</italic> showed a high content of tannins, associated with astringent and anti-inflammatory effects [<xref rid="B61-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>].</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Tetradenia riparia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Leucas martinicensis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Monechma subsessile</italic> help to relieve gastrointestinal pain and combat diarrhea. All of these plants were found to have an antispasmodic activity [<xref rid="B37-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="B41-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">Chenopodium ugandae</italic> is known to be rich in terpene, sterols, saponosides, flavonoids, and traces of alkaloids while flowers of <italic toggle="yes">Erythrina abyssinica</italic> are rich in saponosides [<xref rid="B56-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>].</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Clerodendrum myricoides</italic> has been reported to exhibit strong antibacterial activities against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, which are attributed to high amounts of tannins, flavonoids, and alkaloids, with traces of terpenoids and saponins [<xref rid="B62-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec2dot5-antibiotics-10-01231"><title>2.5.
