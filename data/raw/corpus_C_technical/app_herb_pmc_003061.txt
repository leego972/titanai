Some species, such as <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, are actively cultivated in home gardens because of their frequent use, multiple applications, and ease of propagation. Community members reported practicing careful harvesting to avoid harming plant populations: leaves and fruits are collected without damage, roots and bark are taken only from mature plants to allow regrowth, and latex or resins are tapped without uprooting the species. These practices are especially emphasized for high-value plants like <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>.</p><p id="Par160">Community-based conservation measures include restricting grazing in areas rich in medicinal plants, protecting culturally important or sacred sites from clearing, and replanting harvested species such as <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>. The transfer of ethnomedicinal knowledge from elders to younger generations is crucial, teaching proper plant identification, preparation methods, and sustainable collection techniques. Cultural norms further reinforce conservation by discouraging the harvesting of rare or slow-growing species. Based on informant consensus and frequency of use, priority species for conservation include <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic>. These plants are protected both within the forest (in situ) and cultivated in home gardens (ex situ), reflecting an integration of traditional knowledge with ecological stewardship.</p></sec><sec id="Sec29"><title>Implications of medicinal plant use on health, food security, environmental protection, and cultural value</title><p id="Par161">The medicinal flora of the Karamara forest patches plays a central role in local primary healthcare, addressing most of the commonly reported health problems in the community. The majority of recorded species are used to treat infectious diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory illnesses, skin conditions, musculoskeletal problems, and febrile ailments. Widely used species such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe</italic> spp., <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Clerodendrum myricoides</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ruta chalepensis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus</italic> spp. show high informant agreement, indicating their strong therapeutic relevance. The continued dependence on plant-based remedies reflects both the accessibility of these resources and the limited availability and affordability of formal healthcare services. At the same time, the use of biologically potent and potentially toxic species such as <italic toggle="yes">Datura stramonium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia</italic> spp., and <italic toggle="yes">Ricinus communis</italic> demonstrates the importance of specialized indigenous knowledge for safe dosage and preparation, highlighting both the strength and vulnerability of the traditional medical system.</p><p id="Par162">MPs also make an important contribution to household food security and nutrition. Several species provide edible fruits, leaves, seeds, or pods that supplement daily diets, especially during periods of drought and food scarcity. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Opuntia ficus-indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Tamarindus indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus dubius</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic> were frequently cited as both food and medicine, reflecting their dual nutritional and therapeutic value.
