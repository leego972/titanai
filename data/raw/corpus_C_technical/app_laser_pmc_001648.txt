Retinal imaging was also demonstrated independently by the Medical University of Vienna, Fercher et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b20" ref-type="bibr">20</xref></sup> The MIT study used the prototype (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f06">Fig. 6</xref>), which had a high-speed scanning delay (160 mm/s) enabling rapid image acquisition and real-time display, including software correction of axial eye motion. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f07">Figure 7</xref> shows an in vivo human retinal image with 15-μm axial resolution at 840-nm wavelength. The nerve fiber layer and other architectural features could be visualized with higher resolution than previously possible. The first in vivo images of the anterior chamber were also demonstrated shortly thereafter by our group, Izatt et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref></sup> We were excited, but the ultimate clinical use was unclear.</p><fig id="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f07" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Figure 7</label><caption><p>First in vivo OCT image of the normal retina in a human subject. The image shows the retinal architectural morphology including the choroid, retinal pigment epithelium, nuclear layers, plexiform layers, and retinal nerve fiber layer.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref></sup> Reprinted with permission from Swanson EA, Izatt JA, Hee MR, et al. In vivo retinal imaging by optical coherence tomography. <italic toggle="yes">Opt Lett</italic>. 1993;18:1864–1866. © 1993 Optical Society of America.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f07.jpg"/></fig></sec><sec id="s3c"><title>Clinical Studies at New England Eye Center Suggest the Potential of OCT</title><p>Using the MIT prototype, we began clinical studies in the mid-1990s, working with Carmen Puliafito and Joel Schuman at the New England Eye Center (NEEC) of Tufts University School of Medicine (Boston, MA, USA). Michael Hee was an expert programmer and using the Apple Macintosh, he developed OCT examination protocols including circumpapillary scanning to assess glaucoma<sup>22,23</sup> and radial scanning to map macular edema.<sup>24,25</sup> These methods extracted quantitative information from images and were an important step toward objective assessment of progression and response to therapy. These protocols were later adopted in commercial OCT instruments and continued as clinical standards for decades. Michael Hee published over 30 papers during his doctorate and his 1997 thesis, “Optical coherence tomography of the eye,” continues to be a reference on OCT ophthalmic design. These examples of undergraduate and doctoral research demonstrate that it is possible to make powerful contributions even at an early career stage.</p><p>Over 5000 patients were imaged at NEEC under support from the National Institutes of Health in the mid-1990s and the collaborative team investigated OCT in retinal disease and glaucoma.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b23" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b24" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b25" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b26" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b27" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b28" ref-type="bibr"/><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b29" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref></sup> Carmen Puliafito organized the first OCT atlas “Optical coherence tomography of ocular diseases” in 1996,<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup> which provided a framework for interpreting OCT of retinal pathologies.</p></sec><sec id="s3d"><title>Entrepreneurship and Corporate Investment</title><p>In order to impact clinical care, entrepreneurship and commercialization are critical. In 1992, we (Puliafito C, Swanson E, Fujimoto J) founded an MIT startup company, Advanced Ophthalmic Diagnostics (AOD), to commercially develop ophthalmic OCT. Although the technology, clinical, and regulatory barriers were high and there was limited evidence that OCT would be accepted by the ophthalmic community, there was a strong belief, even at this early stage, that OCT would be impactful and that a startup would expedite its impact on patient care.
