According to 90% of respondents, the transfer of indigenous plant knowledge occurs orally rather than through written documentation, underscoring the fragility of this knowledge system.</p><p id="Par154">The timing of medicinal plant collection varied considerably among practitioners and was largely guided by individual beliefs. Approximately 30% preferred collecting plants before 4:00 p.m., while 70% favored early morning (up to 9:00 a.m.) or late afternoon (after 4:00 p.m.). Some practitioners considered nighttime collection suitable, whereas others emphasized early morning or early afternoon.</p><p id="Par155">Local beliefs also shaped the collection and application of medicinal plants. For instance, some remedies were to be collected only by single females or by males, based on the conviction that deviation from these practices would reduce the efficacy of the herbal medicine. Such practices reflect the integration of cultural norms and spiritual considerations into traditional healthcare in the Karamara forest patches.</p></sec><sec id="Sec27"><title>Threats to medicinal plants in Karamara forest patches</title><p id="Par156">A ranking exercise with ten key informants (R1–R10) was conducted to identify the main threats to MPs in the Karamara forest patches. Informants scored each threat based on perceived severity, and cumulative scores were used to establish the ranking (Table <xref rid="Tab11" ref-type="table">11</xref>).</p><p id="Par157">Agricultural expansion emerged as the most serious threat, followed by firewood collection, indicating that both land-use change and harvesting pressures strongly affect the availability of medicinal plants. Charcoal production ranked third, with construction activities and overgrazing in fourth and fifth positions, respectively. Fodder collection and modernization were considered moderate threats (sixth and seventh), while drought was ranked lowest, reflecting its relatively minor perceived impact on local plant resources.</p><p id="Par158">
