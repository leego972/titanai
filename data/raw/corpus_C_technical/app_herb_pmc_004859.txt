A number of pathologic states are associated with disturbances in the functional activity of certain GPCRs (Lefkowitz, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B65">1996</xref>). GPCRs are extremely important targets for neuropsycho-pharmacology (Roth et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B102">1998</xref>). Many neuropsychiatric drugs either bind directly to specific GPCRs (e.g., antipsychotics) or affect GPCRs indirectly by influencing the amount of available native agonist (e.g., antidepressants; Von Zastrow, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B121">2002</xref>).</p><p>Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T6">6</xref> shows that adaptogens down-regulated the <italic toggle="yes">HTR1A</italic> gene encoding the 5-HT<sub>3</sub> GPCR, which is known to activate an intracellular second messenger cascade resulting in excitatory or inhibitory neurotransmission (Hoyer et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B46">1994</xref>). Activation of serotonin receptors modulate the release of many neurotransmitters including glutamate, GABA, dopamine, epinephrine/norepinephrine, and acetylcholine, as well as many hormones including oxytocin, prolactin, vasopressin, cortisol, corticotropin, substance P, and others. Activation of serotonin receptors also influence various biological and neurological processes such as aggression, anxiety, appetite, cognition, learning, memory, mood, nausea, sleep, and thermoregulation. A variety of pharmaceutical agents and illicit drugs, including many antidepressants and antipsychotics, target serotonin receptors (Yakel, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B134">2000</xref>; Kennett et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">1997</xref>). The binding of serotonin to the 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptor in neuronal cells of the central (e.g., brain-stem cells) and peripheral nervous systems opens the membrane channels, which in turn leads to an excitatory response and anxiety (Kennett et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">1997</xref>). Thus, the observed down-regulation of the <italic toggle="yes">HTR1A</italic> gene by tyrosol (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T6">6</xref>) is in line with publications that have demonstrated the anti-depressive effects of tyrosol in rats (Wikman and Panossian, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B132">2004</xref>; Panossian et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B88">2008</xref>) and recent publications showing that <italic toggle="yes">Rhodiola</italic> extracts affect serotonin receptors (Chen et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B19">2009b</xref>; Mannucci et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B70">2012</xref>). Serotonin receptors are also known to regulate longevity (Murakami and Murakami, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B75">2008</xref>) and behavioral aging (Murakami et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B74">2008</xref>) in the nematode <italic toggle="yes">Caenorhabditis elegans</italic>. This agrees with several publications demonstrating the beneficial effects of <italic toggle="yes">R. rosea</italic> on the lifespan of <italic toggle="yes">C. elegance</italic> and the fruit fly, <italic toggle="yes">Drosophila melanogaster</italic> (Jafari et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B51">2007</xref>; Wiegant et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B130">2009</xref>).</p><p>G-protein-coupled lysophospholipid receptors regulate cellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> homoeostasis, cytoskeletal architecture, proliferation and survival, migration, and adhesion. They have been implicated in development; regulation of the cardiovascular; immune and nervous systems; inflammation; arteriosclerosis; and cancer. Lysophospholipid receptor ligands bind to and activate their corresponding transmembrane receptors. The activated receptors have a wide range of cellular effects depending on which ligand, receptor, and cell-type is involved. Primary effects include inhibition of adenylyl cyclase and release of calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum, while secondary effects include preventing apoptosis and increasing cell proliferation (Meyer zu Heringdorf and Jakobs, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B72">2007</xref>). Down-regulation of GPCRs by adaptogens (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T6">6</xref>) indicates that the number of receptors is greatly reduced leading to strongly attenuated signal transduction via G-proteins and their effectors (Von Zastrow, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B121">2002</xref>), which may contribute to the beneficial effect of adaptogens in stress-related disorders.</p><p>G-protein-coupled receptor expression and G-protein-mediated signal transduction are affected by age and play an important role in the development of the major human pathologies associated with aging, cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases (Joseph et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B52">1993</xref>; Alemany et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B3">2007</xref>).
