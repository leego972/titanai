The district covers an area of approximately 2,960 km² and lies within an altitudinal range of 1,430–1,500 m above sea level. Sheikh District is bordered by the Togdher Region to the northeast and Odwayne District to the southwest, and it was officially established as an administrative district in 1973. Administratively, the district comprises 27 kebeles, including 23 rural and 4 urban settlements (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>).</p><p id="Par14">Sheikh town, the administrative center of the district, is situated within the Golis Mountain Range along the main route between Berbera and Burao, approximately 70 km north of Burao. The town holds strong historical and cultural importance in Somali society and is widely recognized as a traditional center of learning. Its mountainous landscape, relatively cool climate, and high air quality make it one of the most scenic and ecologically distinctive areas in Somaliland [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par15">Climatically, the district experiences a bimodal rainfall pattern, with two main rainy seasons: Gu (April–June) and Dayr (October–November). Rainfall peaks in May, reaching around 300 mm, while January is typically the driest month (9.99 mm). The mean annual rainfall and average temperature are approximately 241 mm and 19.1 °C, respectively. The warmest period occurs between March and June, whereas January and February represent the coldest months of the year [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>].</p><p id="Par16">The livelihoods of local communities are predominantly agro-pastoral. Around 70% of households rely mainly on livestock production, while the remaining 30% engage in crop cultivation and small-scale trade. Approximately 70% of the district’s land is used as rangeland, while 30% is devoted to agricultural activities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>]. Sheikh District has an estimated population of about 90,469 inhabitants. Although unemployment remains high, agriculture, livestock trade, and small businesses provide the primary sources of income. Due to its historical administrative role during the British protectorate period, Sheikh retains relatively better-developed educational and health facilities compared with many other rural areas in Somaliland.</p><p id="Par17">Ecologically, Sheikh District supports diverse plant resources, largely due to its mountainous terrain and semi-arid transitional environment. Dominant indigenous and introduced species include <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">V. tortilis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">V. senegal</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Azadirachta indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. glabra</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. orbicularis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Buxus hildebrandtii</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Cordia gharaf</italic>. However, these plant resources are increasingly threatened by deforestation, charcoal production, unsustainable harvesting, and climate variability. Such pressures pose risks not only to biodiversity but also to the continuity of indigenous ethnomedicinal knowledge systems that depend on these plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par18">Indigenous communities in Sheikh District possess extensive ethnomedical and ecological knowledge developed through long-term interaction with their surrounding landscapes. Elders and knowledgeable community members classify landforms, soils, and vegetation based on locally meaningful indicators such as topography, soil texture and color, plant density, and dominant species. In particular, they recognize three broad landscape categories: the coastal plain (Guban), the mountain zone (Oogo), and the flat plateau (Hawd), each associated with distinct vegetation and ecological conditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>].</p><p id="Par19">Medicinal plant availability and use are closely linked to these indigenous landscape classifications. Local communities further distinguish vegetation into three land-cover types: mountain and highland vegetation, coastal plain vegetation, and plateau vegetation. These categories are consistently recognized throughout the district and guide the collection, management, and cultural use of medicinal plants.</p><p id="Par20">Mountain and highland areas are characterized by evergreen, shrub-dominated vegetation growing mainly on rocky black soils locally known as carro-madow.
