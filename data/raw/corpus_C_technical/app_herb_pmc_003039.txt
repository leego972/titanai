This approach provided insight into community treatment priorities and perceptions of plant efficacy.</p><p id="Par64">To assess multipurpose utility beyond medicinal use, direct matrix ranking was conducted. Five commonly cited tree species were evaluated by ten key informants across seven categories: medicinal value, construction material, firewood, charcoal, furniture, agricultural tools, and food. Informants scored each category from 1 (least important) to 5 (most important). Summed scores identified species valued for both therapeutic and socio-economic roles, informing conservation priorities and highlighting the broader importance of MPs in rural livelihoods [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>].</p><sec id="Sec7"><title>Data analysis</title><p id="Par65">A mixed-methods approach combining qualitative and quantitative analyses was used. Qualitative data local plant names, ailments, and preparation methods were coded, categorized according to standard ethnomedicinal classifications, and validated through cross-checking with multiple informants. Thematic analysis identified patterns of plant use and cultural knowledge.</p><p id="Par66">Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics and ethnobotanical indices (e.g., Informant Consensus Factor, Use Value) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>]. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.3), with normality assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in indices across age, gender, and informant type were evaluated using ANOVA or non-parametric alternatives as appropriate. Pearson correlation and linear regression examined relationships between age and the number of MPs reported [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>]. This integrated framework provided a robust understanding of medicinal plant knowledge, its demographic determinants, and regional patterns of use.</p></sec><sec id="Sec8"><title>Ethical considerations</title><p id="Par67">The study received official approval from the Department of Biology, Jigjiga University, and the administrative offices of Jigjiga district. Research was conducted in accordance with established ethnobotanical ethical guidelines. Prior informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study’s objectives, methods, and voluntary nature. Confidentiality was maintained, and personal identifiers were removed from the dataset. Special care was taken to respect cultural sensitivities, particularly regarding sacred or ritually significant plants. The study adhered to international ethical standards, including the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE), ensuring the protection and respectful use of indigenous knowledge.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Results</title><sec id="Sec10"><title>Diversity and distribution of medicinal plants in Karamara forest patches</title><p id="Par68">A total of 85 medicinal plant species belonging to 70 genera and 40 families were documented in the Karamara forest patches (Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>). Among these, Fabaceae was the most dominant family, followed by Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Lamiaceae and Burseraceae. Several other families were represented by a single species, highlighting the heterogeneous composition of the forest’s medicinal flora (Fig. <xref rid="Fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref>).</p><p id="Par69">
