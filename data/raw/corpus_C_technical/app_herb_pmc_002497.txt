</p><p id="Par36">According to Nordin et al., extracts with an IC<sub>50</sub> between 20 µg/ml and 100 µg/ml are considered ‘active’ (but not highly active) in MTT assays (Nordin et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">2018</xref>). All but one of the tested extracts fell within the ‘active’ category (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>A). The range of activity levels falls within the expected range based on previous <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> cytotoxicity reports against various cell lines (Dolghi et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">2021</xref>; Harsha et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">2020</xref>; Lam et al. <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">2017</xref>; Torres et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">2018</xref>). We did not find any highly active materials but expected that the observed IC<sub>50</sub> range of 57.21–116.16 µg/ml would provide a broad enough activity range to identify compounds that explain the observed variation. Since there are no highly active materials, we did not expect to identify any potent cytotoxic compounds. Instead, we aimed to discover medium-active compounds associated with the observed shifts in <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> biological activity.</p><p id="Par37">The lowest IC<sub>50</sub> resulted from an <italic toggle="yes">O. basilicum</italic> material; six of the 10 most active samples were <italic toggle="yes">O. basilicum</italic>, and the remaining four were split between <italic toggle="yes">O. gratissimum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">O. tenuiflorum</italic> (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>A). This is not to say <italic toggle="yes">O. basilicum</italic> is more active than the other two species; four of the ten least active samples were also <italic toggle="yes">O. basilicum</italic>. Overall, there were no clear species-based patterns guiding <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> cytotoxicity. Similarly, there is no evidence that greenhouse or consumer products trend toward a higher or lower IC<sub>50</sub> (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>A).</p><p id="Par38">We next investigated if there were any underlying patterns in the overall metabolome guiding cytotoxic activity (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>B). Using the untargeted metabolite profiles, we conducted Principal Component Analysis (PCA); there were not clear patterns between the samples’ metabolite profiles and IC<sub>50</sub>. This indicated that the shifts in bioactivity are likely due to metabolites that are not the main drivers of variation amongst the materials. In addition, the source of the sample, either greenhouse grown or commercial product, did have a clear distinction in the PCA (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>C), and is a potential driving factor for metabolite diversity; other factors, like environment or processing, could also potentially be the key drivers of metabolite diversity within the basil samples (Abraham et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2025</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="Sec14"><title>Initial feature list is uninformative for predicting biological activity</title><p id="Par39">As unsupervised PCA provided minimal information about the metabolites associated with the differences in <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum</italic> cytotoxicity, we employed a multi-step data filtration and multivariate statistical modeling approach to identify the key metabolites driving bioactivity diversity amongst our materials. We utilized a Partial Least Squares – Regression (PLS-R) model to discover these important features (Wold et al., <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">2001</xref>) based on collinearity and covariance with the dependent variable (bioactivity).
