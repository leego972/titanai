Specific to AMD, OCT software algorithms for the automated measurement of drusen load in intermediate disease (area and volume) and geographic atrophy in late disease (area and proximity to the foveal centre) may also be clinically meaningful, particularly for disease monitoring;<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0050" ref-type="ref">50</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0051" ref-type="ref">51</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0052" ref-type="ref">52</xref> however, they are prone to the same decentration and segmentation errors.</p><p>The signs of late AMD are often subtle and ill‐defined. To date, OCT‐A for the detection of choroidal neovascularisation has garnered interest but remains heavily user‐dependent, with sensitivities ranging between 50–85 per cent.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0012" ref-type="ref">12</xref> Early detection using OCT still requires careful, systematic scrutiny of multiple layers. Injudicious reliance on global parameters, such as macular thickness values (Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0002" ref-type="fig">2</xref>B, C), is not recommended, although other <italic toggle="yes">en face</italic> scans may be useful in providing a holistic view of the area and volume of involved regions. Significant signs, such as drusen regression,<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0053" ref-type="ref">53</xref> drusen subtypes<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0054" ref-type="ref">54</xref> or nascent geographic atrophy<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0055" ref-type="ref">55</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0056" ref-type="ref">56</xref> may also be better identified using other methods (Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0003" ref-type="fig">3</xref>A, B). </p></sec></sec><sec id="cxo12847-sec-0011"><title>Diabetic retinopathy (DR)</title><p>Clinically, DR is subdivided into non‐proliferative, proliferative, and diabetic macular oedema (DMO) which may occur at either the non‐proliferative or proliferative stage. The non‐proliferative sub‐type is characterised by specific ophthalmoscopic signs, including microaneurysms, dot or blot intra‐retinal haemorrhages, intra‐retinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMA), venous calibre abnormalities, cotton wool spots, and hard exudates (intra‐retinal lipid).<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0057" ref-type="ref">57</xref> Proliferative diabetic retinopathy is characterised by neovascularisation on the optic disc or elsewhere with or without pre‐retinal or vitreous haemorrhage.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0058" ref-type="ref">58</xref> The number and distribution of lesions form the basis for current clinical classification systems.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0057" ref-type="ref">57</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0058" ref-type="ref">58</xref> While dilated fundus examination is the recommended method of grading DR using classifications such as the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy and Diabetic Macular Edema Disease Severity Scales,<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0058" ref-type="ref">58</xref> these techniques are increasingly being supplemented by advanced <italic toggle="yes">en face</italic> and cross‐sectional imaging techniques such as widefield scanning laser ophthalmoscopy and OCT (Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0004" ref-type="fig">4</xref>A).
