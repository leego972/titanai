One of the most important defense mechanisms of the nasal muco-ciliated epithelium is the muco-ciliated clearance and the function of the cilia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>]. There are data supporting the significant and longstanding deregulation of the clearance capability of the nasal mucosa that can last up to 32 days in patients suffering from common cold [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>]. This damage includes both a reduction in the population of of ciliated cells and a change in the frequency of cilia movement [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>]. For postviral rhinosinusitis and ABRS, new systematic reviews and meta-analyses have also recently been performed and we discuss some of the most interesting aspects.</p></sec><sec id="Sec7"><title>Traditional Medicines</title><sec id="Sec8"><title>Antibiotics</title><p id="Par46">Eleven randomized controlled trials conducted after EPOS2012 compared antibiotic treatment versus placebo in patients with symptoms of common cold. Indicatively, a total of 1047 participants who received antibiotics for treating common cold had no better results to their outcome or persistence of complaints compared to those who received placebo (RR response rate 0.95, 95% CI confidence interval 0, 59 to 1.51) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Adult participants who received antibiotics had a significantly higher risk of adverse events than those who received placebo (RR 2.62, 95% CI 1.32 to 5.18) (random effects), whereas the same was not found in the pediatric population (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.51 to 1.63) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>]. The conclusion was that there is no evidence to support the use of antibiotics for common cold or for acute purulent rhinitis in pediatric or adult population [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>]. In contrast, it is well known that antibiotics can have a large amount of side effects when prescribed in adults for common cold and regardless of age when prescribed for purulent rhinitis, therefore antibiotic prescription for these pathologies is not recommended [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Nasal Corticosteroids</title><p id="Par47">The anti-inflammatory effect of nasal corticosteroids is considered to have a beneficial effect on common cold symptoms [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. In 2013, a Cochrane review compared nasal corticosteroids with placebo and standard treatment measures for the common cold in children and adults [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>]. A total of 353 patients were included of which, two trials compared nasal corticosteroids with placebo, while one trial compared nasal corticosteroids with usual symptomatic treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>]. In the two placebo-controlled studies, no benefit from the use of nasal corticosteroids was depicted in terms of length or intensity of complaints [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>].
