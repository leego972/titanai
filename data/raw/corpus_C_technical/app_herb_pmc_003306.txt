For example, <italic toggle="yes">Acmella oleracea</italic> was named <italic toggle="yes">jambu</italic> in our survey, and we traced back this name to circa 375 years ago in the <italic toggle="yes">Historia Naturalis Brasiliae</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>], where it was documented for the same species, in a slightly different spelling (<italic toggle="yes">nhambí</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">nhambu</italic>).</p><p id="Par27">Substantial transformations in social and environmental factors are needed for plant names to change. Migration plays a large role, as migrants usually bring along their own language, plant uses, and cultural elements, including words for plants that may later be included in the dominant language [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>]. In Tanzania, vernacular plant names at urban markets changed as a result of the migration from rural to urban centers of people with different cultural and lingual backgrounds [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>]. Apparently, Belém’s cultural and linguistic context and its natural surroundings have not changed enough in the past decades to stimulate large shifts in vernacular plant names. Even though Belém is a large and expanding city, its surroundings are still inhabited by peoples who have valued and traded in these herbal medicines in the past decades. Their ethnobotanical knowledge of medicinal plants, including vernacular names, is an essential element to their survival and a source of income. Thus, as people worked with these medicinal plants consistently over the past decades, and languages remained the same, it resulted in the preservation of these vernacular names.</p><p id="Par28">Regarding presently recorded names of species, of which the species were not recorded previously at VOP, a broader analysis of names, involving ethnobotanical surveys in and around Pará, could further confirm or refute our results on long-term preservation of plant names in the area.</p></sec><sec id="Sec13"><title>Language origins</title><p id="Par29">The largest part of the vernacular plant names at this Amazonian market bore names of Portuguese origin. For example, the vernacular name <italic toggle="yes">arruda</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Ruta graveolens</italic>) is of Portuguese origin, and the species is native to the Mediterranean region where it is embedded in the local plant pharmacopeia and also used against the “evil eye” [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>]. We noticed this that several vendors at the VOP wore a sprig of <italic toggle="yes">arruda</italic> to protect against the “evil eye,” an interesting usage also commonly found in Bahia among Candomblé practitioners [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>]. Bussmann et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>] also found that most plants sold at markets in Colombia, Bolivia, and Peru have mostly Spanish names. However, in northern Peru, Bussmann and Sharon [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>] found that Spanish names were mainly used for introduced and coastal plant species whereas plants from the montane forests were often referred to by their indigenous name. In our case, there were relatively few plants of European origin at the VOP market and most plants with Portuguese vernacular names were native Brazilian species, such as, <italic toggle="yes">Mansoa alliacea</italic>, called <italic toggle="yes">cipó de alho</italic> (garlic vine).</p><p id="Par30">The second largest group of vernacular names had a Tupi origin. Names like <italic toggle="yes">cajú</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">tapereba</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">buçu</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">sicuriju</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">jambu</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">tauari</italic> reflect an acquisition of indigenous words in the Brazilian lexicon for living organisms found in the natural world, also evidenced by the native and especially Amazonian plants that were sold at the market. Portuguese-speaking people have inhabited the Belém area since the seventeenth century, and their language gradually became the official language [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>]. Nevertheless, Tupinambá indigenous people were still present around Belém during the turn of the twentieth century [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>], and Tupi, Tupinambá, and <italic toggle="yes">língua geral</italic> (a mix of Tupi and Portuguese which served as lingua franca in the region) were spoken in Pará up until the nineteenth or twentieth centuries [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>], alongside various other spoken languages also belonging to the Tupi-Guarani language family [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>]. This indicates that while the Portuguese-speaking peoples that inhabited the area mainly used Portuguese words to describe the natural world, they also relied upon indigenous Tupi names to some degree.
