# PMC: optical coherence tomography clinical application — Overview

ref-type="ref">135</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0136" ref-type="ref">136</xref> It is not surprising that OCT has become indispensible for clinical glaucoma assessment, especially when its speed, precision and relative objectivity contrasts so heavily with the arduous, subjective and variable task of automated perimetry.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0137" ref-type="ref">137</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0138" ref-type="ref">138</xref> </p></sec><sec id="cxo12847-sec-0018"><title>Limitations of OCT imaging in glaucoma</title><p>Given these advantages, is there still a place for tests aside from OCT for assessing glaucomatous damage? As mentioned already, there are a number of key limitations with using OCT imaging in glaucoma. OCT artefacts in glaucoma assessment<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0139" ref-type="ref">139</xref> may contribute to the manifestation of red‐green disease (for example, Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0007" ref-type="fig">7</xref>C, D),<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0140" ref-type="ref">140</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0141" ref-type="ref">141</xref> as determination of the statistical significance of structural loss is contingent upon the underlying normative database.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0142" ref-type="ref">142</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0143" ref-type="ref">143</xref> This is particularly relevant in cases of patient demographics that are not well‐represented by the normative database (for example, high myopia; Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0008" ref-type="fig">8</xref>A). There has been interest in developing normative data for these demographics but there remain challenges associated with the determination of normality in such patients.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0144" ref-type="ref">144</xref>

Abbreviations: as with Figures <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0002" ref-type="fig">2</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0003" ref-type="fig">3</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0004" ref-type="fig">4</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0005" ref-type="fig">5</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0006" ref-type="fig">6</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0007" ref-type="fig">7</xref>.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="nlm-graphic-15" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="CXO-102-242-g008.jpg"/></fig><p>Another critical limitation of OCT is the instrument measurement floor in advanced disease.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0145" ref-type="ref">145</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0146" ref-type="ref">146</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0147" ref-type="ref">147</xref> Remaining non‐neuronal cells or retinal vasculature produce a resultant thickness measurement that is not indicative of actual neuronal change relevant in glaucoma.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0105" ref-type="ref">105</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0148" ref-type="ref">148</xref> In such cases, visual field results may be more reliable and informative. This has given rise to the idea of different utility of OCT and visual fields in different stages of glaucoma.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0149" ref-type="ref">149</xref> More recent studies have clarified the discordance in early glaucoma by using different perimetric stimuli.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0150" ref-type="ref">150</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0151" ref-type="ref">151</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0152" ref-type="ref">152</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0153" ref-type="ref">153</xref> However, the issue with current OCT results providing only limited dimensional information of optic nerve head structure remains. This is an inherent limitation of an optical impression of ocular structure that does not truly represent the underlying anatomy.</p><p>Unlike in AMD and DR, OCT imaging of treatment‐naïve eyes with glaucoma does not necessarily yield ‘classic’ signs, and instead displays significant overlap with other disorders affecting the optic nerve head, such as retinal vascular occlusions and ischaemic optic neuropathies (Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0008" ref-type="fig">8</xref>B–D).<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0154" ref-type="ref">154</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0155" ref-type="ref">155</xref> This further reinforces the idea of glaucoma as a disease of statistical abnormality (for example, statistically depressed visual field or OCT results).<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0145" ref-type="ref">145</xref> As alluded to above, other optic nerve head appearances or variations may mimic glaucomatous damage, and provide a false impression of pathological changes that may be better assessed using alternative clinical techniques (for example, tilted disc syndrome or myopic optic discs).<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0156" ref-type="ref">156</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0157" ref-type="ref">157</xref> OCT in isolation, therefore, does not clearly provide a conclusive diagnosis of glaucoma.</p></sec><sec id="cxo12847-sec-0019"><title>Key points</title><p>
<list list-type="bullet" id="cxo12847-list-0003"><list-item id="cxo12847-li-0007"><p>There exist a number of useful OCT markers for diagnosing glaucoma, particularly in early stages, including structural asymmetry, characteristic ‘wedge’‐like and ‘arcuate’‐like defects, and change over time.</p></list-item><list-item id="cxo12847-li-0008"><p>The measurement of optic disc and peripapillary perfusion using OCT‐A, structural OCT imaging of the lamina cribrosa, and anterior segment OCT applied for visualisation of the anterior chamber angle may be helpful in separating eyes with glaucoma from suspects.</p></list-item><list-item id="cxo12847-li-0009"><p>
<italic toggle="yes">Limitations</italic>: Glaucoma is a disease of statistical abnormality and thus, OCT in isolation cannot provide a conclusive diagnosis of glaucoma.
