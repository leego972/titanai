Southeast Indians have been known to put a great emphasis on traditional knowledge systems and practices, which is supported by their vast intra-ethnic diversity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B17">17</xref>]. India has over 537 different aboriginal and other ethnic groups constituting approximately eight percent of the country's population [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B18">18</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B19">19</xref>]. Traditional knowledge systems including various medicinal plant utilities appear to vary according to local population domain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B20">20</xref>]. Documentation of these local knowledge systems concerning medicinal plants may have high impacts from a bioeconomic point of view [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B6">6</xref>]. Tribal communities living in biodiversity rich areas possess a wealth of knowledge on the local utilization and conservation of food and medicinal plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B18">18</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B21">21</xref>]. This traditional knowledge, which developed over years of observation, trial and error, inference and inheritance, has largely remained with the aboriginal people [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B23">23</xref>]. However, these cultures and their associated botanical knowledge may be in peril and may even become extinct. Migration from one area to another in search of improved livelihoods is a key feature of human history. Many aboriginals in India migrate to access emerging opportunities and industrialization. This widens the gap between TAK and modern knowledge associated with workplace and social skills of the developed mainstream populations. It is a fact that as traditional healers who value TAK are becoming very old; younger generations exhibit a lack of interest in TAK with a trend toward migration to cities for lucrative jobs. TAK in India is declining [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">24</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B25">25</xref>].</p><p>The study of ethnobotanical research is deeply rooted within India. There are many examples of medicinal ethnobotanical surveys conducted in India in the past that have recorded many botanical remedies among many aboriginal groups: Malasars [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B26">26</xref>]; Malamalasars [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B27">27</xref>]; Malayalis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B28">28</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B31">31</xref>]; Irulas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B23">23</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B32">32</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B34">34</xref>]; Gonds [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B15">15</xref>]; Koysd, Konda reddis, Valmikis, Koyas, Chenchus, Lambadis, Jatapus, Savaras, Bagatas, Kammaras, Khondas, Nukadoras, Porjas, Jatapus [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B35">35</xref>]; Paliyar [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B36">36</xref>]; Kanikar [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B37">37</xref>]; Todas, Kotas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B38">38</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B39">39</xref>]; Kattunayakas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B40">40</xref>]; Apatani [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B41">41</xref>]; Chellipale [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B42">42</xref>]. Although there are many descriptive qualitative surveys of TAK, to our knowledge, there are no ethnobotanical studies within India that consider variation in TAK among informants using a quantitative consensus analysis.</p><p>Aboriginal knowledge about plants needs to be reliable and repeatable if it is used as a bridge in scientific inquiry with an application to medicine and society-at-large. Trotter and Logan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>] presented a quantitative method to evaluate consensus among informants in order to identifying potentially effective medicinal plants. In the last 20 years since Trotter and Logan's [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>] publication there has been limited research from several countries: Peru [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">44</xref>]; Indonesian Borneo &amp; Timor [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B45">45</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B46">46</xref>]; Northeastern Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B47">47</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B48">48</xref>]; Mexico [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B9">9</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B49">49</xref>]; Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guatemala [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B50">50</xref>]; Southern Belize [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B51">51</xref>]; Kenya [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B52">52</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B53">53</xref>]; Mali [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">54</xref>]; Ethiopia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B55">55</xref>]; Tanzania [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B56">56</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B57">57</xref>] and the Canadian Arctic [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B58">58</xref>].
