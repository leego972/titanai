# PMC: optical coherence tomography clinical application — Overview

id="f0001" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p><bold>Cumulative sum (CUSUM) curve for optical coherence tomography (OCT)-assisted diagnosis of basal cell carcinoma</bold> (BCC) (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 400), with <italic toggle="yes">p</italic><sub>0</sub> = 16% and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>1 = 25% for cut-off value level of confidence ≥ 2 and ≥ 3.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="ActaDV-100-19-5942-g001.jpg"/></fig><p>When using a cut-off value ≥ 3, only certainty of BCC presence on OCT is defined as a positive test result. There were 48 false-negative and 26 false-positive OCT diagnoses corresponding to an overall error rate of 18.5% (74/400). The curve initially courses around and above the x-axis, indicating a “trial and error” state until case 52 (<xref rid="f0001" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>). It first crosses the acceptable boundary (<italic toggle="yes">h</italic><sub>1</sub>) from above at case 202, but subsequently fluctuates around the critical <italic toggle="yes">h</italic>-line, giving it an overall horizontal course to definitely cross it from above at case 311. At this point, the hypothesis that the diagnostic error rate reached 16%, can be accepted.</p></sec><sec sec-type="discussion" id="sec3"><title>DISCUSSION</title><p>This study illustrates how the CUSUM method can be used to create learning curves and estimate after how many OCT scans diagnostic performance meets pre-specified standards.</p><p>Learning curves graphically show the relationship between learning effort and achievement. The benefit of CUSUM is that it continuously assesses individual performance and progress in mastering a new technique (<xref rid="cit0020" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>). It also serves as a rapid detector of change and allows for early intervention, such as retraining or continued observation, which is especially useful in its application in trainee programmes (<xref rid="cit0026" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>, <xref rid="cit0027" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>). It has become an accepted method for monitoring performance in medical therapeutic and diagnostic procedures (<xref rid="cit0020" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>, <xref rid="cit0024" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>, <xref rid="cit0028" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>, <xref rid="cit0029" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>). The diagnostic error rate can be used as a measure for overall diagnostic performance in learning curves (<xref rid="cit0020" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>). This rate does not distinguish between sensitivity and specificity, which are discussed in another paper (<xref rid="cit0017" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>).</p><p>The OCT trainees reported their diagnosis on a 5-point confidence scale, which enabled us to monitor performance for different thresholds for a positive test result for OCT. Since a score of 3 or more on the Likert scale reflected the assessor being certain of the diagnosis BCC, we considered this as the most appropriate threshold. However, in a scenario in which the aim is not to miss a BCC, one may opt for a confidence level ≥ 2 as the cut-off point for a positive test result. For the latter, the number of cases (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 183) that need to be evaluated before reaching acceptable performance was lower than the 311 required scans when the more strict threshold ≥ 3 was used. A possible explanation is that less experienced OCT users tend to exercise more caution in their judgement, represented by lower confidence scores, which is penalized when using a high confidence score as the cut-off value.</p><p>When the ultimate goal of OCT is to be able to omit punch biopsy, it becomes important to monitor the ability to make both accurate and confident diagnoses. However, such ability requires more and longer training.</p><p>The number of cases required to achieve acceptable performance depends strongly on the choice of the acceptable and unacceptable failure rates (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic><sub>0</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic><sub>1</sub>). These parameters set the target that one wants to achieve and may differ between centres. However, the setting of realistic targets for our centre, where OCT has not yet been implemented in clinical practice, was challenging. Diagnostic error rates of 12% have been reported by 2 (industry-initiated) studies on diagnostic performance of OCT (<xref rid="cit0013" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>, <xref rid="cit0014" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>). However, the prevalence of BCC was higher than in the current study, and thus the study populations may represent a different case mix. Moreover, the level of confidence in the OCT diagnosis used to define a positive test result with OCT was not explicitly reported in these studies (<xref rid="cit0013" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>, <xref rid="cit0014" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>). Therefore, efforts were made to obtain an estimate of the failure rate of a competent, experienced operator.
