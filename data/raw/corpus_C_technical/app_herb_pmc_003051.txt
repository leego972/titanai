Chewing is used for digestive support, musculoskeletal relief, or nutritional supplementation.</p><p id="Par91">Boiling and drinking decoctions is another prevalent method, applied to <italic toggle="yes">Argemone mexicana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Brassica oleracea</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ruta chalepensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic>. This technique extracts bioactive compounds from leaves, roots, or fruits, targeting internal conditions such as fever, gastrointestinal disorders, and systemic infections.</p><p id="Par92">Latex, sap, or gum extraction is employed for species such as <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Acacia senegal</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Boswellia rivae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia tirucalli</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Opuntia ficus-indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic>. The extracted material is applied directly or combined with other substances for topical treatments, including wound healing, skin infections, and inflammation.</p><p id="Par93">Inhalation of smoke or steam from crushed, boiled, or burned plant parts is practiced with species such as <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cleome kelleriana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cyphostemma adenocaule</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus globulus</italic>, primarily for respiratory relief and alleviation of nasal or chest congestion.</p><p id="Par94">Other preparation techniques include mixing with milk (<italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic>), tying crushed powder on wounds (<italic toggle="yes">Solanum jubae</italic>), concoctions for oral ingestion (<italic toggle="yes">Foeniculum vulgare</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic>), and gradual release through chewing or holding plant parts in the mouth (<italic toggle="yes">Acacia nubica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Melia azedarach</italic>). These diverse methods reflect a sophisticated understanding of plant properties and local health needs.</p></sec><sec id="Sec16"><title>Routes and methods of administration</title><p id="Par95">Medicinal plant remedies in the Karamara forest patches were applied through multiple routes, reflecting their diverse therapeutic uses. Oral administration was the most common, followed by dermal applications, with less frequent use via nasal, ocular, anal, and urogenital routes (Fig. <xref rid="Fig8" ref-type="fig">8</xref>). This pattern underlines the community’s practical approach, selecting administration routes based on ailment type, plant preparation, and desired therapeutic effect.</p><p id="Par96">Oral administration was primarily used for treating gastrointestinal disorders, parasitic infections, malaria, and systemic ailments. Species frequently administered orally included <italic toggle="yes">Aloe pirottae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Asepalum eriantherum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Boswellia rivae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora kua</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Crotalaria jijigensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Indigastrum costatum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Iphionopsis rotundifolia</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Pistacia aethiopica</italic>. Remedies were prepared as decoctions, infusions, crushed materials, or plant juices.</p><p id="Par97">Dermal application was the second most common route, targeting wounds, skin infections, swelling, and insect bites. Plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Abutilon fruticosum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cucumis pustulatus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Dodonaea angustifolia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia abyssinica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ficus bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Solanum somalense</italic> were applied topically as crushed leaves, latex, sap, powders, or poultices.</p><p id="Par98">Ocular administration was reported for species like <italic toggle="yes">Aloe pirottae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Leucas jamesii</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Solanum coagulans</italic>, mainly for eye infections and pain, using freshly extracted juices or squeezed leaves.</p><p id="Par99">Urogenital administration was limited to species such as <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, used to treat urogenital infections, while anal administration of <italic toggle="yes">Heliotropium linariifolium</italic> addressed constipation and rectal disorders.</p><p id="Par100">Several species were employed via multiple routes, depending on the target ailment or plant part used.
