The transference of traditional knowledge in the study area is from male parent to their sons since females have low status in the society and do not inherit property on the same basis to the male [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>]. The study conducted in Namibia indicated similar practice [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>]; the females learn by routine observation whereas the boys are taught by their parents besides regular observation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>]. The vertical transference of TK from father to son is a common phenomenon in Ethiopia, Africa, and Asia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>]. However, studies conducted on knowledge of medicinal plants in Ankober district, Samburu district, Kenya and in South America show no difference in the average number of plants reported either by female or male respondents [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>]. The division of labor in the society has determined the TK difference between female and male. The female informants in Yalo woreda reported only three plants with veterinary importance since the males are the sole livestock herders in the region and responsible for the animals’ wellbeing [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>]. The ethnoveterinary knowledge in pastoral society is acquired from their parents during grazing that indicates females’ knowledge of ethnoveterinary medicinal plants is less than men, which is similar to results reported by other studies on important ethnoveterinary plant [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>].</p><p id="Par35">The number of medicinal plants reported in the study area increased with age, and the older informants reported more medicinal plants than the younger individual [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR52">52</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>]. Abera [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>] has shown in the study conducted, in Ghimbi District, on Oromo people that the young generation is losing the interest in using medicinal plants because of changes induced by development and abandons of rural life. This phenomenon leads to the disappearance of associated traditional knowledge and interrupts transference of knowledge to next generations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>]. Similarly, the study conducted, in the semi-arid region of Brazil, has shown that age and income difference had implications on knowledge of informants and correlated to some plants that have an effect on the traditional knowledge of younger generation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>]. The transference of knowledge in the Afar family is similar to most of the pastoral areas where youngsters learn knowledge of medicinal plants from the elders during grazing. A child in Maasai pastoralists’ society in Eastern Africa has to learn and identify grasses and plants with medicinal importance in their area during grazing. The boys are taught in the field and at home, though girls are taught by their mothers and grandmothers only at home [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>].</p><p id="Par36">The religious establishments in Mesgid and Rekrek Kebeles and other cultural aspects had no association with traditional knowledge of medicinal plants in Yalo Wereda [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>]. Though, Abu-Rabia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>] reported that knowledge of plants of nomadic pastoralists in Middle East countries originated from the sayings of Prophet Muhammad’s on health and illness: ‘<italic toggle="yes">The Medicine of the Prophet</italic>”.
