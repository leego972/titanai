Saudi Arabians may use both traditional herbal and biomedical treatments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>], and a wide range of health care resources are available in Mecca: from traditional medicines and healers ([<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>], Al-Qethami, pers. obs), to modern biomedical care provided by the Saudi government for free to all Muslims.</p></sec><sec id="Sec8"><title>Conducting interviews with Meccan women</title><p id="Par16">The ethical guidelines of the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>], the Declaration of Helsinki [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>] and University of Reading ethical protocols were followed in this research. Approval from the Ethics Committee of the School of Biological Sciences, University of Reading, was obtained (Research Ethics Project Submission SBS15-16 11).</p><p id="Par17">Meccan women were interviewed by the first author from May to June 2016; individual free-listing and semi-structured interviews were conducted with 32 female adults. Targeted sampling was used for selecting informants who use medicinal plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>] from the first author’s social network. Snowball sampling was later used to identify other local women who use medicinal plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>]. All the informants were born in the Mecca region, with ages ranging between 28 and 69 years old (see Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref> for the informants’ anonymous social data). Although older informants may hold further knowledge, interviews were conducted until a saturation of information was reached. Prior informed consent was obtained verbally from each woman before they were interviewed. Interviews were conducted in Arabic and recorded when the agreement from the informants was obtained (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 9). Firstly, women were asked to list the medicinal plants they knew, documenting plant names. Then, semi-structured interviews were used to elucidate the parts used, therapeutic uses, preparation and administration processes (including use in mixtures) and perceptions of potential toxicity and side effects of plants used. Moreover, women were asked about how they had acquired this knowledge, if medicinal plants or biomedicine were preferred and when medicinal plants where preferred over biomedicine.
