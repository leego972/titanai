# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

based on methods suggested by Schultes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B81">81</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B4">4</xref>], Jain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B18">18</xref>] and Bernard [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B76">76</xref>].</p><p>Successive free listing was used to interview 80 knowledgeable informants providing data for the consensus analysis. Knowledgeable informants were selected following standard interview protocols [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B76">76</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B82">82</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B83">83</xref>], which included verification whether these informants were traditional healers within their communities (Figure <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F4">4</xref>). We interviewed over 120 informants of which we chose 80 knowledgeable informants equally distributed among four different age categories; elders, middle aged, teenagers, &lt;10 years. During this interview we documented all possible information about a specific plant and then sorted this information according to utilitarian perspective, local name or ecology. The informants were given limited time and there was no differentiation among gender. We also asked the informants to group the plant specimens into different illness categories. We requested all informants to collect specimens of the plants they knew or to show the plant species on site. Interviews were conducted in the regional language, Tamil. The questionnaires were used to obtain information on medicinal plants with their local names, parts used, mode of preparation and administration.</p><fig position="float" id="F4" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 4</label><caption><p>Aboriginal elder sun drying and selling medicinal bulbs.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" xlink:href="1746-4269-4-8-4.jpg" orientation="portrait"/></fig><p>Calculation of a consensus factor (F<sub>IC</sub>) for testing homogeneity on the informant's knowledge was followed by the method provided by Trotter and Logan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>]. A consensus factor of F<sub>IC </sub>is given by:</p><p><disp-formula>F<sub>IC </sub>= N<sub>ur</sub>-N<sub>t</sub>/(N<sub>ur</sub>-1)</disp-formula></p><p>The factor provides a range of 0 to 1, where a high value acts as a good indicator for a high rate of informant consensus. N<sub>ur </sub>is the number of use-reports of informants for particular illness usage, where a use-report is a single record for use of a plant mentioned by an individual, and N<sub>t </sub>refers to the number of specie used for a particular illness category for all informants. The majority of illness types are grouped into predefined ethno/economic botany categories [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B84">84</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>], with the additions of a few other illness categories (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T1">1</xref>), which were commonly mentioned during our interviews because they were prevalent in these communities. The use of "general categories" is adopted here as recommended by other ethnobotanical researchers [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B84">84</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>]. These 51 illnesses were sorted into 10 usage categories (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T1">1</xref>). All of the illness types were translated as best as possible from the Malasars description of the illness/symptoms to known biomedical/english terms, with few exceptions (e.g., spiritualism – repel
