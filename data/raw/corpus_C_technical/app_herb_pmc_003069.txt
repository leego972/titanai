Species such as <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. kelleriana</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">E. globulus</italic> are commonly used in these thermal preparations. Similar practices have been recorded in Ethiopia, India, and Pakistan, reflecting the global use of heat and smoke-based remedies to release volatile compounds and deliver inhalable therapies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par204">Hence, the dosage determination, diagnostic processes, and use of additives in Karamara demonstrate a sophisticated ethnomedical system that integrates patient-centered assessment, knowledge of bioactive plant chemistry, and adaptive modes of preparation. These practices mirror broader ethnobotanical patterns observed globally, revealing both cultural specificity and a shared human logic in the use of MPs for healing.</p></sec><sec id="Sec36"><title>Marketability of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par205">The present study reveals that MPs from the Karamara forest patches possess considerable market potential, shaped by their proven therapeutic value, cultural acceptance, and high local demand. Frequently cited species such as <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">O. lamiifolium</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Z. spina-christi</italic> are among the most sought-after, reflecting both their medicinal efficacy and commercial significance. Similar patterns have been documented in other parts of Ethiopia, where highly utilized species are also the most marketable due to their effectiveness against common ailments and their wide recognition in traditional healthcare systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>].</p><p id="Par206">Across Africa, market trends show comparable dynamics. In Kenya and Nigeria, marketable MPs include both high-biomass trees and rapidly regenerating herbs, suggesting that trade value is influenced not only by therapeutic efficacy but also by ecological abundance and ease of regeneration [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>]. In Asia particularly India, Pakistan, and China market demand similarly correlate with citation frequency and the plants’ effectiveness in treating widespread diseases such as malaria, gastrointestinal infections, and respiratory disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR67">67</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par207">The specific plant parts used and methods of preparation play a central role in determining marketability. Leaves, roots, latex, and fruits are the most frequently traded components, often processed into powders, decoctions, or infusions for ease of transport, storage, and use. Comparable practices are reported in Sudan and other African regions, where powdered leaves, dried roots, and latex constitute the principal forms of trade in traditional herbal markets [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>]. Likewise, in Europe and the Americas, the herbal trade is dominated by processed plant products typically derived from leaves, bark, and seeds underscoring a global preference for materials that are stable, portable, and easily standardized [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR91">91</xref>].</p><p id="Par208">In the Karamara region, marketable species are predominantly trees and shrubs, such as <italic toggle="yes">(A) nilotica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">M. stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">(B) rivae</italic>, valued for their large biomass and year-round availability. At the same time, fast-regenerating herbs like <italic toggle="yes">(A) mexicana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">(B) pilosa</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">L. savoryi</italic> present sustainable options for trade, given their rapid growth and resilience. Conversely, slow-growing woody species, including <italic toggle="yes">A. senegal</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">(C) myrrha</italic>, require careful management and harvesting regulation to prevent overexploitation and ensure long-term ecological stability.
