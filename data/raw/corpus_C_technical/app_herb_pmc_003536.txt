In this study, 52 plant species were recorded, used to manage a broad spectrum of ailments, including gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory infections, malaria, diabetes, skin diseases, and eye infections. This diversity reflects a sophisticated ethnomedicinal knowledge system, in which communities rely heavily on MPs as primary healthcare providers, particularly where access to modern medical facilities is limited. High informant consensus for gastrointestinal and respiratory ailments, particularly for species such as <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>, underscores their perceived efficacy. Comparable patterns have been observed in Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where certain plants are repeatedly cited for specific ailments due to their recognized therapeutic reliability [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR184">184</xref>].</p><p id="Par209">Beyond medicinal applications, many MPs contribute directly to nutritional security and household resilience. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Ziziphus hamur</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Tamarindus indica</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Moringa stenopetala</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Capsicum annuum</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Cordeauxia edulis</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Carica papaya</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Citrullus colocynthis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic> provide essential nutrients while supporting overall health and productivity. This dual functionality strengthens food and economic security, enabling communities to withstand environmental stressors and disease outbreaks. Similar multifunctional roles of ethnomedicinal plants have been reported in Ethiopia, Nigeria, India, and Pakistan, highlighting the synergistic connection between medicinal plant use, nutrition, and animal husbandry in semi-arid and rural settings [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR184">184</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR201">201</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR204">204</xref>].</p><p id="Par210">The study also revealed that many MPs, including <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia</italic> species, <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic>, are harvested directly from natural habitats, emphasizing the need to conserve local ecosystems. Overharvesting, habitat degradation, and climate change pose significant threats to both plant populations and the associated indigenous knowledge. Encouragingly, local communities employ traditional sustainable harvesting practices, such as collecting leaves, fruits, or bark rather than uprooting whole plants. These practices reflect a nuanced understanding of ecological balance, consistent with findings from East Africa, China, and Australia, where indigenous methods have successfully maintained plant populations while enabling continued use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR177">177</xref>].</p><p id="Par211">Medicinal plant use is deeply entwined with cultural identity and social cohesion. Elders, herbalists, and pastoralists act as custodians of this knowledge, transmitting it orally across generations. Many species hold symbolic or ritual significance, serving preventive, magico-religious, or socially important functions, highlighting the cultural embedding of ethnomedicinal knowledge. Similar patterns have been reported in Uganda, Cameroon, and India, where traditional practices integrate health, spirituality, and social values [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR130">130</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR166">166</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR167">167</xref>]. Moreover, marketable species such as <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic> provide economic incentives that support sustainable management and reinforce cultural relevance.</p><p id="Par212">Hence, MPs in Sheikh District provide multidimensional benefits: they are essential for human health, enhance food security, promote environmental sustainability, and preserve cultural heritage.
