Fright illnesses often include physical symptoms, psychological/behavioral symptoms, or a period of misfortune in the sufferer's life [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B7">7</xref>]. Those fright illnesses tend to share a local diagnosis involving soul loss: Distress potentially dislodges a sufferer's soul (or vital force), or scares the soul out of the body. Soul loss is <italic toggle="yes">not</italic>, however, part of the Anglo-Franco Caribbean fright explanation.</p><p>These Caribbeans blame fright not on soul loss but on physical changes in blood and nerves that occur in response to a trauma. Dominicans, like other Caribbean people, subscribe to a version of the New World hot/cold humoral system that has been documented throughout the New World, particularly in Latin America (for an overview, see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B16">16</xref>]). Foster claims, in fact, that "humoral medicine in the Americas is the most completely described of all ethnomedical systems" [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B16">16</xref>]:2]. In the hot/cold humoral system, people group mental and physical states, plants, and animals into "hot" and "cold" categories. Here, "cold" or "hot" may refer to the temperature of air or bathing water, however "hot" and "cold" often refer to culturally ascribed symbolic values having nothing to do with thermal state. Health requires balancing hot and cold influences to an individual's body system [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B17">17</xref>]. Strong emotions charge the blood with humoral heat or cold; and frightful emotions are blood-chilling.</p><p>Cold blood leads to tense nerves. Sobo notes that Jamaicans regard nerves as anatomical (rather than mental) equipment, which is susceptible to malfunction [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B18">18</xref>]. Dominicans hold the same view, and they attribute malfunctions to "wear" on the nerves. Nerves wear through overuse but if one's blood is cold and soured by the fright, it exacerbates the rate and degree of nerve damage. Dominicans thus maintain that shock, fear, panic, or anguish and the resulting blood changes can leave a person in a state of constant stress, anxiety, or nervousness.</p><p>Villagers in Bwa Mawego use several herbal infusions to treat anxiety, or in their view, to hinder the cold humoral effects of frightful emotions. As they reckon fright to be extremely cold, they treat fright with herbs that they consider to be hot, or "heating." Ingesting the medicine thaws or warms the body toward the normal warm (neither too hot not too cold) state, at which nerves function best.</p><p>Dominicans recognize two types of fright. Most fright cases are the "short," "regular," "normal," fright, which is relatively acute, lasting around fourteen months or less. "Chronic fright," in contrast does not heal and ranges from reoccurring fright episodes to a continuous "frightened" state, which can be terminal. Though fright illnesses occur throughout the world, Franco/Anglo Caribbean frights particularly resembles syndromes in neighboring Hispanic populations. The term "fright" literally translates in Spanish to <italic toggle="yes">susto. Susto </italic>is a Latin American fright illness that also begins with a shock and includes symptoms of trembling, agitation, crying, difficulty sleeping, and general malaise [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B19">19</xref>]. These qualities overlap with Caribbean fright or <italic toggle="yes">sésisma</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">susto </italic>shares some traits with other Latin American folk illnesses, <italic toggle="yes">nervios </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">ataques de nervios </italic>(see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B20">20</xref>]). Caribbean fright illnesses also resemble Hispanic experiences of <italic toggle="yes">ataques de nervios </italic>("nerve attacks")(see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B21">21</xref>]), which are panic attacks triggered by acute stress characterized by uncontrollable outbursts of shouting and crying, trembling, palpitations, and aggressiveness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>]. Finally, the Latin American concept of <italic toggle="yes">nervios </italic>("nerves"), which stems from social overburden and conflict and includes sadness, anger, sleep troubles, hopelessness (see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B25">25</xref>]), seems not unlike Caribbean descriptions of the circumstances and symptoms of "chronic fright."
