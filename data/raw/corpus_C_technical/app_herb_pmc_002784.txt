Unfortunately, the earlier data (notably including the species lists compiled by Langdale-Brown et al<italic toggle="yes">.</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>]) do not indicate relative abundance or frequency of occurrence, and the inventory and size-class distribution data collected on fixed and variable plots within the study area concurrently with data collection, some results of which were presented in an earlier paper [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>], include only woody and not herbaceous species. Nonetheless, the degree to which Fabaceae have been selected for medicinal use stands in contrast to their natural occurrence—comprising over 24% of all medicinal use reports, as compared to the woody species inventory data, which places the family at just 4.2% of woody species by basal area in Otuke, and 18.6% by species composition in Amuria.</p></sec><sec id="Sec12"><title>Folk taxonomy and medicinal use categories</title><p id="Par68">Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref> is organized by family, and some commonality of use is visually evident within particular genera and families—examples including the remedies for cough and diarrhea within the Combretaceae, wound care among the Compositae, treatment for diarrhea in Moraceae, hernia among the Rubiaceae, and measles and cough among the Rutaceae.</p><p id="Par69">Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref> provides the common vernacular phytonyms for each taxon cited according to each of the cultures citing it for a medicinal use. Classification systems within the vernacular nomenclature reflect emic dimensions of cultural meaning [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>] by which plants are classified according to folk systematics [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>]. In general, the vernacular phytonyms presented here differ between cultures in the interpretive accessibility of information held within them, partly a function of the linguistic structures of the vernacular languages in which they are recorded.</p><p id="Par70">Among the Lwo phytonyms, such linguistic qualifiers include adjectives referring to morphological characters, with the adjectives white, black and red (<bold><italic toggle="yes">atar</italic></bold>, <bold><italic toggle="yes">achol</italic></bold> and <bold><italic toggle="yes">rema</italic></bold>) commonly used to distinguish plant species within a genus or family. Taxonomically, the generic name for a group of species formerly classified together under the genus <italic toggle="yes">Acacia</italic> is <bold><italic toggle="yes">okutu</italic></bold>, of which <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia sieberiana</italic> is known as <bold><italic toggle="yes">okutu atar</italic></bold>, or white acacia; the genus <italic toggle="yes">Terminalia</italic> is called <bold><italic toggle="yes">opok</italic></bold> (of which <italic toggle="yes">T. mollis</italic> is <bold><italic toggle="yes">opok acol</italic></bold>, black terminalia), and in the genus <italic toggle="yes">Albizia</italic> (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ibata</italic></bold>), <italic toggle="yes">A. schimperiana</italic> (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ibata atar</italic></bold>) is distinguished from another, non-medicinal species known as <bold><italic toggle="yes">ibata achol</italic></bold>.</p><p id="Par71">Other Lwo adjectives and descriptive nouns are likewise used as qualifiers in the vernacular nomenclature to distinguish related species within a genus, such as <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus caudatus</italic> L. (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ocobo</italic></bold>), from which <italic toggle="yes">A. graecizans</italic> L. is distinguished as local by the qualifier ‘Lango’ (as <bold><italic toggle="yes">ocobo Lango</italic></bold>), in the same manner that ash filtrate is called ‘local salt’ (<bold><italic toggle="yes">kado Lango</italic></bold>). Similarly, the cultivated <italic toggle="yes">Cleome gynandra</italic> L. (<bold><italic toggle="yes">akeo</italic></bold>) is distinguished from <italic toggle="yes">C. monophylla</italic> L. as <bold><italic toggle="yes">akeo Jok</italic></bold>, in which the qualifier may signify its wild occurrence, or other properties.</p><p id="Par72">While some Lwo plant names have a clear meaning for which a basis is not evident (for example, <bold><italic toggle="yes">kongo ogwal-ogwal</italic></bold> or ‘frog’s beer’ for <italic toggle="yes">Physalis minima</italic>), the basis for others is more directly related to the pharmacological activities attributed to the plants.
