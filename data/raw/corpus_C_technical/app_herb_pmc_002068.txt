Major phenolic compounds found in almond skin are flavanols and flavonol glycosides, followed by non-flavonoids [<xref rid="B30-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. Within flavanols, the most abundant compounds identified are catechin and epicatechin, while isorhamnetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rutinoside and kaempferol-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rutinoside are the major flavonol glycosides present in almond skin [<xref rid="B30-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>,<xref rid="B66-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">66</xref>]. Milbury et al. (2006) reported that isorhamnetin (in the form 3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-glucoside or 3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rutinoside), representing around 70.0% of the total, is the predominant flavonoid in most of the varieties assessed so far, with 97.1 ± 2.3% of compounds originating from skin and 2.9 ± 2.3% from kernel [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. However, during the blanching process around 69.0% of isorhamnetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-glucoside and isorhamnetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rutinoside are extracted to the blanch water [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Garrido et al. (2008) stated that catechin, which represents 10.0–23.0% of the phenolics identified, and isorhamnetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rutinoside (6.8–17%) are the most abundant phenolics in almond skin in their study [<xref rid="B53-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>]. The presence and the quantity of each phenolic compound in almond skin might depend on factors such as the industrial process to which it was subjected, the storage conditions, and the extraction method, among others. Predominant phenolic acids (non-flavonoids) in many foods of plant origin are derivatives of hydroxybenzoic and hydroxycinnamic acids. Wijeratne et al. (2006) identified derivatives of cinnamic acids in almonds (with, caffeic, ferulic, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric, and sinaptic acids as major ones in almond skin) (<xref rid="molecules-22-01774-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>) [<xref rid="B24-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>], whilst others identified hydroxybenzoic acids (protocatechuic, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic, and vanillic acid) [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>,<xref rid="B28-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>,<xref rid="B57-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>] and hydroxycinnamic acids (chlorogenic acid) [<xref rid="B30-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>,<xref rid="B53-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>] as most abundant in almond skin.</p></sec><sec id="sec3dot3-molecules-22-01774"><title>3.3. Phenolic Composition of Almond Blanch Water</title><p>Blanching is a process consisting in the removal of almond skin by scalding almonds in hot water for a short period of time, in effect removing most flavonoids and other phenols from almond skin. Milbury et al. (2006) noticed that, in the blanching process, kaempferol and quercetin do not leach to the water [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>], while Mandalari et al. (2010) confirmed that quercetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-galactoside and isorhamnetin are two additional compounds that are not extracted to water during blanching [<xref rid="B32-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>]. This fact has been attributed to their poor water solubility. In addition, Hughey et al. (2012) did not detect quercetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rutinoside in blanch water [<xref rid="B67-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>], as well as nonpolar aglycones, such as isorhamnetin, quercetin, and kaempferol. To test the hypothesis of the constraint represented by the “limited water solubility” to the leaching of phenolic compounds into the blanching water, blanch water precipitate was analyzed. This approach demonstrated that nonpolar aglycones (isorhamnetin and kaempferol), partially insoluble glycosides (quercetin glycoside, kaempferol glycoside, rutinoside, isorhamnetin glycoside, rutinoside, and naringenin glucoside), and even more water-soluble catechins were present in blanch water precipitate, indicating that these compounds leach from the skin into blanch water, dissolve until their solubility limit, and then precipitate [<xref rid="B67-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>].</p><p>Furthermore, Mandalari et al. (2013) studied the antioxidant effect of blanch water [<xref rid="B43-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>], determining the polyphenolic content of blanch water extracts, the level of proanthocyanidins, and the vanillin index. The total phenolic content, the antioxidant activity, and the radical scavenging activity were evaluated with different in vitro tests (Folin–Ciocalteu method, DPPH radical scavenging activity, β-carotene bleaching test, reducing power test, UV-induced peroxidation in liposomal membranes).
