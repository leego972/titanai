# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">44</xref>]; Indonesian Borneo &amp; Timor [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B45">45</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B46">46</xref>]; Northeastern Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B47">47</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B48">48</xref>]; Mexico [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B9">9</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B49">49</xref>]; Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guatemala [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B50">50</xref>]; Southern Belize [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B51">51</xref>]; Kenya [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B52">52</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B53">53</xref>]; Mali [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">54</xref>]; Ethiopia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B55">55</xref>]; Tanzania [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B56">56</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B57">57</xref>] and the Canadian Arctic [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B58">58</xref>]. This body of literature suggest that there is considerable variation in consensus factors and how this technique has been implemented. Moerman [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B59">59</xref>], Phillips and Gentry [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">44</xref>] and Heinrich [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>] readapted Trotter and Logan's [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>] factor of informant consensus factor (F<sub>IC</sub>) in order to quantitatively evaluate the degree of selection of certain plants for a particular utility (e.g., ailment). One of the traditional intentions of F<sub>IC </sub>is to test the homogeneity among informants' knowledge [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>]. In fact some researchers use consensus analysis to test falsifiable hypotheses concerning informant selection and use of plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B53">53</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">44</xref>]. Many other researchers have employed consensus analysis as a decision making factor [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B48">48</xref>] to examine the variation in TAK of cultivars by traditional aboriginal farmers [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B49">49</xref>], weighing the relative importance of TAK [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B60">60</xref>], identifying discrepancies in ratings [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B50">50</xref>], estimating the competence of informants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B61">61</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B62">62</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B50">50</xref>] and ethnopharmacolgical surveys [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">54</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B48">48</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B47">47</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B55">55</xref>].</p><p>The theoretical importance of our study is to test consensus (reliability/repeatability) of TAK within one ancient culture; the Malasars of the Velliangiri hills in the Western Ghats of Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, India. We chose to work with the Malasars of India, because 1) there are known to be exceptional healers and keepers of TAK of the flora in the Velliangiri holy hills [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B63">63</xref>] and 2) there is limited research on the Malasars TAK [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B64">64</xref>]. We hypothesize that consensus of TAK of specific plants used for different illness categories are high indicating reliable and repeatable TAK among informants at a local scale (within one localized aboriginal group – Malasars of the Velliangiri hills), because it has been used within their culture without interruption for many generations. Scientific inquiry demands repeatability in order to substantiate claims of medicinal utility within any aboriginal culture. Alternatively, consensus of TAK may be low at local scales [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B51">51</xref>] because of i) unreliable TAK, ii) informant bias, iii) local remedies; certain villages may have unique uses for plants, iv) variability in local ethnotaxa; certain communities may have found variants or ecotypes for some plants that result in unique qualities that are of particular use at only a local scale, iv) use of pharmaceutical supplements; the availability of modern pharmaceuticals for a particular ailment may result sporadic use of traditional remedies and v) availability of multiple remedies; there may be groups of plants and therefore several remedies available that are preferentially selected by individual healers for various utility (e.g., healing some ailment), thus indicating the potential biological activity for a group of plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B59">59</xref>]. These groups may represent Linnaean taxa (i.e., genus or family) that share similar biological processes, or aboriginal classifications may group plants (e.g., 'chedi' or 'kodi' etc.) that serve a similar utility [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B65">65</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B25">25</xref>].</p></sec><sec><title>The Malasars and their land</title><sec><title>Ethnography</title><p>Murugesan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B63">63</xref>] and Murugesan et al [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B66">66</xref>] previously described ecosystems and aboriginal communities for our area of study.
