</p></table-wrap-foot></table-wrap></p><p id="Par34">When the activities of different plant part extracts were compared, the stem and bark showed the best activities. For example, the water and ethanol extracts of <italic toggle="yes">E. latifolius</italic> stem and bark showed best antioxidant activity with EC<sub>50</sub> values in the ranges of 0.024–11.21 μg/mL compared to its leaf extract that showed EC<sub>50</sub> values in the ranges of 51.99–232.80 μg/mL. This is consistent with the moderate to high TPC content of <italic toggle="yes">E. latifolius</italic> extract (46.66 ± 11.96 GAE/g extract, Fig. <xref rid="Fig3" ref-type="fig">3</xref>). <italic toggle="yes">B. oblongifolia</italic> leaf (128.36 ± 14.09 GAE/g extract) and <italic toggle="yes">C. amoenus</italic> root and stem (119.67 ± 3.03 and 94.23 ± 10.16 GAE/g) showed the highest TPC and these same extracts showed moderate antioxidant activities (Table <xref rid="Tab3" ref-type="table">3</xref>).</p><p id="Par35">When the leaf extracts of different plant species were compared, the ethanol and water extracts of <italic toggle="yes">C. reticulatus</italic> showed the best DPPH-free radical scavenging activity with EC<sub>50</sub> values of 16.31 and 20.45 μg/mL, respectively. It also showed good antioxidant activity. Having observed similar antioxidant activities in water and ethanol extracts, we chose water extract of five plants for testing the extracts for their anti-inflammatory activities using PBMC assay. This is also partly because the Mbabaram Aboriginal community use water extracts of these medicinal plants to treat wounds, sores and inflammation.</p></sec><sec id="Sec16"><title>Effect of crude extracts on cell viability</title><p id="Par36">The cells were first treated with the crude extracts to determine the cell viability/percentage of live/dead cells resulting from treatment. The crude extract was considered toxic if the percentage of dead cells is ≥ 50% [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>]. None of the tested crude extracts affected the cell viability, as the percentage of live cells was more than 99% in all treatment groups (data not shown). Thus, the culture supernatant was further analysed to determine the quantity of pro-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines released by the LPS-stimulated PBMCs after treatment with the aqueous crude extracts of five medicinal plants.</p></sec><sec id="Sec17"><title>Anti-inflammatory activity of aqueous crude extracts</title><p id="Par37">The anti-inflammatory activities of the aqueous crude extracts were measured after treating LPS-stimulated PBMCs with each crude extract at a concentration of 100 μg/mL post overnight incubation. Of the crude extracts tested, <italic toggle="yes">B. oblongifolia</italic> leaf (BL in Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5</xref>A-H) showed the best anti-inflammatory activity by significantly reducing the levels of four pro-inflammatory cytokines, namely IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF in both PBMC donors (Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5</xref>A-H). Interleukin (IL)−1β is considered a key pro-inflammatory cytokine implicated in the inflammatory processes observed in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), with heightened IL-1β levels often correlating with increased disease severity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>].<fig id="Fig5" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 5</label><caption><p>The effects of aqueous crude extracts (100 μg/mL) from five medicinal plants (ES-<italic toggle="yes">Exocarpos latifolius</italic> stem; CS-<italic toggle="yes">Coleus amoenus</italic> stem; CL-<italic toggle="yes">Cajanus reticulatus</italic> leaf; DL-<italic toggle="yes">Dodonaea lanceolata</italic> var. <italic toggle="yes">subsessilifolia</italic> leaf; BL-<italic toggle="yes">Breynia oblongifolia</italic> leaf) on the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 10 ng/mL) stimulated-PBMCs incubated overnight.
