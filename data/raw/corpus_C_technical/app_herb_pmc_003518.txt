# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR176">176</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR179">179</xref>].</p><p id="Par130">Among the various methods of application, drinking was the most predominant (42.1%), followed by application to the skin (14.3%) and washing with plant extracts (13.3%). Dosage was largely informal and guided by traditional knowledge. Local healers determined quantities based on patient age, type and location of illness, severity, disease stage, physical condition, and extent of infection. Traditional units included finger length for roots and stems, spoons, tea cups, or glasses for powders and liquids, and counting leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, bulbs, and latex. Such practices reflect careful observation and adaptation to ensure safe and effective treatment. Similar informal dosing has been reported in Ethiopian districts, where healers rely on “handfuls” or cup measurements, demonstrating that traditional systems adjust doses empirically rather than through formal standardization [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR146">146</xref>].</p><p id="Par131">Some plant species exhibited remarkable versatility, being used for multiple ailments with different routes of administration. For instance, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe microdonta</italic> latex was ingested orally for typhoid and jaundice, whereas <italic toggle="yes">Aloe pirottae</italic> leaves were applied topically for stomachache. Similarly, <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic> roots were consumed orally for asthma, bark applied for snakebite, and fruits ingested for diarrhea. This versatility aligns with patterns observed in Kenya, Uganda, and India, where multipurpose plant species are integral to traditional medicine systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>].</p><p id="Par132">Overall, the findings from Sheikh District are consistent with ethnobotanical patterns reported across East Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR97">97</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">137</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR145">145</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>]. Oral remedies dominate systemic treatments, while dermal and ocular applications address localized conditions, reflecting convergent strategies across diverse cultural contexts. These similarities highlight the universality and robustness of indigenous knowledge systems, which balance empirical observation with culturally informed practices to ensure effective treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR175">175</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR178">178</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR180">180</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR181">181</xref>].</p><p id="Par133">Hence, routes of administration, methods of application, and dosage are carefully adapted to the type of ailment and plant part used, reflecting a sophisticated and context-specific ethnomedicinal knowledge system. The predominance of oral remedies emphasizes the importance of liquid formulations, while dermal and ocular routes illustrate the specificity and diversity of traditional practices. By situating Sheikh District within a comparative global framework, these findings contribute to a broader understanding of medicinal plant use and provide a foundation for pharmacological validation, conservation, and culturally sensitive healthcare interventions.</p></sec><sec id="Sec19"><title>Marketability of medicinal plants in Sheikh district</title><p id="Par134">The present study revealed that only a limited proportion of MPs documented in Sheikh District are actively traded in local markets, with 27.7% (15 of 54 species) reported as marketable (Table <xref rid="Tab3" ref-type="table">3</xref>). This pattern underscores the dual role of MPs as both therapeutic resources and economic commodities, while the majority remain primarily used within households or community networks rather than through formal market channels. Comparable observations have been reported in Ethiopia, where only 20–30% of ethnomedicinal species are sold commercially, often for culinary or non-medicinal purposes rather than explicit therapeutic use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR128">128</xref>].</p><p id="Par135">Among the most frequently marketed species in Sheikh District were <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Azadirachta indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Citrullus colocynthis</italic>. These plants are valued not only for their medicinal effectiveness but also because their parts bulbs, leaves, seeds, bark, and fruits are relatively easy to harvest, process, store, and transport, enhancing their commercial viability.
