Ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological research have recorded traditional use of <italic toggle="yes">P. suaveolens</italic> for managing menstrual discomfort, numbness, pregnancy complications, and postnatal care to reduce fatigue and prevent postpartum hemorrhages.<xref rid="cit0037" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="cit0038" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>,<xref rid="cit0040" ref-type="bibr">40</xref> These results demonstrate a significant role for the reproductive and maternal health systems.</p><p>Various parts of <italic toggle="yes">P. suaveolens</italic> are widely used in traditional therapies, as stated in the ethnobotanical surveys (<xref rid="t0002" ref-type="table">Tables 2</xref> and <xref rid="t0003" ref-type="table">3</xref>). The leaves are extensively used for their analgesic effect<xref rid="cit0003" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="cit0012" ref-type="bibr">12</xref> and are used topically for dermatological conditions such as eczema and psoriasis. In addition to medicinal applications, the leaves are also incorporated into local diets and brewed into herbal teas, which are believed to promote general health and well-being. Root extracts and decoctions are traditionally prepared to treat digestive diseases, fever, and infections, providing a broad therapeutic spectrum and deep-rooted integration into indigenous knowledge systems.<xref rid="cit0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="cit0011" ref-type="bibr">11</xref> Collectively, the cultural practices and formulations demonstrated the multifaceted role of <italic toggle="yes">P. suaveolens</italic> as a medicinal and cultural resource. The profound association between traditional healing, maternal care, and craftsmanship affirms the relevance of ethnobotanical heritage to contemporary scientific exploration.
