# PMC: phytochemical bioactive compound anticancer — Overview

It is a substrate for peroxidases, which convert scopoletin to non-fluorescent compounds, and has thus been widely used for many decades, in combination with horseradish peroxidase, as the basis for high-sensitivity hydrogen peroxide detection assays (<xref rid="B495" ref-type="bibr">495</xref>–<xref rid="B498" ref-type="bibr">498</xref>). Several studies have demonstrated scopoletin’s antioxidant capabilities <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic>, using common cell-free methods such as the DPPH, Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC), ferric reducing ability of plasma (FRAP), or beta-carotene/bleaching assays, while superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, nitric oxide, and peroxynitrite are among the reactive species shown to be effectively scavenged by scopoletin (<xref rid="B114" ref-type="bibr">114</xref>, <xref rid="B180" ref-type="bibr">180</xref>, <xref rid="B216" ref-type="bibr">216</xref>–<xref rid="B223" ref-type="bibr">223</xref>, <xref rid="B247" ref-type="bibr">247</xref>). Hepatoprotective antioxidant effects of scopoletin have also been observed in cultured HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes (<xref rid="B154" ref-type="bibr">154</xref>, <xref rid="B200" ref-type="bibr">200</xref>), however one study failed to detect significant antioxidant activity for scopoletin (<xref rid="B499" ref-type="bibr">499</xref>).</p><p>Given the important role of oxidative stress in the etiology of numerous disorders, it is not surprising that some of scopoletin’s favorable effects in disease states are attributed to its antioxidant properties. For example, scopoletin has been reported to have antioxidant effects in hyperthyroid-induced hyperglycemia in rats (<xref rid="B224" ref-type="bibr">224</xref>), as well as in oxidant-induced hemolysis of rat erythrocytes (<xref rid="B225" ref-type="bibr">225</xref>). The brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress, which is known to be central to many neurodegenerative conditions (<xref rid="B500" ref-type="bibr">500</xref>). A study performed in mouse brain homogenates revealed that scopoletin strongly inhibited the oxidative protein modifications induced by copper (<xref rid="B226" ref-type="bibr">226</xref>), which can contribute to the pathologies associated with atherosclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and Wilson’s disease (<xref rid="B501" ref-type="bibr">501</xref>–<xref rid="B503" ref-type="bibr">503</xref>). A recent study examined several aspects of oxidative stress involved in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and showed that scopoletin attenuated depletion of cellular reduced glutathione or ATP, inhibited ROS generation, and prevented cell death in oxidative conditions <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> (<xref rid="B227" ref-type="bibr">227</xref>). These findings were extended to a <italic toggle="yes">Drosophila</italic> mutant model of PD, where scopoletin treatment reduced accumulation of mitochondrial ROS and promoted recovery from degenerative phenotypes (<xref rid="B227" ref-type="bibr">227</xref>). Several other studies have shown that scopoletin can prevent oxidative injury in models relevant to diseases such as PD and AD. These include prevention of oxidative injury and induction of antioxidant gene expression in HT-22 and SHSY-5Y cells (<xref rid="B248" ref-type="bibr">248</xref>, <xref rid="B249" ref-type="bibr">249</xref>), as well as inhibition of monoamine oxidase activity (<xref rid="B106" ref-type="bibr">106</xref>, <xref rid="B250" ref-type="bibr">250</xref>).</p><p>One important contributor to oxidative stress in many cell types is the xanthine oxidase (XO)/xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) system (<xref rid="B201" ref-type="bibr">201</xref>, <xref rid="B504" ref-type="bibr">504</xref>, <xref rid="B505" ref-type="bibr">505</xref>).
