PFO closure was not recommended by cardiologists.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>Discussion</title><p id="Par31">We present three cases with three distinctly different types of retinal vascular occlusion in pregnancy. Even though case 1 and 2 both depict CLRAO, there is a difference in the pathogenesis. Etiologically, CLRAO is of three distinct types: CLRAO associated with CRVO or hemi-CRVO (like in case 1), non-arteritic CLRAO alone (like in case <xref rid="Sec3" ref-type="sec">2</xref>), and arteritic CLRAO associated with giant cell arteritis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>].</p><p id="Par32">The pathogenesis of CLRAO in CRVO is due to transient hemodynamic blockage of the cilioretinal artery caused by a sudden sharp rise in intraluminal pressure in the retinal capillary bed (due to CRVO) above the level of that in the cilioretinal artery. Unlike regular non-arteritic CLRAO, there is no thrombotic or embolic occlusion of the artery in this type [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>]. Consistent with this, OCTA in our case showed reperfusion of the cilioretinal artery. (F<xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">i</xref>g. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1I</xref>). The resulting retinal atrophy (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>L, P) is likely to be caused by transient ischemia lasting longer than 1–2 hours [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>].</p><p id="Par33">Non-arteritic CLRAO, as seen in patient 2, is caused by classical thromboembolic occlusion. In our case, the OCTA showed reperfusion 10 days after the onset of the scotoma (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>E), indicating transient occlusion of the cilioretinal artery. Over time, there was progressive atrophy of the retina, in particular of OPL and INL (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>H, M). Consistent with this, OCTA depicted no perfusion in the DCP (located in the INL and OPL) in the area of atrophy, while the ICP and SVP showed regular perfusion (F<xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">i</xref>g. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2I</xref>, N).</p><p id="Par34">Paracentral acute middle maculopathy (like in case 3) was first described in 2013 by Sarraf <italic toggle="yes">et al.</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>] and was identified as a variant of acute macular neuroretinopathy. However, it is currently regarded as a different entity from the latter and is a spectral-domain OCT finding characterized by a hyper-reflective band spanning the INL, which typically evolves to INL atrophy in later stages [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>] as depicted in Fig. <xref rid="Fig3" ref-type="fig">3</xref>C. As previously described by Chu <italic toggle="yes">et al.</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>], we were able to locate an irregular vascular network in ICP and nonperfusion in DCP on OCTA (Fig. <xref rid="Fig3" ref-type="fig">3</xref>D)—corresponding to the atrophy in INL. PAMM can be caused by potential infectious, inflammatory, vascular, toxic, and iatrogenic causes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>]. Recently, cases of PAMM during pregnancy were described [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. Like our patient, the three women were described as healthy and had an otherwise uncomplicated pregnancy. One was in her first and two were in their second trimester of pregnancy, like our patient.</p><p id="Par35">OCTA was successfully performed in all three cases.
