These components exhibit significant antibacterial and antifungal activities, positioning <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> as a promising natural source of antimicrobial agents for pharmaceutical, food preservation, and biotechnological applications.</p><p>The antimicrobial activity of <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> hydrolysates is strongly influenced by the type of flour and the enzymatic hydrolysis process. Notably, hydrolysates derived from debittered and defatted flour demonstrated enhanced antimicrobial effects compared to those from raw flour. Moreover, optimal hydrolysis conditions—specifically the use of pancreatin for 48 h—maximized the inhibition of <italic toggle="yes">S. aureus</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, suggesting their potential as natural antimicrobial agents in food preservation and health applications [<xref rid="B172-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">172</xref>]. In a similar vein, enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation have been used to generate bioactive peptides from <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> seeds, which have shown strong antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive <italic toggle="yes">(S. aureus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. subtilis</italic>) and Gram-negative (<italic toggle="yes">P. aeruginosa</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>) bacteria. These peptides exert their antimicrobial effects by disrupting bacterial membranes and interfering with essential cellular processes, with some fractions demonstrating over 85% inhibition against specific bacterial strains [<xref rid="B161-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">161</xref>].</p><p>In addition to peptides, <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> seeds contain a variety of alkaloids with significant antimicrobial potential. A study analyzing 22 genotypes of <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> spp. (<italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">L. luteus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">L. angustifolius</italic>) identified more than 30 alkaloids, with notable variations among species and genotypes. Extracts from these seeds displayed potent antibacterial activity against <italic toggle="yes">K. pneumoniae</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">P. aeruginosa</italic>, suggesting their potential in combating infections caused by these pathogens. Interestingly, <italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic> landraces exhibited the highest alkaloid concentrations, with major compounds including lupanine, 13α-hydroxylupanine, and albine, whereas <italic toggle="yes">L. angustifolius</italic> primarily contained lupanine, 13α-hydroxylupanine, and angustifoline [<xref rid="B173-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">173</xref>]. The presence of these bioactive alkaloids underscores the pharmaceutical potential of <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> as a natural antimicrobial source.</p><p>Beyond seeds, Lupinus leaf and root extracts have also demonstrated antimicrobial effects. <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus arboreus</italic> leaf extract and its fractions, obtained through cold maceration with methanol, exhibited strong antibacterial activity, particularly against <italic toggle="yes">B. subtilis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">S. aureus</italic>, although its antifungal effects were weaker. Among the fractions tested, the ethyl acetate fraction showed the highest antimicrobial potency, highlighting its potential as a source of novel bioactive compounds for therapeutic applications [<xref rid="B174-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">174</xref>]. In contrast, while <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus termis</italic> root oil extract lacked antimicrobial activity, its methanolic extract, rich in quinolizidine alkaloids, demonstrated moderate antibacterial effects. These findings, alongside the traditional use of <italic toggle="yes">L. termis</italic> seed extracts in ethnopharmacology for treating shingles, reinforce the medicinal significance of <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> spp. [<xref rid="B175-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">175</xref>].</p><p>Protein fractions from <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> seeds have also been identified as key contributors to their antimicrobial properties. Specifically, 11S globulins and a basic subunit (BS) from <italic toggle="yes">L. termis</italic> seeds demonstrated significant antibacterial effects against <italic toggle="yes">Listeria monocytogenes</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Klebsiella oxytoca</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Proteus mirabilis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">S. aureus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Listeria ivanovii</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Salmonella typhimurium</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">P. aeruginosa</italic>. The BS exhibited the strongest activity, with MIC values ranging from 0.05 to 2 μg/mL, while the 11S globulin displayed comparatively weaker effects. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed bacterial cell deformation and decay, indicating membrane disruption as a probable mechanism of action [<xref rid="B176-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">176</xref>].</p><p>Moreover, the methylated derivative of 11S globulin (M11S) exhibited even greater antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity.
