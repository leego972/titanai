# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

In East Africa, the population using traditional medicine, especially in rural areas, is over 80% [<xref rid="B29-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. The preference for folk medicine over conventional health services has been motivated by its easy accessibility, low cost, social-cultural influence, and the belief that herbal medicine is regarded as safe [<xref rid="B2-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="B29-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. However, CAM faces various challenges related to its limited understanding by patients and low acceptance by conventional healthcare providers [<xref rid="B30-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. The use of CAM may lead to a drop in using conventional healthcare systems, which may increase morbidity and mortality due to complications that cannot be handled by traditional healers.</p><p>Since 2000, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that LMICs integrate CAM into their national health systems but only a limited number of countries (Tanzania, South Africa, Sierra Leone, Cameroon, Ethiopia, and Ghana) have managed to do so [<xref rid="B30-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. Africa is considered a cradle of several medicinal plants and a source of cultural diversity [<xref rid="B31-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>]. The integration of CAM into national health systems would help to prevent its complete disappearance as a result of being orally transferred from parents to their offspring [<xref rid="B30-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>,<xref rid="B32-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="B33-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. In Rwanda, traditional medicine has been exercised by traditional healers known as <italic toggle="yes">abavuzi gakondo</italic> to treat various diseases of both humans and livestock [<xref rid="B33-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>,<xref rid="B34-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>]. However, research on traditional medicine has dated back to 1972 and it has helped to transform its image from a magical art to a trusted science [<xref rid="B35-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>]; it is estimated that 70% of the population in Rwanda uses traditional medicine [<xref rid="B36-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>]. The over-exploitation of medicinal plants for commercial purposes and the accelerated level of urbanization threaten the extinction of this precious heritage [<xref rid="B37-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="B38-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]. The European colonization introduced several invasive species which led to a significant decline in indigenous plants up to the verge of extinction [<xref rid="B38-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]. The use of medicinal plants was attributed to poor people and was regarded as witchcraft [<xref rid="B34-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>]. In Rwanda, CAM was run through the University Research Centre for Pharmacopoeia Knowledge and Traditional Medicine (CURPHAMETRA), which was under the Institute of Scientific and Technological Research [<xref rid="B39-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>]. Despite not being well-integrated into the Rwandan healthcare system, a national policy of traditional, complementary, and alternative medicine has been established [<xref rid="B40-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>].</p><p>In Africa, the culture of collecting and using medicinal plants has been transferred orally throughout generations [<xref rid="B37-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>]. Globally, there is a shortage of information on traditional medicine available for research purposes [<xref rid="B36-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>]. The association of systematics, vernacular names, and indigenous uses of medicinal plants should be seen as paramount to either protect traditional knowledge or lay a foundation for further studies [<xref rid="B29-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. It is reported that successful research in ethnopharmacology requires the concerted efforts of botanists, chemists, taxonomists, pharmacologists, tradipractitioners, and beneficiaries [<xref rid="B37-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>]. Traditional medicine still faces challenges, including the scarcity of medicinal plants, the absence of harmonized preparation methods and quality control measures, and poor storage conditions [<xref rid="B23-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>].</p><p>The Diarrheal Disease Control Programme of the WHO advocates the use of traditional folklore medicines in the control and management of diarrhea [<xref rid="B7-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B23-antibiotics-10-01231" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>].
