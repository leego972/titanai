Morphology</title><p>The morphology of nanocrystals varies based on the preparation methods or stabilizers employed. Stabilizers have been shown to influence crystal growth rates in distinct directions [<xref rid="B48-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>], yielding diverse morphologies, such as needle-like, spherical, flaky, cubic, and rod-like crystals. This phenomenon significantly impacts the dissolution and bioavailability of nanocrystals [<xref rid="B11-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>].</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec4-molecules-28-06370"><title>4. Application of Nanocrystals in Herbal Medicines</title><p>This section offers an overview of the application of herbal medicine nanocrystals for diverse diseases, including cancer, inflammatory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, mental and nervous diseases, and antimicrobial diseases. <xref rid="molecules-28-06370-f003" ref-type="fig">Figure 3</xref> illustrates the chemical structures of several active ingredients found in herbal medicines.</p><sec id="sec4dot1-molecules-28-06370"><title>4.1. Cancer Therapy</title><p>In recent decades, herbal medicines have garnered substantial attention due to their potent biological activities, including antiproliferation, antioxidant effects, apoptosis induction, and antitumor properties, rendering them promising agents for cancer therapy. Nonetheless, many active constituents in herbal medicines fall under the BCS II or BCS IV categories, characterized by poor water solubility and membrane permeability which hinder therapeutic efficacy. Nanocrystal technology emerges as a promising strategy to address these challenges. The application of nanocrystals in herbal medicines for cancer therapy is outlined in <xref rid="molecules-28-06370-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>.</p><p>Annonaceous acetogenins (ACGs) are prominent antitumor agents extracted from seeds of the <italic toggle="yes">Annonaceae</italic> species [<xref rid="B49-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>]. They exhibit significant antitumor activity against various cancer cell lines, including A549, MCF-7, L1210, SMMC7721, HeLa, MKN-45, and HepG2 [<xref rid="B49-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>,<xref rid="B50-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>,<xref rid="B51-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>,<xref rid="B52-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>,<xref rid="B53-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>]. Despite their efficacy, ACGs may cause acute toxicity upon oral administration, for example, liver and kidney damage [<xref rid="B54-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>,<xref rid="B55-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>]. To enhance ACGs’ antitumor effectiveness while mitigating toxicity, Hong et al. produced an ACG nanocrystal (ACG-NSps) using an antisolvent sonoprecipitation technique. Employing mPEG2000-PCL2000 as a stabilizer, the resulting ACG-NSps exhibited particle sizes of 123.2 ± 3.54 nm and a zeta potential of −20.17 mV. This formulation demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity against 4T1, MCF-7, and HeLa cells, superior to the free drug. Notably, ACG-NSps exhibited efficient tumor accumulation and achieved enhanced therapeutic efficacy with a mere 1/10th of the dose compared to the drug oil solution [<xref rid="B50-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>].</p><p>Gambogenic acid, a key component of <italic toggle="yes">Garcinia hanburyi</italic>, displays a broad antineoplastic spectrum across breast cancer, lung cancer, and multiple myeloma [<xref rid="B56-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B57-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>,<xref rid="B58-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>,<xref rid="B59-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>]. It holds the potential to reverse P-gp-mediated multidrug resistance and influence apoptosis through ROS-dependent activation of IRE1α/JNK [<xref rid="B60-molecules-28-06370" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>].
