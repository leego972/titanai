Thyme oil (whose principle constituent is thymol, like <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera</italic>'s essential oil) displays spasmolytic muscle-relaxant, as well as antibacterial, antimycotic and antioxidative properties, and is among the world's top ten essential oils [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B98">98</xref>]. Thyme oil was formerly prescribed for dyspepsia, dysmenorrhea, headache, to relieve "hysteria" and "exhausting diseases", and as a soporific [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B99">99</xref>], and biomedicine continues to use the thymol, in various common topical ointments and antiseptic solutions (e.g. Vicks<sup>® </sup>and Listerine<sup>®</sup>). It is a prescription for intestinal parasites, particularly hookworm [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B100">100</xref>]. Because thymol can irritates gastric mucosa, pharmaceuticals largely avoid its internal use; yet thymol remains an official United States Pharmacopeia (USP) drug [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B101">101</xref>]. Most interesting in terms of treating fright, thymol, the principal constituent in <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera</italic>, inhibits neurotransmission in the central nervous system (has GABAergic activity) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B102">102</xref>] and thus regulates excitability and has general anesthetic properties (similar to propofol) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B103">103</xref>].</p></sec></sec><sec><title>Go dité - <italic toggle="yes">Plectranthus amboinicus </italic>Sprengel</title><sec><title>University of Missouri herbarium vouchers UMO-185582 and UMO-186388</title><p>The third most salient treatment for fright is <italic toggle="yes">Plectranthus </italic>[Coleus] <italic toggle="yes">amboinicus </italic>[Loureiro] Sprengel (Lamiaceae), which Dominicans call <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">go djai</italic>, whether speaking English or French Creole (figure <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F5">5</xref>). (The species is widely used in tropical regions and outside of Dominica has numerous English common names including Cuban oregano, Mexican mint, Indian borage, Spanish thyme, and French thyme.) In freelists, Dominicans listed <italic toggle="yes">go dite/go djai </italic>one time more than <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>(38 mentions, or 46% of informants), however these mentions fell further toward the ends of individuals' freelists making the plant slightly less salient statistically (<italic toggle="yes">go dité (P. amboinicus) </italic>silence score is .31 and <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>(<italic toggle="yes">L.micromera</italic>) has a .39, a 7.9% difference). <italic toggle="yes">Plectranthus amboinicus </italic>is a native of the East Indies that has become naturalized in some of the West Indies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B104">104</xref>]. It is a semi-succulent herb that reaches a height of 50 cm. It has opposite, deeply veined leaves 3-6 cm wide by 4-8 cm long with obvious hair and serrated edges. The square stems are rounded. The flower is violet. I have only observed this plant growing in village home gardens, where it is an essential household herb.</p><fig id="F5" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 5</label><caption><p><bold>Close-up view of Dominican <italic toggle="yes">go dité</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Plectranthus </italic>[Coleus] <italic toggle="yes">amboinicus </italic>[Loureiro] Sprengel (photo by Sarah Council)</bold>.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="1746-4269-6-9-5.jpg"/></fig><p>In addition to its medicinal role, <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>is an everyday cooking herb in Dominica, (as elsewhere in the middle Americas). Nearly every family has some of this plant growing near their home. Dominican villagers consume <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>almost every day in very dilute amounts (one leaf per 4 liters of stew). Medicinally, it is a hot herb that Bwa Mawegans always ingest in the form of tea. Besides using <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>for fright, some Dominican women use it to induce menstruation and labor, throughout labor, and after childbirth. To make a medicinal bush tea, villagers use two average stalks (containing around 24 leaves) to one liter of water. Some people make a decoction, boiling the tea for a couple of minutes, while others make an infusion by steeping <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>for several minutes.
