The knowledge of medicinal plants from ancient literature and text came to light through Carl Linnaeus's classification system, described in his book “<italic toggle="yes">Species Plantarum</italic>” [<xref rid="B25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>].</p><p>In the early 19<sup>th</sup> century, the advent of advanced synthetic chemistry helps us to decipher the mechanism, isolation and synthesis of active compounds from popular medicinal plants, such as poppy, ipecacuanha, strychnos, quinine, and pomegranate [<xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>]. Despite the enriched history and success of medicinal plants, during the late 19<sup>th</sup> and early 20<sup>th</sup> centuries, the research on medicinal plant did not progress as it was supposed to. The reluctance of pharmaceutical industries about plant-based components caused a significant shift of focus from plant to synthetic chemistry on drug development [<xref rid="B27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>].</p><p>Fortunately, the gear has been shifted in recent years. In 2015, the Nobel Prize in physiology and medicine was awarded to Tu Youyou for her discovery of artemisinin and dihydroartemisinin as antimalarial drug and highlight of the importance of plant-based components as a potentially powerful source of drug discovery. During the Vietnam War, Ho Chi Minh urged to develop antimalarial drugs for his soldiers. Tu Youyou became the part of that project to find the antimalarial treatment, and she screened over 2,000 traditional herbal medicines and discovered <italic toggle="yes">Artemisia annua</italic> recipe [<xref rid="B28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>].</p><p>The success story of antimalarial drug based on traditional herbal medicine is not an isolated story; rather, it is a tiny part of plant-based arsenal as potential drugs. As a result, the significant effort on finding therapeutic agents for cancer treatment has been focused on plant-based compounds by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), USA. NCI-initiated Cancer Moonshot<sup>SM</sup> project, aimed to accelerate the cancer research by making more cancer therapeutics available for patients, is focused on phytochemicals. As a part of this project, they have established a repertoire of natural products, and their purified chemical components to make them available for researchers to find new anticancer drugs.</p><p>Till date, several plant-based compounds have been reported for their anticancer activity, and among them, a good number of compounds is clinically successful as well. Success Stories of Medicinal Plant-Based Anticancer Drug</title><p>Till date, more than thousand plants species have been identified with noteworthy anticancer potential [<xref rid="B90" ref-type="bibr">90</xref>, <xref rid="B91" ref-type="bibr">91</xref>]. The isolation of the vinca alkaloids, vinblastine [<xref rid="B92" ref-type="bibr">92</xref>] from the Madagascar periwinkle, and <italic toggle="yes">Catharanthus roseus</italic> G. Don. (Apocynaceae) is one of the major examples of anticancer medication. This along with vincristine and other cancer chemotherapeutic drugs are used for the treatment of a range of cancers such as leukemias, lymphomas, advanced testicular cancer, breast and lung cancers, and Kaposi's sarcoma [<xref rid="B30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>, <xref rid="B91" ref-type="bibr">91</xref>]. The discovery of paclitaxel (Taxol) [<xref rid="B93" ref-type="bibr">93</xref>] from the bark of the Pacific Yew, <italic toggle="yes">Taxus brevifolia</italic> Nutt. (Taxaceae), is another major success story in natural product drug discovery. Utilization of various parts of <italic toggle="yes">Taxus brevifolia</italic> from which paclitaxel was discovered and other <italic toggle="yes">Taxus</italic> species (e.g., <italic toggle="yes">Taxus Canadensis</italic> Marshall and <italic toggle="yes">Taxus baccata</italic> L.) by several Native American Tribes kindle the idea of indigenous knowledge-based medicinal plants [<xref rid="B30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>, <xref rid="B91" ref-type="bibr">91</xref>]. Another potent plant-acquired active compound, Homoharringtonine [<xref rid="B94" ref-type="bibr">94</xref>], was extracted from the Chinese tree <italic toggle="yes">Cephalotaxus harringtonia</italic> var. drupacea (Sieb and Zucc.) (Cephalotaxaceae) and has been used successfully for a long time in China in a racemic mixture with harringtonine for the treatment of acute myelogenous leukemia [<xref rid="B30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. Elliptinium, a derivative of ellipticine, isolated from a Fijian medicinal plant <italic toggle="yes">Bleekeria vitensis</italic> A. C. Sm., is shipped to France for the treatment of breast cancer [<xref rid="B30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>, <xref rid="B91" ref-type="bibr">91</xref>].
