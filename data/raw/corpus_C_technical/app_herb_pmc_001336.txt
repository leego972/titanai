Variations across years and accessions highlight the influence of both genetic and environmental factors [<xref rid="B96-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">96</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec6dot2-nutrients-17-01749"><title>6.2. Peptides</title><p>Amaranth peptides are derived from the storage proteins found in amaranth grains. These proteins contain encrypted amino acid sequences that, once released through enzymatic hydrolysis, exhibit a range of physiological activities [<xref rid="B97-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">97</xref>]. Amaranth is particularly valued for its high-quality protein content and complete amino acid profile, including lysine, which is typically limited in other cereals [<xref rid="B98-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">98</xref>]. The main storage proteins are globulins, with globulin 11S—also known as amarantin—being a principal source of bioactive peptides. During food processing or digestion, proteolytic cleavage of these proteins liberates peptides with potential health-promoting effects.</p><p>Several studies have identified bioactive peptides from Amaranthus protein hydrolysates, with molecular weights ranging from 500 Da to 1.44 kDa. These peptides are relatively small, favoring intestinal absorption and enhancing bioactivity. For example, the peptide ITASANEPDENKS (1.44 kDa) was among the largest identified, while others ranged between 8 and 14 amino acids in length [<xref rid="B99-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">99</xref>]. Their sequences frequently contain hydrophobic amino acids such as leucine, valine, proline, and isoleucine, which may facilitate interaction with membrane-bound enzymes such as ACE or HMG-CoA reductase, suggesting antihypertensive and hypocholesterolemic potential [<xref rid="B98-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">98</xref>,<xref rid="B100-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>].</p><p>Using sequence data from databases like UniProt and predictive tools such as BIOPEP, researchers have mapped fragments from amaranth proteins with predicted ACE-inhibitory, DPP-IV inhibitory, and antioxidant properties [<xref rid="B100-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>]. These peptides often include residues like tryptophan and tyrosine, known for stabilizing free radicals and enhancing antioxidant effects. Acidic amino acids (e.g., aspartic and glutamic acid) contribute to metal chelation, further supporting their antioxidant role.</p><p>Among the peptides identified through in vitro hydrolysis of <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus cruentus</italic>, GGV, IVG/LVG, and VGVI/VGVL were notable for their small size (all &lt; 3 kDa) and high hydrophobic content. These features may promote interaction with lipid membranes and key enzymes involved in metabolic regulation [<xref rid="B98-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">98</xref>].</p><p>A detailed study on the gastrointestinal digestion of 11S globulin revealed ten bioactive peptides, mainly from the acidic subunit, with sequences such as TEVWDSNEQ, IYIEQGNGITGM, and KFNRPETT. These peptides showed molecular weights around 1 kDa. GRAVY index analysis indicated that most were hydrophilic, although some exhibited amphipathic characteristics. Their net charge at physiological pH, combined with features like terminal arginine residues, may modulate their interaction with cell membranes or molecular targets [<xref rid="B100-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>]).</p><p>Together, these findings highlight the potential of amaranth-derived peptides as multifunctional bioactive agents for use in nutraceutical and functional food applications.</p></sec><sec id="sec6dot3-nutrients-17-01749"><title>6.3.
