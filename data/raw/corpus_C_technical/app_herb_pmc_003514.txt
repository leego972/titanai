# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

The use of <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic> corresponds with reports from Kenya, Uganda, India, China, and Europe, where these taxa are valued for antibacterial, antiviral, and immune-supporting properties [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">137</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR141">141</xref>]. Preparation methods such as steam inhalation further demonstrate culturally refined knowledge of appropriate administration routes for respiratory infections.</p><p id="Par107">Skin diseases, wounds, and inflammatory conditions were treated using 15 species (24.1%), reflecting frequent exposure to injuries and harsh environmental conditions. Similar ethnomedicinal reliance on topical remedies has been documented in Ethiopia, Tanzania, and South America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR86">86</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR142">142</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR145">145</xref>].</p><p id="Par108">In Sheikh District, the topical use of latex from <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic> reflects broader African and Asian ethnomedical traditions in which latex-bearing plants are culturally valued for wound healing and antimicrobial effects.</p><p id="Par109">A smaller number of species were reported for ocular ailments such as conjunctivitis and trachoma, demonstrating cautious cultural practices when treating sensitive organs. Plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe citrina</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Olea europaea</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Terminalia prunioides</italic> were applied with controlled dosage, consistent with reports from Ethiopia, Sudan, and Pakistan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR146">146</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR150">150</xref>].</p><p id="Par110">MPs were also used for chronic and systemic conditions including diabetes, tuberculosis, musculoskeletal pain, urinary disorders, and sexually transmitted infections. For example, the use of <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic> for diabetes aligns with studies recognizing its hypoglycemic and antioxidant properties across East Africa and South Asia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR151">151</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR156">156</xref>]. Similarly, the use of <italic toggle="yes">Albizia anthelmintica</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Carica papaya</italic> seeds for infectious and parasitic diseases reflects well-established practices across Africa and Latin America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR97">97</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR158">158</xref>].</p><p id="Par111">Across all remedies, leaves were the most frequently used plant part, followed by roots, seeds, fruits, bark, and latex. The predominance of leaves is often associated with ease of access, simple preparation, and more sustainable harvesting compared with destructive root or bark collection. Leaves are also known to contain high concentrations of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, alkaloids, and phenolics [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">137</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR159">159</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR160">160</xref>]. Similar plant-part use patterns have been widely documented across Africa, Asia, and Europe [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR161">161</xref>].</p><p id="Par112">Roots and bark were generally reserved for more severe or systemic illnesses, reflecting local beliefs that underground parts contain concentrated medicinal strength. Multipurpose use of different plant parts was also common. For instance, <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic> was used to treat asthma (roots), snakebite (bark), and diarrhea (fruit), illustrating its therapeutic versatility and high cultural value. Such multipurpose use, while signaling pharmacological potential, may also increase conservation pressure, as widely used species often face greater harvesting intensity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>].</p><p id="Par113">Thus, the findings demonstrate that communities in Sheikh District rely extensively on MPs and diverse plant parts to address a broad spectrum of health challenges.
