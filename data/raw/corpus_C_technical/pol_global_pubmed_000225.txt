Transforming drug and vaccine delivery for equitable access in global health.

Yang X(1), Zhang L(2), Staykov K(1), Wu Z(1), Jaklenec A(3).

Author information:
(1)David H. Koch Institute for Integrative Cancer Research, Massachusetts 
Institute of Technology, Cambridge, USA.
(2)Department of Chemical Engineering, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 
Cambridge, USA.
(3)David H. Koch Institute for Integrative Cancer Research, Massachusetts 
Institute of Technology, Cambridge, USA. Electronic address: jaklenec@mit.edu.

Ensuring equitable access to medicines and vaccines remains a major global 
health challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where 
infrastructure, cost, and supply chain constraints limit the reach of essential 
therapies. Conventional delivery methods often rely on cold-chain logistics, 
multi-dose regimens, trained personnel, and centralized manufacturing-factors 
that hinder effective distribution in resource-limited settings. This review 
highlights how emerging drug and vaccine delivery technologies can overcome 
these barriers and advance global health equity. We discuss key design 
requirements-such as thermostability, ease of administration, single-dose 
potential, and scalable production-that must be met for delivery platforms to be 
viable in low-resource environments. Promising technologies include 
controlled-release systems that provide sustained or pulsatile dosing, 
microneedle patches that enable painless, self-administered delivery, and dry 
formulation strategies like lyophilization and spray drying that reduce 
cold-chain dependence. Oral and edible vaccines leveraging nanoparticle carriers 
offer non-invasive immunization routes, while digital tools such as smart 
packaging, ingestible sensors, and wearable devices improve adherence and 
support remote care. Despite their potential, these innovations face challenges 
in regulatory approval, large-scale manufacturing, and end-user adoption. Global 
initiatives such as the Vaccine Innovation Prioritisation Strategy (VIPS) and 
the African Vaccine Manufacturing Accelerator (AVMA) reflect growing efforts to 
address these hurdles and translate innovation into access. By aligning 
technical advances with the practical realities of underserved communities, 
next-generation delivery technologies offer a pathway toward more inclusive and 
sustainable healthcare systems.
