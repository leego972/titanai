These properties have proven to be useful in classification tasks, allowing to discriminate between different types of tissue.</p><sec id="sec3.1.1"><label>3.1.1</label><title>Static analysis</title><p>Roy et al.<xref rid="r12" ref-type="bibr"><sup>12</sup></xref> used the mean (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math4" display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), standard deviation (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math5" display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>σ</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), kurtosis (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math6" display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>κ</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), skewness (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math7" display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>ν</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), and an estimate of optical attenuation and signal confidence measures to detect plaques’s susceptibility to rupture using intravascular OCT, with the objective of assessing atherosclerosis. The features proved to have high performance in the identification of such tissues using a random forest predictive model (area under the receiver operating characteristics curve of 0.9676).</p><p>Wang et al.<xref rid="r13" ref-type="bibr"><sup>13</sup></xref> implemented a model for the detection of soft tissue sarcomas in OCT images of <italic toggle="yes">ex vivo</italic> human tissues, also based on speckle statistical properties. Specifically, the standard deviation of the signal fluctuations (speckles) of a single axial line (A-scan) was used. The statistical analysis (Student’s <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math8" display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>t</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>-test) of the standard deviation showed it is effective for comparing normal fat tissue and soft tissue sarcoma (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math9" display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>p</mml:mi><mml:mtext> value</mml:mtext><mml:mo>&lt;</mml:mo><mml:mn>0.01</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>).</p></sec><sec id="sec3.1.2"><label>3.1.2</label><title>Dynamic speckle analysis</title><p>Ossowski et al.<xref rid="r14" ref-type="bibr"><sup>14</sup></xref> used statistical properties of the OCT speckle to infer the dynamic properties of blood samples. Specifically, they used the mean horizontal and vertical speckle sizes, calculated from intensity data, and the sum of standard deviations of selected windows, calculated from phase data. These three statistical parameters were computed in OCT images of blood samples [<xref rid="f4" ref-type="fig">Figs. 4(a)</xref> and <xref rid="f4" ref-type="fig">4(b)</xref>], enabling a visual distinction between the signal modulation from erythrocytes and leukocytes as shown in <xref rid="f4" ref-type="fig">Fig. 4</xref>.
