All the above mentioned cinnamic acids, with exception to 1-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-<italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaroylglycerol were reported in the aqueous methanol extracts of <italic toggle="yes">Z. aquatica</italic> [<xref rid="B55-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>,<xref rid="B92-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">92</xref>] (<xref ref-type="app" rid="app1-ijms-20-01067">Table S1</xref>). Additionally, sinapic acid (<bold>85</bold>), known for its numerous biological activities including the ability to inhibit lipid peroxidation (IC<sub>50</sub> (half minimum inhibitory concentration): 500μmol/kg) [<xref rid="B93-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">93</xref>] and to promote anxiolytic effects (IC<sub>50</sub>: 4 mg/kg) [<xref rid="B94-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">94</xref>], was also described in this species. Similarly, ferulic acid (<bold>84</bold>) and its derivatives are well known for their anti-inflammatory (IC<sub>50</sub>: 500 µg/mL) [<xref rid="B95-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">95</xref>], antidiabetic (maximum concentration tested, 50 mg/(kg body weight·day)–1) [<xref rid="B96-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">96</xref>], anti-carcinogenic (IC<sub>50</sub>: 25–75 µm) [<xref rid="B97-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">97</xref>] anti-aging [<xref rid="B98-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">98</xref>], and radioprotective (10 μM and 100 µM, dose-dependent) [<xref rid="B99-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">99</xref>] properties, among others. These results along with the literature data about the medicinal attributes of cinnamic acids and derivatives [<xref rid="B100-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>] contribute to the halophytic grasses’ great potential to be used in food and pharmaceutical industries.</p><p>Plant benzoic acids are considered to be building blocks or key structural elements for primary and specialized metabolites [<xref rid="B101-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">101</xref>]. In halophytic grasses, nine benzoic acids and derivatives (<bold>86</bold>–<bold>94</bold>) (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-20-01067-f004">Figure 4</xref>) have been reported (<xref ref-type="app" rid="app1-ijms-20-01067">Table S1</xref>). The species with greater variety were <italic toggle="yes">E. crus-galli</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Z. aquatica</italic> with five compounds each. Protocatechuic acid (<bold>94</bold>), only reported in <italic toggle="yes">Z. aquatica</italic>, has been widely studied for its biological activities. A full review on this aspect was performed by Kakkar and co-workers (2014), emphasizing its antibacterial, antioxidant, antidiabetic, anticarcinogenic, antiviral, antiaging, antifibrotic, anti-inflammatory, antipyretic and analgesic effects [<xref rid="B102-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">102</xref>].</p><p>Other short chain carboxylic acids and derivatives (<bold>95</bold>–<bold>101</bold>) were also reported in <italic toggle="yes">C. dactylon</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">D. spicata</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">I. cylindrica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">S. spontaneum</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Z. aquatica</italic> (<xref ref-type="app" rid="app1-ijms-20-01067">Table S1</xref>). These compounds are known for their wide range of pharmacological activities. For instance, citric acid (<bold>96</bold>), only reported in <italic toggle="yes">C. dactylon</italic>, have protective effects on myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (IC<sub>50</sub>: 400 μg/mL and 200 μg/mL) [<xref rid="B103-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">103</xref>], while succinic acid (<bold>101</bold>) is known for its broad range of applications in the medicinal area as an antioxidant, antiradical and adaptogenic agent [<xref rid="B104-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">104</xref>].</p><p>Cinnamic acids and derivatives, as well as short chain carboxylic acids, are common in diets rich in vegetables and fruits [<xref rid="B105-ijms-20-01067" ref-type="bibr">105</xref>].
