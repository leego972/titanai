Precautions/Contraindications</title><p>Based on studies in animals suggesting that <italic toggle="yes">E. longifolia</italic> reduced blood glucose in hyperglycemic animals [<xref rid="B168-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">168</xref>] and unpublished studies in humans suggesting the possibility for increased blood glucose, it should be used cautiously in patients using hypoglycemic agents. Also use with in individuals using propranolol, as in healthy males, a water-based extract of <italic toggle="yes">E. longifolia</italic> decreased the bioavailability of propranolol [<xref rid="B202-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">202</xref>].</p><p>It should be used cautiously in people with weakened immune systems, as some evidence suggests that it may further weaken immune function, according to secondary sources [<xref rid="B185-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>]. Use is to be avoided in individuals with diseases like breast cancer, prostate cancer, heart disease, kidney disease, liver disease, or sleep apnea, according to secondary sources [<xref rid="B185-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>,<xref rid="B203-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">203</xref>].</p><p>Use in patients with known allergy or hypersensitivity to <italic toggle="yes">E. longifolia</italic>, its constituents, or hypersensitivity to other members of the Simaroubaceae family is also to be avoided [<xref rid="B185-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>,<xref rid="B203-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">203</xref>]. Use during pregnancy and lactation and in children is not suggested due to a lack of sufficient data [<xref rid="B185-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>]. Information on <italic toggle="yes">E. longifolia’s</italic> effects on lactation is lacking in the National Institute of Health’s Lactation and Toxicology Database (LactMed) [<xref rid="B203-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">203</xref>].</p><p>One <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> study indicates that in animals, no negative effect on the offspring could be found, neither in terms of malformations nor of any effect on body weight or the number of the offspring [<xref rid="B195-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">195</xref>]. Fertility index and litter size of the treated rats were significantly increased, compared to non-treated rats [<xref rid="B200-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">200</xref>].</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec8-molecules-21-00331"><title>8. Conclusions</title><p>Novel molecular diversity (abbreviate as NMD) poses a formidable challenge for a rational drug design. Bioassay-guided fractionation of natural products is one high-throughput screening (HTS) approach to identify potent bioactive molecules. Today natural products continue to play a major role as active substances and model molecules for the discovery and validation of drug targets. Herbal medicines have been used for thousands of years in almost all developing countries and recently, the World Health Organization estimated that 80% of people worldwide rely on herbal medicines for some part of their primary health care. A multidisciplinary approach in new drug discovery, mostly involving the generation of truly novel molecular diversity from natural herbal sources, combined with combinatorial synthetic methodologies, provides the best solution to increase the novelty and productivity in novel drug discovery and further development. Screening for new drugs in plant sources implies the screening of extracts for the presence of novel compounds as well as investigation of their biological activities.</p><p>Whereas over 100,000 secondary metabolites are already known, only a small percentage of all species have been studied for the presence of secondary metabolites. It is currently estimated that approximately 420,000 plant species exist in Nature [<xref rid="B204-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">204</xref>], and less than 5% of known plants have been screened for one or more biological activities [<xref rid="B205-molecules-21-00331" ref-type="bibr">205</xref>].</p><p>The advances in the phytochemical analysis, especially the impact of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-coupled spectroscopy on natural product research, have been tremendous in the rapid characterization of natural product extracts.
