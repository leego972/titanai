# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

This integrated approach allowed thorough documentation of local knowledge on medicinal plants, including identification, preparation methods, therapeutic applications, and conservation practices, providing rich, contextually grounded information.</p><p id="Par32">Semi-structured interviews targeted informant’s native to, or long-term residents of, the Karamara region, ensuring authentic and experienced perspectives. Interviews were conducted in Somali with the support of local translators, and responses were recorded in English for analysis (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>). A guiding questionnaire captured demographic data (age, sex, residence) and detailed ethnobotanical information, including vernacular names, ailments treated, plant parts used, sources (wild or cultivated), relative abundance, preparation methods, administration routes, seasonal availability, and conservation practices. The semi-structured format allowed probing for nuanced knowledge, individual experiences, and local variations, following established ethnobotanical protocols [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>].</p><p id="Par33">Plant specimens were collected with prior informed consent from landowners and in coordination with district administrative and agricultural offices, adhering to ethical research and conservation guidelines. Guided field walks with key informants enabled the identification and documentation of MPs in their natural habitats, with observations on morphological characteristics, growth habits, ecological context, and plant distribution. Voucher specimens were labeled, preserved via pressing, air-drying, or deep-freezing, and photographed to support taxonomic verification and create visual references (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>).</p><p id="Par34">Field walks also facilitated the collection of supplementary ethnobotanical data, including preparation techniques, alternative uses, and local conservation practices. Observations highlighted threats to MPs and patterns of knowledge transfer across generations, providing an integrated understanding of both ecological and cultural dimensions of plant use.</p><p id="Par35">Data collection relied primarily on self-reported information, which may be affected by recall bias or personal perception. Seasonal constraints limited access to some plant species, potentially underrepresenting seasonal or ephemeral taxa. Additionally, the oral transmission of traditional knowledge may have introduced variability in information accuracy. These limitations were mitigated through cross-validation across multiple informants, repeated field visits, and triangulation with guided observations, but they should be considered when interpreting the findings.</p><p id="Par36">
