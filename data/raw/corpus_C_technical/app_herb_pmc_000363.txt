Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis is a very serious disease with high morbidity and mortality requiring prompt diagnosis and treatment with open or endoscopic sinus surgery [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. Diagnosis is usually histopathological, so early endoscopic evaluation is indicated followed by biopsy in cases where disease is suspected [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>Pathophysiology</title><p id="Par36">ARS includes acute viral, post-viral and ABRS [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Besides the particular virus strain and its virulence, the severity and pathogenesis of ARS is highly dependent on the host and predisposing elements such as age, immunodeficiency, previous infection or immunization [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>]. Other contributing factors include preexisting mucosal inflammation caused by exposure to allergens, pathogens or other environmental risk factors, as well as anatomical variations of the nose and paranasal sinuses [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>].</p><p id="Par37">The pathogenesis and pathophysiology of ARS is not fully understood. This is mainly due to the lack of prospective clinical studies and basic research during the natural course of ARS, since in the literature, most of the studies cited are in vitro studies, using human tissue or cell lines from volunteers and experiments on animals and therefore the results have not yet been validated in cases with a naturally acquired viral infection and ARS [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>]. The pathophysiology and inflammatory process of viral and postviral infection, as well as ABRS, may overlap significantly and the same applies to their clinical presentation.</p><p id="Par38">Viral ARS (or common cold) is by definition an acute rhinitis caused by respiratory viruses such as rhinovirus (RV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza virus (IFV), coronavirus (CorV), parainfluenza virus (PIV), adenovirus (AdeV) and enterovirus (EV) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>]. The most common viruses isolated from adults with ARS accounting for approximately 50% of viral ARS are RV and CorV [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>]. In children there is a broader phasma of viruses that are isolated, i.e. in addition to rhinoviruses and coronaviruses, RSV, PIV and AdeV are also often present [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>]. Depending on the geographic distribution, other viruses have also been isolated from patients with ARS, e.g. the human vocavirus [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>]. With the advancement of medical technology and the development of highly sensitive methods for the detection of viruses, it is now possible to detect multiple viruses. To yet, it is rather difficult however to identify the exact viruses that cause or lead to an exacerbation of ARS in clinical practice.</p><p id="Par39">The pathophysiology and the pathogenic mechanisms of postviral ARS have not been fully elucidated. A viral infection of the nose and paranasal sinuses causes many changes, such as mucosal infiltration by various inflammatory cells or activation of others, as well as impairment in innate and humoral immunity, which increase the risk of bacterial infection [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>]. Only a small percentage of patients with ARS will eventually develop ABRS suggesting that postviral ARS is not an indication of the development of a bacterial infection [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>].</p><p id="Par40">According to EPOS2020, ABRS is an uncommon complication of viral upper respiratory tract infections causing mucosal damage and bacterial 'super-infection' [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Disruption of mucosal function due to viral infection is probably a major cause of super- or secondary bacterial infection [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>]. Bacterial and fungal infections are usually accompanied by viral infections, such as in the common cold (RV-infection) and CRS [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Streptococcus pneumoniae, Hemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis are the most common pathogens in rhinosinusitis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>]. In another study, in cell cultures with RV-1b infection, a mechanism involving virus-induced release of IL-6 and IL-8, and overexpression of ICAM-1, can promote Staphylococcus aureus penetration [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>].
