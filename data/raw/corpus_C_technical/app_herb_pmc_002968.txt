H.S.Irwin &amp; Barneby, another species of the Fabaceae family. In vitro and in vivo studies showed the anti-inflammatory and/or wound healing activities of transgenic hairy roots [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR158">158</xref>] and aerial parts [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>] of <italic toggle="yes">S. obtusifolia</italic>. An in vivo study with mice revealed that oral ingestion of the powder of the leaves of other species belonging to the same genus, <italic toggle="yes">S. occidentalis</italic> (L.) Link, has anti-inflammatory action, helping to reduce edema [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR159">159</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">S. occidentalis</italic> also appears in an ethnopharmacological survey of plants used for wound healing in Dogonland, Mali (West Africa). In that study, traditional healers from over 20 villages were interviewed and reported that the decoction of its leaves is used to wash any type of wound and that the powder of ground leaves is applied to aid healing [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR160">160</xref>].</p><p id="Par77">Cashew nut (<italic toggle="yes">Anacardium occidentale</italic> L.) is described by Merian as a vermifuge. Components extracted from the nut, such as anacardic acid, have an anti-<italic toggle="yes">Trypanosoma cruzi</italic> effect [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR136">136</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">137</xref>], as well actions against echinococcosis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR138">138</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR176">176</xref>] and a larvicidal and antimicrobial effect [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR177">177</xref>]. The ethanolic extract of the bark of this plant showed an in vitro anthelmintic effect against the parasite <italic toggle="yes">Onchocerca ochengi</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR176">176</xref>]. In an endemic region of tegumentary leishmaniasis in Bahia State, Northeastern Brazil, <italic toggle="yes">A. occidentale</italic> was reported as one of the plants used for the treatment of leishmaniasis by most of the interviewees [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR134">134</xref>]. This plant is also used by some indigenous communities in the Amazon to treat leishmaniasis and the extract of the leaves and stem has been observed to have an anti-promastigote effect [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR131">131</xref>]. The ethanol extract of cashew nuts showed an in vitro effect against adult larvae of <italic toggle="yes">Schistosoma mansoni</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">132</xref>] and also had an in vitro anthelmintic effect against <italic toggle="yes">Haemonchus contortus</italic> larvae [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR133">133</xref>]. Also, intake of cashew nuts decreased <italic toggle="yes">Haemonchus contortus</italic> infection in sheep [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR135">135</xref>].</p><p id="Par78">In the work of Maria Sibylla Merian, the traditional medicinal use of watermelon, <italic toggle="yes">Citrullus lanatus</italic> (Thunb.) Matsum. &amp; Nakai, is described in a very generic way only as useful in the recovery of the sick. This reported improvement in recovery may be due to the fact that this plant is a great source of vitamins such as thiamine, riboflavin, folate and niacin, and of minerals such as potassium, magnesium, calcium and phosphorus. Watermelon consumption has several health benefits such as reduced heart disease, better control of high blood pressure, lower LDL and a cardioprotective effect [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>]. Watermelon seeds contain several amino acids, vitamins and components with a potential protective effect on the lungs, liver and pancreas, in addition to having an antioxidant effect [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR178">178</xref>]. The seeds and pulp of <italic toggle="yes">C. lanatus</italic> are used in traditional medicine to treat gastrointestinal, respiratory and urinary diseases in India and Pakistan. In silico, in vitro and in vivo models have shown that the seed extract of this plant has an antiperistaltic and antidiarrheal effect, which is consistent with its traditional use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR140">140</xref>]. These effects can be helpful in recovery from gastrointestinal illnesses. The pulp extract of this plant showed diuretic and antiurolytic activity in both in vitro and in vivo models. The pulp extract also prevented weight loss in mice with induced kidney stone formation compared to the group that received no treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR141">141</xref>]. Weight maintenance is an important factor in recovery from illness.
