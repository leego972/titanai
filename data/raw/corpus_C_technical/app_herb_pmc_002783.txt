One species, <italic toggle="yes">Solanum incanum</italic>, was cited by respondents of Teso and Ethur for unrelated ailments (root for diarrhea, fruit for eye infection).</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Discussion</title><sec id="Sec10"><title>Context of historical studies</title><p id="Par61">As provided in the supplemental file, review of the secondary research yielded 403 citations from 139 ethnobotanical publications, including full or partial matches to species/genus, plant part and administration by indication (malady or condition), providing full or partial corroboration of 193 (73%) of the 263 reported use applications. Of the 70 use applications which were not found in any earlier ethnobotanical reports, 16 (22%) were found to be possibly supported by secondary data from in vitro and in <italic toggle="yes">vivo studies</italic>, including those involving compounds reported to be present in the respective taxon or genus.</p><p id="Par62">Of the 263 use applications by taxon listed in Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>, 191 are at least partially corroborated by one or more earlier ethnobotanical studies drawn from across the entire Africa region. Indeed, several of the cited taxa have been identified among the earliest medicinal plant remains recovered by archeological studies in northern Nigeria, including <italic toggle="yes">Vitex</italic> spp. dating back to c. 2800–2450 BP, and <italic toggle="yes">Bridelia scleroneura, Pavetta</italic> sp<bold>.</bold> and <italic toggle="yes">Sarcocephalus latifolius</italic> dated earlier than 800 CE [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>].</p><p id="Par63">No earlier mention can be found within the earlier ethnobotanical literature of 72 of the 263 medicinal applications in the current study (about 27% all use reports obtained. One notable example is <italic toggle="yes">Lepistemon owariense</italic> (Convolvulaceae), with an RI value of 79% based on four reported pharmacological properties affecting seven body systems, for which no earlier mention has been found. Other use applications undocumented in the earlier literature include seven taxa used to treat measles (<italic toggle="yes">Albizia coriaria</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Gardenia ternifolia.</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Leonotis nepetifolia, Pavetta crassipes, Strychnos innocua</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vitex madiensis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Zanthoxylum chalybeum</italic>); three as anti-emetic (<italic toggle="yes">Celosia leptostachya</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Indigofera arrecta, Phoenix reclinata</italic>); <italic toggle="yes">Dicoma sessiliflora</italic> for urinary problems, and <italic toggle="yes">Vernonia perrottetii</italic> for eye problems.</p><p id="Par64">Of the apparently novel use applications, eight have some association with bioactivity in pharmacological (in vitro or animal model) or phytochemical studies, including antiviral compounds in <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora Africana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Chrysanthellum americanum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vernonia perrottetii</italic>; antibacterial compounds in <italic toggle="yes">Albizia amara</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Synedrella nodiflora</italic>; anti-inflammatory compounds in <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia hockii</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Senegalia polyacantha</italic>, diuretic compounds in <italic toggle="yes">Sarcocephalus latifolius</italic>, and antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic and antipyretic compounds in <italic toggle="yes">Physalis minima</italic>, the leaf-paste of which is applied to the umbilical cord of the new-born infant.</p><p id="Par65">Figure <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5</xref> provides a geographic and historical context of the ethnopharmacological studies undertaken within Uganda, indicating that no earlier data were collected within the study area.<fig id="Fig5" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 5</label><caption><p>Geographic context of the Uganda studies</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="MO5" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="13002_2023_620_Fig5_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par66">Of the earlier Ugandan studies mapped in Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5</xref>, most accounts were limited to a specific ailment or condition [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR66">66</xref>] or a specific locality and culture [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR52">52</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR67">67</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec11"><title>Floristic and ecological context</title><p id="Par67">From an ecological perspective, the number of cited taxa and use reports was compiled by plant family with intention to compare the findings with earlier studies, and with what is known of the floristic composition of the study area.
