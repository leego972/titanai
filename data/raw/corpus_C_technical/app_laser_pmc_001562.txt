# PMC: optical coherence tomography clinical application — Overview

id="ceo14544-li-0042"><p>optic disc coloboma</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0043">ODD</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0044"><p>optic disc drusen</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0045">PAMM</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0046"><p>paracentral acute middle maculopathy</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0047">PCC</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0048"><p>peripapillary choriocapillaris</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0049">PHOMS</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0050"><p>peripapillary hyperreflective ovoid mass‐like structures</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0051">PPS</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0052"><p>peripapillary staphyloma</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0053">RNFL</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0054"><p>retinal nerve fibre layer</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0055">RPC</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0056"><p>radial peripapillary capillaries</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0057">RPE</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0058"><p>retinal pigment epithelium</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="ceo14544-li-0059">TAO</term><def id="ceo14544-li-0060"><p>thyroid‐associated orbitopathy</p></def></def-item></def-list><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0001"><label>1</label><title>Introduction</title><p>Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is a rapidly evolving in vivo flow‐based interferometric imaging modality with resolution to the microcirculation in the retina and optic nerve. It allows objective, quantifiable measurements of the retinal and choroidal circulation [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0001" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. Due to its comparative non‐inferiority, speed of analysis, and non‐invasive nature, it stands to revolutionise diagnostics in ophthalmology. It visualises the retinal vasculature in multiple layers in a non‐invasive way, compared to the traditional dye‐based vascular imaging like fundus fluorescein angiography and indocyanine green angiography [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0002" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>].</p><p>The utility of OCTA in neuro‐ophthalmology can aid in the diagnosis, monitoring, and management of various neuro‐ophthalmic disorders. Most clinicians felt OCTA is useful in retinovascular diseases only. However, the application goes beyond the retinovascular disorders, and it serves as a powerful tool to understand the pathophysiology of neuro‐ophthalmic diseases by characterising the vascular changes that can precede functional changes.</p><p>In this review article, we will explore the OCTA findings in ischaemic optic neuropathies, inflammatory optic neuropathies, hereditary optic neuropathies, and optic nerve swelling.</p></sec><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0002"><label>2</label><title>
<styled-content style="fixed-case" toggle="no">OCTA</styled-content> Findings in Ischemic Optic Neuropathies Vascular Aetiology</title><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0003"><label>2.1</label><title>Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (<styled-content style="fixed-case" toggle="no">CRAO</styled-content>)</title><p>CRAO is a visually devastating condition, with 80% of affected individuals having a visual acuity of hand movements or worse on presentation [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0003" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>]. CRAO is diagnosed on clinical examination, with the classic signs of retinal pallor, retinal arterial attenuation, and a cherry red spot manifesting within 15 min of occlusion [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0006" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. However, in those that pose a diagnostic challenge, inner retinal hyperreflectivity, outer retinal hyperreflectivity, and increased parafoveal thickness on OCT cross‐sectional imaging can be reliably demonstrated in the acute phase [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. A rapid diagnosis is crucial, in light of the possible treatment option of thrombolysis in selected CRAO cases presenting within a 4.5 h time window [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0007" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0009" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0010" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0011" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0012" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. Part of the barrier to early diagnosis includes the reliable diagnosis of CRAO, and several emergency departments have tried to institute a non‐mydriatic fundus camera or ocular point of care ultrasound (POCUS) [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0013" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0014" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0015" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0016" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. Fundoscopic findings in CRAO depend on the duration and degree of retinal ischaemia from the arterial occlusion [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0017" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. Fundus changes in the acute phase include a cherry red spot in acute central retinal artery occlusion, box carring, or retinal arterial attenuation.
