For example, Rhodiola exhibits the biphasic shape of the dose–response (hormetic) action in many studies [<xref rid="B63-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>,<xref rid="B88-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">88</xref>,<xref rid="B269-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">269</xref>], but this dual action is insufficiently understood due to the complexity of the stress system, including multiple “players” involved in stress response on various levels of regulation. The typical pattern of dose–response effects of <italic toggle="yes">Rhodiola rosea</italic> extracts was observed on the duration of thiopental-induced sleep in mice, ranging from stimulation at low doses (10 mg/kg), where the sleep period was reduced by 12.5 times, to sedation at high doses (500 mg/kg), where the sleep period was increased 3-fold [<xref rid="B88-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">88</xref>]. Calabrese et al. (2023) reviewed the dose–response relationship of <italic toggle="yes">Rhodiola rosea</italic> extracts and one of its major constituents, salidroside, and evaluated their capacity to induce hormesis/hormetic effects [<xref rid="B269-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">269</xref>]. The authors conclude that the findings indicate that the <italic toggle="yes">Rhodiola rosea</italic> extracts and salidroside commonly induce hormetic dose responses within a broad range of biological models and cell types and across a wide range of endpoints, with particular emphasis on longevity and neuroprotective endpoints [<xref rid="B269-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">269</xref>]. It should be emphasized that the results of available dose–response studies demonstrate that Rhodiola is not toxic in the highest tested doses despite the decrease or lack of positive effect in the highest dose. That is typical for adaptogens and different toxic poisons, which could have beneficial positive effects in low doses (<xref rid="pharmaceuticals-18-00261-f010" ref-type="fig">Figure 10</xref>). Only in a few of the numerous in vitro tests did the Rhodiola extract and salidroside exhibit negative effects in concentrations that are too high, which have no therapeutic significance. It is generally accepted that in vitro studies with concentrations of active compounds beyond the IC50 concentration of 25 µM for pure compounds and the IC50 of 100 µg/mL for the extracts have no therapeutic significance because they could not be implemented in vivo and are not appropriate for further pharmaceutical development [<xref rid="B272-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">272</xref>]. The results of the dose–response effect of Rhodiola studies in rats are confusing: the dose of the maximal positive effect differs 200-fold: 15 mg/kg BW [<xref rid="B51-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>,<xref rid="B273-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">273</xref>,<xref rid="B274-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">274</xref>,<xref rid="B275-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">275</xref>,<xref rid="B276-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">276</xref>] vs. 3000 mg/kg BW [<xref rid="B277-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">277</xref>].</p><p>The term “hormesis”, derived from the Greek word for exciting, was initially defined as a biphasic dose–response curve [<xref rid="B219-pharmaceuticals-18-00261" ref-type="bibr">219</xref>]. Hormesis is a biological phenomenon where a low dose of a potentially harmful stressor, such as a toxin or environmental factor, stimulates a beneficial adaptive response in an organism.
