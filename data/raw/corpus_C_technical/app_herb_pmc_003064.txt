Differences in dominant growth forms reflect vegetation type, resource availability, and cultural adaptation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>].</p><p id="Par173">In Karamara, shrub dominance may also result from ecological disturbance and forest fragmentation, as canopy openings favor shrub proliferation, contrasting with herb-dominated floras in less disturbed sites [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>]. Herbs and shrubs dominate across Africa and Asia due to ecological flexibility, accessibility, and regenerative capacity. Shorter life cycles and ease of propagation make them practical for traditional use, whereas trees and climbers are less accessible and slower to regenerate [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>].</p><p id="Par174">Understanding growth form distribution aids conservation and sustainable use. Identifying heavily exploited growth forms can guide strategies to prevent overharvesting and maintain species diversity, safeguarding the ethnomedicinal heritage of the Somali community and other indigenous populations reliant on forest resources for primary healthcare [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec32"><title>Major human ailments and medicinal plant species used by local people</title><p id="Par175">In the Karamara forest patches, the Somali ethnic community uses 85 medicinal plant species to treat 54 human ailments. The most frequently addressed conditions include wounds, malaria, gastrointestinal disorders, snakebites, eye pain, and toothaches. This pattern aligns with findings from other parts of Ethiopia, where gastrointestinal and dermatological problems are commonly managed through traditional medicine. For instance, studies from the Sheka Zone reported similar plant use for digestive and skin-related conditions, highlighting the enduring role of traditional medicine in Ethiopian healthcare systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>].</p><p id="Par176">The diversity of ailments treated in Karamara is relatively high compared to other Ethiopian studies. For example [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>], documented 48 plant species for 47 ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>], recorded 77 species for 49 ailments, and [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>] reported 78 species for 50 diseases. The higher number of ailments addressed in Karamara reflects both the rich medicinal plant diversity and the extensive ethnomedicinal knowledge of the Somali community.</p><p id="Par177">Similar therapeutic patterns are observed across Africa. In Uganda [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR79">79</xref>], documented plant species used for gastrointestinal disorders and skin diseases, echoing the focus in Karamara. In Nigeria and neighboring countries, MPs are commonly employed to manage wounds, malaria, and digestive ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>], suggesting a shared ethnobotanical heritage and convergent adaptation of plant remedies across African communities.</p><p id="Par178">Comparable trends extend to Asia. In Bangladesh [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR81">81</xref>], recorded numerous plants traditionally used for dermatological disorders, while in China [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR82">82</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR83">83</xref>], reported plants employed against gastrointestinal and respiratory conditions. European studies mirror these patterns; in Spain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>], noted MPs frequently used for gastrointestinal and dermatological conditions. Similarly, in the Americas, indigenous groups have long utilized plants to treat wounds, digestive disorders, and respiratory problems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR84">84</xref>].</p><p id="Par179">The recurring use of plants for common ailments worldwide highlights a universal reliance on traditional remedies, reflecting both empirical effectiveness and the shared biocultural evolution of medicinal knowledge. Preserving such ethnobotanical wisdom is essential for maintaining cultural heritage and for the discovery of novel pharmacologically active compounds.</p><p id="Par180">Within Karamara, the Somali community identifies <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic> as key medicinal species, each used to treat four distinct ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR85">85</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR86">86</xref>].
