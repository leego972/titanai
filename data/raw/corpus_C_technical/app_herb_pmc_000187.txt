For example, Hu, Li, Qin, Zhang, Liu, Zhang, Liu, Jia, Yin, Han, Zhu, Luo and Liu [<xref rid="b0375" ref-type="bibr">77</xref>] reported that application of US (25 kHz, 550 W, 70 °C for 38 min) in the extraction of tea (<italic toggle="yes">Camellia sinensis</italic>) oil increased β-carotene by 38% compared to conventional extraction methods. The authors suggested the increase was due to the breakage of cross-links between these compounds and other macromolecules like polysaccharides and proteins, which release the carotene in the extraction medium. However, high-intensity US application (25 kHz and 600 W at 4 °C for 6 min) significantly degraded (all-E)-astaxanthin carotenoid [<xref rid="b0395" ref-type="bibr">81</xref>]. Additionally, after optimization of US parameters to extract antioxidants from thyme (Thymus vulgaris) and rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis), Munekata, Alcantara, Zugcic, Abdelkebir, Collado, Garcia-Perez, Jambrak, Gavahian, Barba and Lorenzo [<xref rid="b0400" ref-type="bibr">82</xref>] reported that US-assisted extraction at 400 W and 40 °C for 10 min increased the extraction yields of carotenoids compared to conventional extraction (heating under magnetic stirrer method). Also, the authors mentioned that US improved the aqueous extraction of antimicrobial compounds from thyme.</p></sec><sec id="s0055"><label>4.5</label><title>Ultrasound effect on herbal flavonoids</title><p id="p0120">While the formation of free-radicals during US processing is considered a main disadvantage of the technology as it affects the bioactivity of components such as phenols [<xref rid="b0405" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>], chemical modification such as increasing the extent of hydroxylation of flavonoids [<xref rid="b0410" ref-type="bibr">84</xref>] can enhance the antioxidant activity or at least counteract some of the negative effects of free radicals. This led to the fact that optimum US processing conditions where maximum yield and bioactivity could be optimized for various materials. In which, US inhibits the hydrolyzing enzymes (such as α-amylase and α-glucosidase) which affects the total flavonoids yields and their antioxidant functionality [<xref rid="b0325" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>]. For instance, the extraction efficiency of dihydromyricetin yield, which is the main flavonoid in Chinese vine herbal tea (Ampelopsis grossedentata), was increased up to 40% (3% yield) with the increase in US power and time until a certain extent (5.5 min at 240 W), after which, the yield was decreased due to hydrolysis of the compound [<xref rid="b0415" ref-type="bibr">85</xref>]. Similarly, total flavonoids extracted from <italic toggle="yes">Syzygium cumini</italic> seeds using US (22–24 kHz and 44–215 W at 35 °C for 12 min) were increased with the increase in processing time [<xref rid="b0230" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>]. Maximum total flavonoid content was obtained after 12 min of processing at US power of 125 W at 35 °C for 12 min. The yields of gallic acid and catechin exhibited similar trends with 54.5 and 2.2 mg/g after 12 min [<xref rid="b0230" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>]. Collectively, the above information suggests a scope for optimization where maximum bioactives could be obtained before negative effects are caused by heating or free radicals.</p><p id="p0125">Optimum US extraction conditions for total flavonoids from sour jujube seeds (<italic toggle="yes">Caenorhabditis elegans</italic>) were obtained at 404 W and 60 °C for 60.03 min.
