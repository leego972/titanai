Actinic Keratosis</title><p>AK is a precancerous lesion (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="molecules-25-05195-f005">Figure 5</xref>) that occurs on sun-damaged skin. AK lesions are prone to field cancerization and around 0.5–10% may develop into squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) [<xref rid="B40-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>,<xref rid="B41-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>,<xref rid="B42-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>]. Field cancerization was first named by Slaughter in 1953 when studying the epithelium of oral SCC. Slaughter implied that oral SCC arose from multifocal areas of precancerous changes under carcinogenic exposure rather than from key malignant cells. Braakhuis et al. defined field cancerization as a preneoplastic status. Therefore, it does not have the ability of invasion or metastasis [<xref rid="B43-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>]. However, the field may or may not have visible histopathological changes of dysplasia and some are found to have genetic alterations. Molecular studies raised the issue of genetic mutations in the malignant transformation of normal cells [<xref rid="B41-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. The mutation in the gene TP-53 is well documented and presents more in photodamaged skin and head and neck cancer [<xref rid="B41-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. Studies also proposed that it takes more than one genetic alteration to turn normal tissue into subclinical lesions then into clinical or even invasive cancer. These genes include the famous TP-53 and the p-16, antiapoptotic complex Bcl-2 and cyclin D1 genes [<xref rid="B41-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. The field originates from a stem cell that acquires one or more genetic alterations. Sub-clones may originate in the field after the acquisition of new gene alterations and may undergo carcinomatous transformation [<xref rid="B41-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. Thus, field-directed therapy is a means to treat not only clinically visible lesions but also nonvisible subclinical lesions within the field. If a new AK lesion is developed followed by another AK in the same chronically photodamaged skin area, the risk of cancerous change of the third or fourth AK presented in this field is high. Field-directed therapy in photodamaged areas in AK patients [<xref rid="B44-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>] has been widely used in recent years. Imiquimod, Ingenol mebutate, PDT and cryotherapy are considered field-directed therapies.</p><p>The Olsen grading system grades AK into three grades: grade 1, slightly palpable; grade 2, moderately thick; and grade 3, very hyperkeratotic lesions [<xref rid="B45-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>]. dPDT on AK is supported by evidence from randomized studies and established consensus from the Western countries with impressive cosmetic results and few side effects [<xref rid="B28-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>,<xref rid="B42-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>]. dPDT is especially suitable for patients with multiple lesions on large actinic damaged skin, recommended by the International Society for Photodynamic Therapy in Dermatology (ISPTD) [<xref rid="B28-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Multiple randomized trials revealed no statistical difference in treatment response between cPDT and dPDT. The complete remission rate ranged from 70% to 92% [<xref rid="B24-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>,<xref rid="B39-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>,<xref rid="B46-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>]. However, it was recommended that dPDT should be applied to superficial to moderate thick AK (grade 1–2 AK) instead of thick AK (grade 3 AK) [<xref rid="B42-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>,<xref rid="B47-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>]. The subgroup analysis of dPDT-treated AK revealed higher response rates of thin AK (76%) but lower response rates of moderate to thick AK, similar to the results of cPDT [<xref rid="B42-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>,<xref rid="B47-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>]. Perez et al. reported similar efficacy of dPDT and cPDT for grades 1 and 2 AK but revealed inferiority of dPDT to cPDT for grade 3 AK [<xref rid="B48-molecules-25-05195" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>].
