Phytochemicals in Bioprospecting Research</title><p>The search for bioactive secondary metabolites in plants as anticancer agents is gaining increasing interest from researchers as it offers a more promising future in detecting novel compounds rather than the synthetic approach [<xref rid="B15-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>]. Natural products, especially those from plants, have a unique structural diversity, enabling new possibilities in drug research [<xref rid="B16-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. Traditional medicine has been a reliable source for obtaining useful information about plants with medicinal properties [<xref rid="B17-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. Some compounds were discovered by accident, i.e., by random screening of plants, microorganisms, and marine organisms or by bioactivity-guided fractionation [<xref rid="B18-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. Many drug lead structures were discovered through these methods; nevertheless, the precious gifts of nature to humankind still must be used satisfactorily. It is worth noting that among the sources of natural products, plants remain a huge vase of abundant chemical compounds with unprecedented biological activities and mechanistic action. Conversely, only a small portion of the world’s flora has been tested for their potential bioactive compounds [<xref rid="B19-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B20-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>] and surprisingly, drugs derived from plants are particularly low in number, unlike those from other natural product sources [<xref rid="B21-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>].</p><p>There are two basic types of phytochemicals: primary and secondary metabolites. The former class contains the components used for the basic physiological processes in plants, while the latter class is not necessarily used by plants’ physiology but ensures survival in an ecological context. These compounds are known as secondary metabolites, and some have been identified to promote human health and treatment of diseases [<xref rid="B22-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>]. <xref rid="molecules-27-08307-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref> shows different classes of plant bioactive secondary metabolites that are of clinical and pharmacological importance, with examples of newly purified compounds that have been studied for their anticancer potentials [<xref rid="B23-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec3-molecules-27-08307"><title>3. Carcinogenesis and Phytochemicals: Mechanisms of Action and Cellular Targets</title><p>Cancer is a multifactorial disease with diverse etiological factors. The events that lead to cancer arise from the alterations in the genetic constituents of an individual, which may be in the form of mutation, epigenetic alteration, or perhaps the crosstalk between the two processes [<xref rid="B24-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. As illustrated in <xref rid="molecules-27-08307-f002" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>, the conversion of cellular proto-oncogenes into oncogenes or inactivation of tumor suppressor genes (i.e., loss or gain of function mutations) result in carcinogenesis. Similarly, alteration in methylation or acetylation of promoters of certain genes could consequently result in aberrant proteins or lead to over-expression of some facilitating components of cell signaling pathways. Examples of these facilitators include growth factors and their receptors, such as RTKs, small GTPases, kinases, nuclear receptors, developmental signaling pathway components (e.g., Wnt [<xref rid="B25-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>], Hedgehog [<xref rid="B26-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>], Notch [<xref rid="B27-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>], Hippo [<xref rid="B28-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]), nuclear targets of various signaling pathways (e.g., transcription factors, chromatin remodeler, tumor suppressor genes, cell cycle regulators) [<xref rid="B29-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>,<xref rid="B30-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>,<xref rid="B31-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>,<xref rid="B32-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="B33-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>], and lots more [<xref rid="B34-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>,<xref rid="B35-molecules-27-08307" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>].
