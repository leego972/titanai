# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

Similarly, in Spain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR102">102</xref>] and across the Americas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR103">103</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>], leaves and roots dominate traditional preparations, illustrating a universal ethnomedicinal preference for pharmacologically potent and accessible plant parts.</p><p id="Par186">The Somali community in Karamara employs diverse preparation methods. Crushing and homogenizing with water is the most common, producing extracts administered orally as decoctions, infusions, or juices. Species prepared this way include <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. papaya</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. quadrangularis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">L. jamesii</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">M. stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">R. communis</italic>, primarily for gastrointestinal, respiratory, and febrile conditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>].</p><p id="Par187">Most remedies (77.33%) involve single plant species, while 22.66% use mixtures. This predominance of single-species preparations mirrors other Ethiopian studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR105">105</xref>], reflecting confidence in the efficacy of specific taxa.</p><p id="Par188">Topical applications involve crushing, pounding, or grinding plant materials into a paste. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">A. fruticosum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. dubius</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">T. minuta</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. amabile</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">V. sinaiticum</italic> are applied to wounds, skin infections, or insect bites [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>]. Chewing or direct oral consumption is also common, particularly for <italic toggle="yes">A. nilotica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. nubica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. edulis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">D. abyssinica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">M. africana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">O. ficus-indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">P. guajava</italic>, mainly for digestive issues, musculoskeletal pain, and nutritional deficiencies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>].</p><p id="Par189">Decoctions are widely used for species such as <italic toggle="yes">(A) mexicana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">(B) oleracea</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">E. camaldulensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">R. chalepensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">W. somnifera</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Z. spina-christi</italic>, as boiling enhances extraction of bioactive compounds for internal ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>]. Latex, sap, and gum preparations from species like <italic toggle="yes">V. bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">(A) senegal</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">(B) rivae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">E. tirucalli</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">O. ficus-indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Z. mauritiana</italic> are applied topically for wound healing, skin infections, and inflammation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>]. Inhalation therapies, using crushed, boiled, or burned plant parts (e.g., <italic toggle="yes">(C) procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. kelleriana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. adenocaule</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">E. globulus</italic>), target respiratory ailments, reflecting practices common in Asian ethnomedicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>].</p><p id="Par190">Additional methods include mixing extracts with milk (<italic toggle="yes">P. granatum</italic>), tying crushed powders to wounds (<italic toggle="yes">S. jubae</italic>), and chewing plant parts (<italic toggle="yes">A. nubica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">M. azedarach</italic>) to gradually release bioactive compounds. These diverse preparation techniques demonstrate the community’s deep empirical understanding of plant chemistry and therapeutic efficacy.</p><p id="Par191">Medicinal preparations are administered through multiple routes. Oral administration is dominant, mainly for gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, parasitic infections, and fever, using decoctions, infusions, or juices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR109">109</xref>].
