Notable examples here include <bold><italic toggle="yes">ocoko lac</italic></bold> for <italic toggle="yes">Dicoma sessiliflora</italic>, a treatment for urinary infections, in which <bold><italic toggle="yes">ocoko</italic></bold> stands for the name of the plant, while the suffix ‘<bold><italic toggle="yes">lac</italic></bold>’ signifies urine, thus indicating its application.</p><p id="Par73">Perhaps the most pharmacologically significant example is the qualifier ‘<bold><italic toggle="yes">yat</italic></bold>’. Defined by Kokwaro and Johns as “a general term for shrub, tree or medicine” [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>], these three meanings are listed separately by other sources [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>], the latter separating its meanings as referring to a tree (or wood), to medicine or to an herb. In each such case, <bold><italic toggle="yes">yat</italic></bold> is followed by a second noun, often indicating a malady or condition to which the plant can be applied as a remedy, and thus indicative of its primary pharmacological property according to the current or past therapeutic application.</p><p id="Par74">Examples of such nomenclature provided in Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref> include <italic toggle="yes">Aerva lanata</italic>, <bold><italic toggle="yes">yat dobo</italic></bold> (or ‘leprosy medicine’ in leb Acholi); <italic toggle="yes">Pleurolobus gangeticus</italic>, <bold><italic toggle="yes">yat aola</italic></bold> (cough medicine); <italic toggle="yes">Oxalis corniculata</italic>, <bold><italic toggle="yes">yat leny</italic></bold> (heartburn medicine). Another example is <italic toggle="yes">Gloriosa superba</italic>.—<bold><italic toggle="yes">yat ania</italic></bold>, referring to its use in treatment of pneumonia (despite the notoriously extreme colchicine toxicity of the plant), and the tuber of an unidentified creeper called <bold><italic toggle="yes">yat cak</italic></bold> (milk medicine), credited with increasing milk production in humans and in livestock. Although no identification could be made on basis of the specimen provided, the taxonomic description and reported morphology of the plant are consistent with <italic toggle="yes">Dioscorea</italic> L. species, of which several are known to contain estrogenic compounds [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>], and nine are present within the study area [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>]. Estrogen is known to stimulate development of the mammary ducts, and, in association with progesterone, to stimulate proliferation of secretory tissues [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>].</p><p id="Par75">Examples of Ateso plant names which seem to imply their given therapeutic use may include <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia hockii</italic> (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ekisim</italic></bold>), the flowers of which are rubbed on the breasts (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ekisin</italic></bold>) of a mother who has lost her child to stop milk flow; <italic toggle="yes">Pseudocedrela kotschyi</italic> (<bold><italic toggle="yes">eputon</italic></bold>), the root of which is used to treat measles (<bold><italic toggle="yes">epuru</italic></bold>), and <italic toggle="yes">Zanthoxylum chalybeum</italic> (<bold><italic toggle="yes">eusuk</italic></bold>), various parts of which are used as a cough treatment—the word for lung given being <bold><italic toggle="yes">euko</italic></bold> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR79">79</xref>]. By contrast, the name for <italic toggle="yes">Rhynchosia</italic> sp. (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ookot</italic></bold>) is strongly suggestive of blood (<bold><italic toggle="yes">aokot</italic></bold> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>]), but is only cited here as a diarrhea treatment in Teso. This again may imply a former medicinal use no longer in practice within the respondent communities of the study area.</p></sec><sec id="Sec13"><title>Data interpretation in the epidemiological context</title><p id="Par76">From the etic perspective, results are consistent with the documented epidemiology of the study area. Together accounting for over 36% of use reports, the findings around the three major maladies—measles, diarrhea and cough—largely reflect the historical prevalence of these conditions among the respondent communities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR81">81</xref>]. The national measles vaccination coverage rate in 2002 was just 74% [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR82">82</xref>], leaving over a quarter of all Ugandan children unprotected against measles; this number is likely to be considerably higher within the study area, given regional disparities in health-related outcomes and access and availability of treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR83">83</xref>].
