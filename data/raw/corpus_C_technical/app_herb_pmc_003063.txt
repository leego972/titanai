# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

The high representation of Fabaceae and Asteraceae aligns with their prominence in Ethiopian flora [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>], while studies elsewhere emphasize the ecological and medicinal importance of Asteraceae [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>]. This dual ecological and therapeutic value suggests that local plant selection is guided by both availability and proven efficacy.</p><p id="Par167">Comparative studies show convergence and regional variation in medicinal plant use. Across Africa and Asia, Fabaceae and Asteraceae rank among the top medicinal families, reflecting ecological dominance and integration into traditional healthcare [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>]. In Europe, aromatic families such as Asteraceae and Lamiaceae are emphasized [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR66">66</xref>], whereas Latin American inventories highlight Asteraceae, Fabaceae, and Solanaceae, influenced by ecosystem and cultural preferences [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR66">66</xref>]. Thus, medicinal plant use is shaped by both biodiversity and cultural knowledge.</p><p id="Par168">Ecological and methodological factors can influence reported diversity. Habitat type, forest fragmentation, sampling intensity, and inclusion of cultivated species affect species richness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR67">67</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR68">68</xref>]. Studies that include cultivated taxa report higher diversity, while those limited to forest patches often emphasize woody and resinous species, explaining the prominence of Burseraceae in Karamara.</p><p id="Par169">These findings have implications for conservation, pharmacology, and public health. Dominant families such as Fabaceae, Asteraceae, Solanaceae, Lamiaceae, and Euphorbiaceae are promising targets for phytochemical research. Conservation strategies should prioritize ecologically restricted and culturally important taxa, particularly resinous Burseraceae species. Standardized ethnopharmacological protocols can improve study comparability, strengthen sustainable utilization, and support evidence-based integration of MPs into local healthcare systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>].</p><p id="Par170">Policy recognition of these plant families can inform environmental management strategies that protect biodiversity and ecosystem services. Community-based cultivation, habitat restoration, and regulated harvesting can maintain access to culturally relevant medicines while preserving ecological integrity. Linking ethnobotanical knowledge with policy and management fosters resilient healthcare systems, biodiversity conservation, and cultural heritage preservation in semi-arid forests.</p><p id="Par171">Analysis of growth forms showed shrubs were most dominant, followed by trees, herbs, and climbers, reflecting patterns in other Ethiopian studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>]. For example, in Addi Arkay District, shrubs accounted for 37.5% of medicinal species, herbs 36.6%, trees 17%, and climbers 9% [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>], while in Sheka Zone, herbs were most prevalent [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>]. Shrubs and herbs are accessible, abundant, fast-growing, and species-rich, making them practical for frequent use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>].</p><p id="Par172">Similar trends occur beyond Ethiopia. In Sulaymaniyah Province, Iraq, herbs made up 75% of medicinal species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>], while in Mana Angetu District [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>] and Tigray [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>], shrubs dominated. Continental comparisons show herbs are commonly used in Uganda (41% of species) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>], shrubs and herbs in South China [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>], and herbaceous/shrubby species in Latin America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR66">66</xref>]. Differences in dominant growth forms reflect vegetation type, resource availability, and cultural adaptation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>].</p><p id="Par173">In Karamara, shrub dominance may also result from ecological disturbance and forest fragmentation, as canopy openings favor shrub proliferation, contrasting with herb-dominated floras in less disturbed sites [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>].
