These findings suggest that PBMT may have a multifaceted effect on glycaemic control. However, standardisation of treatment protocols is required before these results can be applied to clinical settings.</p><p>Although limited, evidence from small-scale human studies has also suggested potential benefits. Clinical investigations have reported modest reductions in fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c levels in patients with type 2 diabetes following PBMT protocols [<xref rid="B226-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">226</xref>,<xref rid="B231-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">231</xref>]. Improvements in peripheral insulin sensitivity and endothelial function have also been observed, suggesting that PBMT could complement standard pharmacological therapy by simultaneously targeting metabolic and vascular mechanisms [<xref rid="B227-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">227</xref>]. However, as these clinical trials vary in design with respect to wavelength, energy density, irradiation site, and treatment duration, cross-study comparisons are difficult [<xref rid="B231-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">231</xref>]. Furthermore, differences in patient characteristics, such as age, disease duration, and comorbidities, may influence treatment outcomes and should be considered in future studies.</p><p>Although the preliminary data are promising, the current evidence base is insufficient to establish PBMT as the standard therapy for glycaemic control. Several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, most studies are preclinical or involve small patient cohorts, which limits generalisability [<xref rid="B226-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">226</xref>]. Secondly, heterogeneity in laser parameters and treatment protocols contributes to conflicting outcomes across studies. For instance, while some trials report significant reductions in fasting glucose or HbA1c levels, others find no statistically significant changes [<xref rid="B231-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">231</xref>]. This inconsistency highlights the need for harmonised methodologies and consensus on therapeutic dosing guidelines.</p><p>In addition, the long-term safety and sustainability of the glycaemic effects of PBMT remain poorly understood. Most available studies assess outcomes over periods ranging from weeks to months, and there is limited data on the effects of long-term use in diabetic populations [<xref rid="B232-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">232</xref>,<xref rid="B233-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">233</xref>]. Another unresolved issue is the determination of the optimal irradiation parameters, including wavelength, energy density, frequency, and target tissue, to maximise therapeutic efficacy while minimising risks [<xref rid="B73-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">73</xref>]. Finally, the variability in clinical results may be due to placebo effects and difficulties in blinding patients to PBMT interventions [<xref rid="B73-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">73</xref>]. To address these challenges, future trials should incorporate rigorous blinding procedures, placebo controls, and stratified patient selection to ensure robust and reproducible outcomes.</p><p>Overall, however, PBMT represents a novel non-invasive strategy with the potential to enhance insulin sensitivity and improve glycaemic regulation in preclinical models and small-scale human studies [<xref rid="B226-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">226</xref>]. By modulating mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, inflammation, and vascular responses, laser therapy may offer multifactorial benefits in diabetes management [<xref rid="B233-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">233</xref>]. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of the available studies as well as their limited sample sizes and conflicting results emphasise the necessity for large-scale standardised clinical trials to confirm efficacy, establish optimal treatment protocols, and ascertain long-term safety [<xref rid="B73-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">73</xref>,<xref rid="B232-ijms-27-02078" ref-type="bibr">232</xref>]. Until such evidence is available, PBMT should be considered a promising experimental adjunctive intervention in glycaemic control management to be used within research settings or alongside established therapies.</p></sec><sec><title>10.
