Phytochemical compounds effective in treating diarrhea include polyphenol catechins and tannins [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR102">102</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR103">103</xref>] and the flavonol quercetin [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>], specifically through increased colonic water and electrolyte reabsorption [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR103">103</xref>].</p><p id="Par81">Cough was ranked at second in terms of use reports in all for regions, including Teso with 16 use reports. Persistent cough in Uganda has been linked to household air pollution including exposure to indoor smoke from cooking and lighting [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR105">105</xref>], and to infection by human pathogens including the influenza virus, particularly during the rainy season [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>] and the Gram-negative, aerobic, pathogenic, encapsulated coccobacillus <italic toggle="yes">Bordetella pertussis</italic>, responsible for an estimated 400,000 annual deaths, mostly of infants in developing countries [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR99">99</xref>]. Several other bacteria—most notably the Gram-positive <italic toggle="yes">Streptococcus pneumoniae</italic> but also the Gram-negative <italic toggle="yes">Mycoplasma pneumoniae</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Chlamydophila pneumoniae</italic>—are implicated in infectious pneumonia, the leading cause of mortality among children under 5 in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for 12.8% of all such deaths [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR98">98</xref>]. In Uganda, Acute Respiratory Infections (ARIs) remain the leading cause of childhood morbidity and mortality among under-five children [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>].</p><p id="Par82">Phytochemical compounds with documented therapeutic activity in the treatment of cough include polysaccharides, anthraquinones and their derivatives. An in vivo study of cats [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>] found cough suppressive activity in polysaccharide compounds isolated from nine plant species. Another study found that anthraquinone derivatives ameliorate lung inflammatory response, an effect recently reviewed with particular reference to Aloe (Asphodelaceae), Senna (Fabaceae) and Rheum L. (Polygonaceae) species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].</p><p id="Par83">Of the taxa cited for treatment of cough, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe volkensii</italic> is conspicuously abundant in polysaccharides [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>], with a phytochemical profile including anthraquinones and pre-anthraquinones [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>], as well as alkaloids, saponins, glycosides, flavonoids, and tannins [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>] [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>], noting that antioxidant compounds have been associated with a protective effect in cough suppression [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR115">115</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>].</p><p id="Par84">Other maladies and conditions for which fewer use reports were obtained and species cited are nonetheless highly significant in terms of their mortality or morbidity, notably including snakebite—responsible for an estimated 32,000 annual deaths across sub-Saharan Africa (a quarter of the global total), leaving as many as 100,000 survivors with permanent physical disabilities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR117">117</xref>]. The health burden on survivors of snakebite may involve hemorrhage, tetanus, contractures (debilitating stiffening of muscle or connective tissue), myonecrosis (life-threatening muscle infection), scarring, and tissue inflammation that result from the bites [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR118">118</xref>], resulting in 5000–15,000 amputations annually [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>].</p><p id="Par85">Horse-derived antivenin sera comprise the sole medical treatment for snakebite—but these are highly perishable, are unable to prevent local tissue damage, can induce adverse reactions including anaphylactic shock, and are scarce and unaffordable in the rural areas where nearly all envenomation occurs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR118">118</xref>]. A 2018 study decried a compound global "crisis" of poor antivenom quality, availability and reliability of supply [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR120">120</xref>], and other authors have questioned the clinical effectiveness of available products within the Africa region in particular, as many such antivenin products were developed specifically to treat envenomation by Asian snake species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR117">117</xref>]. In northern Uganda, where more than a third of snakebite victims are younger than 18 years, antivenom supplies are insufficient even at hospitals for optimum treatment of envenomation according to WHO guidelines [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR121">121</xref>].
