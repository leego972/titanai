# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

rowspan="1">2</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">3</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">1</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">2</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">1</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">1</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">1</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">11</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">5th</td></tr></tbody></table><table-wrap-foot><p>R1–R7 = Respondents; higher scores indicate higher preference</p></table-wrap-foot></table-wrap> </p></sec><sec id="Sec23"><title>Direct matrix ranking and multiple uses of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par167">The direct matrix ranking conducted in Sheikh District highlights the multifunctional significance of MPs within local communities. Beyond their therapeutic roles, these species are integral to daily livelihood practices, including firewood, fodder, charcoal production, construction, fencing, and tool making. This dual utility underscores the deep integration of ethnobotanical knowledge with community survival strategies, where a plant’s value is assessed not only by its medicinal efficacy but also by its contribution to food security, energy needs, and economic resilience [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR196">196</xref>].</p><p id="Par168">Among the seven species evaluated, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic> ranked highest, demonstrating remarkable versatility across all six use categories (Table <xref rid="Tab7" ref-type="table">7</xref>). This pattern aligns with observations from Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where multipurpose <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia</italic> species are highly valued for their durability, biomass, and accessibility, supporting both healthcare and subsistence needs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR96">96</xref>]. Similarly, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia nilotica</italic> ranked second and third, reflecting their critical role in local livelihoods and the significant anthropogenic pressure placed upon them through combined medicinal and non-medicinal uses.</p><p id="Par169">The prominence of these multipurpose species underscores their centrality to community life, yet intensive utilization raises concerns regarding overexploitation. Evidence from Nigeria and other East African countries indicates that plants heavily relied upon for multiple purposes are at higher risk of depletion, especially when harvested simultaneously for fuel, fodder, timber, and medicinal uses [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR88">88</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR130">130</xref>]. The trends observed in Sheikh District mirror these global patterns, emphasizing the need for proactive conservation strategies that balance human needs with ecological sustainability.</p><p id="Par170">Species with lower total scores, such as <italic toggle="yes">Maytenus undata</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>, are primarily used for medicinal purposes, with limited non-medicinal applications. While immediate overexploitation risks may be lower, these species remain culturally and pharmacologically important, warranting targeted conservation interventions to ensure continued availability for therapeutic use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR176">176</xref>]. Likewise, <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Terminalia prunioides</italic> exhibit moderate multifunctional use, reflecting selective harvesting influenced by local preferences, accessibility, and ecological distribution.</p><p id="Par171">The direct matrix ranking approach provides a practical framework for understanding how communities assign relative importance to MPs, integrating both utilitarian and healthcare considerations. By quantifying local perceptions and use intensity, this method identifies species at high risk of overharvesting and informs priorities for sustainable management. Globally, similar approaches from India and Pakistan to Europe and Australia have shown that multipurpose plants face heightened ecological pressures, emphasizing the need for conservation strategies that combine local knowledge with sustainable harvesting, cultivation, and stewardship [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR152">152</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR196">196</xref>].</p><p id="Par172">In conclusion, the direct matrix ranking in Sheikh District demonstrates that <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia nilotica</italic> are key species for both medicinal and livelihood practices.
