Comparative studies from Ethiopia and Tanzania also highlight the use of <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia</italic> species as traditional remedies for diarrhea and other gastrointestinal ailments, revealing a regional convergence in recognizing legumes and thorny shrubs as effective antidiarrheal agents [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR193">193</xref>]. Other frequently cited species, including <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Citrullus colocynthis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Hydnora cornii</italic>, exemplify multifunctional MPs valued not only for gastrointestinal relief but also for broader therapeutic applications, such as antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR194">194</xref>].</p><p id="Par162">The application of preference ranking and direct matrix ranking provides a structured means to understand community priorities in medicinal plant use. Despite eight reported antidiarrheal species, five were consistently favored, suggesting that local knowledge is selective and grounded in practical experience. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Lippia carviodora</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic> were moderately preferred, whereas <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia seyal</italic> ranked lowest (Table <xref rid="Tab6" ref-type="table">6</xref>). This differentiation likely reflects factors such as effectiveness, seasonal availability, ease of preparation, cultural familiarity, and plant versatility, consistent with ethnobotanical patterns observed in Nigeria, Pakistan, and other regions with rich indigenous medicinal traditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR68">68</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR136">136</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR195">195</xref>].</p><p id="Par163">High preference scores indicate both cultural prominence and potential pharmacological relevance. Species like <italic toggle="yes">Citrus × limon</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic> were repeatedly cited across informants, reflecting perceived therapeutic consistency and reliability. Comparative studies from East Africa, Asia, and other global contexts suggest that frequently cited plants often possess reproducible bioactivity and are more likely to be integrated into both traditional and modern medicinal frameworks [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>].</p><p id="Par164">From a conservation perspective, these preference patterns have significant implications. High-demand species may face overharvesting, especially when collected from wild populations. Similar ecological concerns have been documented in Ethiopia, Cameroon, and Tanzania, emphasizing the importance of integrating community knowledge with sustainable harvesting practices to preserve both biodiversity and the healthcare value of MPs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>]. Prioritizing commonly used species in conservation planning ensures the continuity of indigenous healthcare practices while mitigating risks to plant populations.</p><p id="Par165">In summary, preference ranking in Sheikh District reveals that local communities selectively prioritize MPs for diarrhea based on perceived effectiveness, accessibility, and multifunctionality. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Citrus × limon</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, and other highly ranked plants represent both cultural keystones and candidates for pharmacological validation. These findings highlight the intersection of traditional knowledge, sustainable resource use, and scientific research, echoing patterns observed across Africa, Asia, and globally, and provide a foundation for integrating indigenous practices into broader health and conservation strategies.</p><p id="Par166">
