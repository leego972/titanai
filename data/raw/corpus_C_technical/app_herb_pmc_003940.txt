Despite the noise harmful effect on all body systems, differential effect of immune responsiveness in animals as well as in humans is still under investigation. It may be because of the involvement of hormones secreted via hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic adrenal medullary axis related with the limbic system in response to stress <xref rid="bib1" ref-type="bibr">[1]</xref>. Third stress axis, which involves neuropeptides, neurotrophins, and the sensory neuropeptide substance P activates the neurokinin 1 receptor and is generally described to act locally as a pro-inflammatory way in close contact between substance P containing peptidergic nerve fibers with mast cells and subsequent neurogenic inflammation <xref rid="bib2" ref-type="bibr">[2]</xref>. The key mechanisms involved in transformed immune competence and neuronal plasticity after stress experience could be due to the result of HPA axis and sympathetic adrenal-medullary axis; it generates Th2 bias on one hand and local pro-inflammatory NNA activation on the other hand <xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr">[3]</xref>, <xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr">[4]</xref>. Noise being an environmental stressor and immunotoxin <xref rid="bib5" ref-type="bibr">[5]</xref> cannot be avoided, instead attenuatation or minimizing the exposure to environmental noise can be considered. For effective immune protection the recruitment of leukocytes in the target area from circulation or lymphoid organs is needed. The leukocyte trafficking and infiltration in response to the antigen were intermediated by the cytokines of TH1 subsets (IL-2, IFN-γ and TNF-α) and TH2 subsets of IL-4 <xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr">[6]</xref>. In acute stress, enhancement of cell-mediated immunity <xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">[7]</xref> was observed whereas, chronic stress depleteted the immune system <xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr">[8]</xref>. Acute stress has been reported to increase both cortisol and levels of plasma cytokines <xref rid="bib9" ref-type="bibr">[9]</xref>. Even perceived stress is associated with elevated maternal serum pro-inflammatory cytokines, lower anti-inflammatory cytokines, and exaggerated production of the pro-inflammatory cytokines by lymphocytes stimulated <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic>

There is evidence which shows that cytokine HPA axis circuit plays a relevant role in controlling excessive inflammatory reactions and the non-specific expansion of immune cells with no or low affinity for the antigen that triggers an immune response <xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">[11]</xref>. Further, stressful conditions affect the body by the excessive free radical production via increase in glucocorticoids <xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr">[12]</xref>. Free radicals act continuously like a chain reaction until the unpaired electron is paired or blocked. Studies show that there is an inverse relationship between the dietary intake of antioxidants from foods and incidence of human diseases. The oxidative stress also reported to stimulate the adaptive immunity that results in pathogenesis of the disease <xref rid="bib13" ref-type="bibr">[13]</xref>. Hence it is very important to search for plants as a source of antioxidants because they have few side effects and are more effective because of their synergistic effects. <italic toggle="yes">Scoparia dulcis</italic> (SD) is a herb widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions. <italic toggle="yes">S. dulcis</italic> is a species of flowering plant belonging to the Family Plantaginaceae. It is an erect annual herb, producing white flowers that grows to a height of 0.5 m with various indigenous tribes in the rain-forest zone are well-documented <xref rid="bib14" ref-type="bibr">[14]</xref>. Aqueous extracts of <italic toggle="yes">S. dulcis</italic> possess important hepatoprotective and antioxidant activity <xref rid="bib15" ref-type="bibr">[15]</xref>; traditional practitioners have claimed that the <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> antioxidant activity supports the therapeutic effects <xref rid="bib16" ref-type="bibr">[16]</xref>. Phytochemical screening of <italic toggle="yes">S. dulcis</italic> revealed that it is rich in flavonoids and terpenes that are believed to be responsible for its pharmacological action <xref rid="bib17" ref-type="bibr">[17]</xref>. It has been used for blood cleansing in childbirth and acts as a general tonic <xref rid="bib18" ref-type="bibr">[18]</xref>. <italic toggle="yes">S. dulcis</italic> plants have been traditionally used as remedies for stomach trouble <xref rid="bib19" ref-type="bibr">[19]</xref>. The indigenous tribes in Nicaragua use a hot water infusion or decoction of the leaves or the whole plant for stomach pain, menstrual disorder, as an aid in child birth, as a blood purifier, for insect bites, fever, heart problems, liver and stomach disorders, malaria, sexually transmitted diseases and as a general tonic <xref rid="bib20" ref-type="bibr">[20]</xref>.
