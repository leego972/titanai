Systems such as these can be brought to patients, rather than having to bring patients to instruments, which overcomes a significant hurdle that often exists in the delivery of more advanced healthcare.</p></sec><sec id="sec7"><label>7</label><title>Stage 4: Future Point-of-Care Devices</title><p>Handheld OCT systems have demonstrated comprehensive clinical utility for the early diagnosis of infection and disease. Unfortunately, no fully developed Stage 4 OCT systems (pocket-sized, self-contained) exist to date. Researchers are actively working to reduce the size and cost of OCT systems by considering alternate detection methods<xref rid="r198" ref-type="bibr"><sup>198</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r199" ref-type="bibr"><sup>199</sup></xref> and light sources,<xref rid="r200" ref-type="bibr"><sup>200</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r201" ref-type="bibr"><sup>201</sup></xref> as well as exploring costs and tradeoffs among various OCT platforms. For specific clinical imaging applications, it may be more practical to reduce the performance requirements of the OCT system to save costs and reduce system complexity and size, while still providing the needed data for clinical decision making.</p><p>The original configuration for OCT systems was named time-domain OCT (TD-OCT), given its detection scheme. Researchers have continued system development since inception, pushing the limits of these TD-OCT systems.<xref rid="r202" ref-type="bibr"><sup>202</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r203" ref-type="bibr"><sup>203</sup></xref> However, TD-OCT systems have been used less frequently in the last decade because of the improved imaging speed and SNR afforded by Fourier-domain OCT (FD-OCT) systems.<xref rid="r35" ref-type="bibr"><sup>35</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r204" ref-type="bibr"><sup>204</sup></xref> One important advantage of TD-OCT systems, however, is their potential cost advantage over FD-OCT systems, as they do not require expensive spectrometers or fast swept laser sources.</p><p>Recently, an integrated handheld LCI system has been developed and demonstrated for <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> ear imaging,<xref rid="r205" ref-type="bibr"><sup>205</sup></xref> based on the principle of linear-OCT.<xref rid="r206" ref-type="bibr"><sup>206</sup></xref> The overall cost to develop this system was significantly lower than portable cart-based FD-OCT systems, roughly by a factor of four.<xref rid="r81" ref-type="bibr"><sup>81</sup></xref> Other than the light source, all components needed for this initial system design were housed in a 3-D-printed handheld probe, as shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="f29">Fig. 29</xref>.
