Medical immunity, international law and just war theory.

Allhoff F(1), Potts K(2).

Author information:
(1)Department of Philosophy, Western Michigan University, Kalamazoo, Michigan, 
USA.
(2)University of Michigan Law School, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA.

Under customary international law, the First Geneva Convention and Additional 
Protocol I, medical personnel are protected against intentional attack. In § 1 
of this paper, we survey these legal norms and situate them within the broader 
international humanitarian law framework. In § 2, we explore the historical and 
philosophical basis of medical immunity, both of which have been underexplored 
in the academic literature. In § 3, we analyse these norms as applied to an 
attack in Afghanistan (2015) by the United States; the United States was 
attempting to target a Taliban command-and-control centre but inadvertently 
destroyed a Médecins Sans Frontières hospital instead, killing 42 people. In § 
4, we consider forfeiture of medical immunity and, more sceptically, whether 
supreme emergency could justify infringement of non-forfeited protected status.
