This is a previously unstudied area and thus provides new insights in the field.</p></sec><sec id="Sec2"><title>Methods</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Description of the study area</title><p id="Par9">The study was conducted in the city of Heredia, located in the Central Valley, which is part of the greater urban area of Costa Rica, called the <italic toggle="yes">Gran Área Metropolitana Region</italic> (GAM). Heredia county has a total population of 19,138 inhabitants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>] in an area of just three km<sup>2</sup> and has a population density of 6379.33 inhabitants per km<sup>2</sup> which makes this the third largest city in Costa Rica, after the capital San José and Alajuela. With respect to the ethnic background of the inhabitants, most identify as white or <italic toggle="yes">mestizo</italic> (of mixed European and Amerindian heritage) in the population census of 2011 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>]. Heredia is a popular destination for economic migration within the country, as well as from the neighboring country Nicaragua [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR52">52</xref>]. With respect to the climate, the average annual rainfall is 2374.3 mm. The wettest months are September and October with average amounts of 410.88 mm and 424.6 mm, respectively [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>]. A more detailed ecological description of the area can be found in [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Selection of field sites</title><p id="Par10">Findings on the possibility of a socioeconomic gradient in biodiversity and ethnobotanical plant knowledge tend to differ across the literature [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>]. Some studies have shown that more affluent urban neighborhoods tend to have a higher species richness, yet also show that these species are more often non-native, purely ornamental species, while garden owners from less affluent areas often cultivate more utilitarian, native species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>] to provide food and medicines. A recent meta-analysis by Kuras and colleagues [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>] offered a more nuanced interpretation to this dichotomous view. To avoid bias and to reach maximum variation and address possible intracultural variation in field sites and possible ethnobotanical practices, three types of socioeconomically different neighborhoods in Heredia were selected for the recruitment of urban domestic garden owners (see Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>). We selected a mixed area characterized by commercial activities and housing (i.e. Central Heredia, here further referred to as UH), a residential area with a higher socioeconomic status (i.e. Maria Auxiliadora further referred to as MA) and a welfare housing development area (i.e. Bernardo Benavides further abbreviated as BB), as described in [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>].<fig id="Fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>Location of the three neighborhoods where the urban private gardens were visited in Heredia, Costa Rica</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="MO1" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="13002_2022_505_Fig1_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par11">In the next step, gardens were identified through a stratified, random sample using Google Earth maps and the ArcGis software. The above information was later corroborated using a GPS to record the position of each garden. Subsequently, 10% of the total sample was selected for each category using SPSS. Each of the thus selected garden owners was contacted and invited to participate after providing information about the purpose of the research. If they did not agree to participate, another number from the generated random table was selected in order to complete the designated number of gardens devised for each of the three neighborhoods.</p></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>Characteristics of the gardens</title><p id="Par12">We defined domestic or home gardens as green areas (including backyards) that are located on the perimeter of a private property and contain plants that are grown in soil (and not in plant pots). This research was conducted only in urban domestic gardens whose owners lived in a home on the property, but did not take into account allotments or green areas of apartments or condominiums [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>].</p><p id="Par13">Prior visits to garden owners were made to obtain oral consent for participation.
