Other indicators included body temperature, swelling, edema, coughing, bleeding, diarrhea, vomiting, presence of parasites, and sores, allowing practitioners to prescribe appropriate treatments and adjust dosages according to the duration and intensity of the illness.</p><p id="Par108">Herbal remedies were used with or without additives, depending on the preparation and desired effect. Most plants were administered without additional ingredients. However, certain remedies incorporated water, milk, coffee, honey, or meat to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Cold water was the most common solvent, used for homogenizing, preparing decoctions, or drinking, as seen with species such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Croton macrostachyus</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic>. Milk was occasionally employed, for instance with <italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic>, particularly for spiritual ailments.</p><p id="Par109">Topical applications frequently involved plant oils, juices, or latex, directly applied to affected areas, as observed with <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Jasminum grandiflorum</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Boscia angustifolia</italic>. Additionally, some species, including <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cleome kelleriana</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus globulus</italic>, were burned or heated to produce smoke for inhalation, primarily for respiratory conditions, spiritual purposes, or pain relief.</p><p id="Par110">Traditional healers also emphasized the use of antidotes to mitigate potential adverse effects of medicinal preparations, reflecting a sophisticated understanding of safety and efficacy in local ethnomedicine.</p></sec><sec id="Sec19"><title>Marketability of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par111">MPs in the Karamara forest patches demonstrate substantial market potential, reflecting both their therapeutic importance and cultural acceptance. Informants reported that numerous species are actively traded in local markets. Commonly sold MPs include <italic toggle="yes">Senna italica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus globulus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Pistacia aethiopica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Crotalaria jijigensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Lippia savoryi</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Clerodendrum myricoides</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Dodonaea angustifolia</italic>. Other species, such as <italic toggle="yes">Zingiber officinale</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Capsicum annuum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Carica papaya</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Dovyalis abyssinica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ruta chalepensis</italic>, are marketed primarily for their food and nutritional value, highlighting the multifunctional utility of local plants.</p><p id="Par112">Certain species <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic> showed high citation frequencies, indicating both frequent use and strong local demand. These species are prized for treating common ailments, including hemorrhoids, malaria, cough, common cold, and stomachache, making them commercially viable for traditional healers and small-scale herbal traders.</p><p id="Par113">The marketability of plants is influenced by the plant parts used and preparation methods. Leaves, roots, latex, and fruits are the most frequently traded materials, commonly processed through crushing, decoction, infusion, or extraction. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Cucumis prophetarum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Cucumis pustulatus</italic> are sold as dried or powdered preparations for wound and hemorrhoid treatment, facilitating storage and transport. Similarly, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Ficus bussei</italic> are marketed either for topical application or direct chewing, demonstrating adaptability for both raw and processed forms.</p><p id="Par114">Marketable species are predominantly trees and shrubs, owing to their larger biomass and year-round availability. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Acacia nilotica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Boswellia rivae</italic> hold particular promise as sustainable economic resources. The potential for commercialization is also influenced by ease of cultivation and regeneration. Fast-growing herbs with high turnover, including <italic toggle="yes">Argemone mexicana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Bidens pilosa</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Lippia savoryi</italic>, provide sustainable trade options, whereas slower-growing or seasonally available species, such as <italic toggle="yes">Acacia senegal</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, require regulated harvesting to maintain ecological balance and continuous market supply.</p></sec><sec id="Sec20"><title>Informant consensus factor (ICF) and fidelity level (FL) of medicinal plants used for human ailments</title><p id="Par115">The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) was employed to explore patterns of agreement among local informants regarding the use of medicinal plants for different categories of human ailments.
