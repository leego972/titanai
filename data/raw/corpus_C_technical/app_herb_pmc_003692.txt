As many as 74 women replied that they used the medicinal plants until they felt better, without specifics about their treatment period. The treatment periods depended on the specific herb used and the condition treated. Different parts of the plants were used depending on the perceived need. The use of bundles (leaves and stem bark together) was most common (60.7 %), followed by leaves only (30.8 %), fruits (8.1 %) and stem bark (5.6 %). The majority of preparations were in form of decoctions (76.6 %).</p></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>The most commonly used plants</title><p>Of all the incidents of medicinal plant use, 162 out of 369 (43.9 %) happened during the first trimester; <italic toggle="yes">Lippia chevalieri</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 67) and <italic toggle="yes">Combretum micranthum</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 50) were most commonly used. Considering all trimesters together, <italic toggle="yes">Lippia chevalieri</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 116) and <italic toggle="yes">Combretum micranthum</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 83) were, by far, the most commonly used medicinal plants (Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>). In Africa, but also in other parts of the world, the leaves from <italic toggle="yes">Lippia</italic> spp. have traditionally been used to make aromatic teas. In rural areas in West Africa, the tea is typically drunk after a long working day to relax and to improve sleep, while in the urban areas the tea is often taken in the morning to relieve stress for the coming day [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>]. Neither the constituents of <italic toggle="yes">L. chevalieri</italic> nor any biological activities, have been thoroughly investigated. It is therefore impossible to make any predictions about possible effects of <italic toggle="yes">L. chevalieri</italic> on a pregnant woman or the fetus. Leaves from <italic toggle="yes">Combretum micranthum</italic> are used in West Africa as a general remedy for the treatment of different kinds of diseases. One of the local names “Kinkeliba” has become a synonym for “medicine” in several languages [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec6"><title>Medicinal plants used against malaria</title><p>Symptoms of malaria were frequently cited by the women to be treated with medicinal plants (Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>). <italic toggle="yes">C. micranthum</italic> was the plant most commonly mentioned (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 48). Several other plants were also reported used for this indication; <italic toggle="yes">Stylosanthes erecta</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 12), <italic toggle="yes">Mitragyna inermis</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 12), <italic toggle="yes">Opilia amentacea</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 7), <italic toggle="yes">Vepris heterophylla</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 6), <italic toggle="yes">Trichilia emetica</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 5), <italic toggle="yes">Combretum glutinosum</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3) and <italic toggle="yes">Parkia biglobosa</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3).
