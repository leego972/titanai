Feliks [6:200] opines: “It is common to identify <italic toggle="yes">La</italic>’<italic toggle="yes">ana</italic> as plants from the genus <italic toggle="yes">Artemisia</italic> which contain bitter juice.” Zohary [7:184] identified two citations (Jeremiah 23:15 and Amos 5:7 as <italic toggle="yes">Artemisia herba-alba</italic> Asso<italic toggle="yes">,</italic> but “<italic toggle="yes">La’ana</italic>” appears in at least six more citations (Deuteronomy 29:18, Job 30:4, Proverbs 5:4, Lamentations 3:15 and 19, Hosea 10:4, Amos 6:12). The wormwood mentioned in Revelations 8:11 appears as “<italic toggle="yes">Apsinthos</italic>” in the Greek version. Padosch et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">133</xref>] commented: “The Greek equivalent to “<italic toggle="yes">Apsinthos</italic>” is used as a name for a star that fell into the waters and turned them bitter. The Greek word “<italic toggle="yes">Apsinthion</italic>”—undrinkable—is most probably the ancestor of the word “absinthe.” The Talmud (Abodah Zara 30a) mentions preparing a special “Apsintin wine,” which is still produced today [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">133</xref>]. Thus, we prefer to treat “<italic toggle="yes">La’ana</italic>” as <italic toggle="yes">Artemisia</italic> spp., especially <italic toggle="yes">A. herba alba</italic>, which is known as a common medicinal plant in the Middle East and North Africa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR134">134</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR136">136</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">A. absinthium</italic> L. is also well known as a medicinal plant [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR138">139</xref>] and the wide use of <italic toggle="yes">A. absinthium</italic> in Egypt and Mesopotamia (Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>). <italic toggle="yes">B. aegyptiaca</italic> seems confined to rare oases [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR136">137</xref>] and <italic toggle="yes">E. elaterium</italic> is rarely mentioned in ancient sources from the Holy Land [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Conclusions</title><p id="Par42">All our suggested BMPs are known as such also in Ancient Egypt and/or Mesopotamia (Tables <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>–<xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>). Explicit evidence for use of medicinal plants is very rare in the Bible as well as in the Jewish post-Biblical writings. The comparison to adjacent ancient civilizations (in time and space) enables us to reconstruct the suggested list of BMP’s. Examination of our list shows that all the plants in our suggested list are in continuous medicinal use in the Middle East down generations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">138</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR138">139</xref>] and are used in the Holy Land today [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">138</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR140">141</xref>].</p><p id="Par43">Shakya [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR141">142</xref>] published a review, “Medicinal plants: future source of new drugs.” His “top 25 bioactive compounds of medicinal plants” include <italic toggle="yes">Ricinus communis, Piper nigrum,</italic> Aloe vera, <italic toggle="yes">Nigella sativa, Artemisia absinthium</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic>.
