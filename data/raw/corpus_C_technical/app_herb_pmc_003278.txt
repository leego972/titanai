We reduced this list to 73 popular species that were independently reported by at least three survey participants (Additional files <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">1</xref> and <xref rid="MOESM2" ref-type="media">2</xref>). Included in this list are food plants which are species primarily used for consumption and/or are common in either Haitian or Dominican cuisine and have a secondary role as medicines. These include vegetables, spices, Caribbean-specific staple foods, fruits, and condiments, such as <italic toggle="yes">Cajanus cajan</italic> (L.) Huth (<italic toggle="yes">pwa kongo</italic>, pigeon pea), <italic toggle="yes">Chamissoa altissima</italic> (Jacq.) Kunth (<italic toggle="yes">lyann panye</italic>, false chaff flower), <italic toggle="yes">Mangifera indica</italic> L. (mango), and <italic toggle="yes">Syzygium aromaticum</italic> (L.) Merr. &amp; L.M.Perry (<italic toggle="yes">jiwòf</italic>, clove), and excludes others, such as <italic toggle="yes">Cymbopogon citratus</italic> (DC.) Stapf (<italic toggle="yes">sitwonel</italic>, lemongrass) which holds significance in Asian cuisine but is not a focal element in Caribbean cooking. The latter are called here non-food medicines (hereafter “non-foods”).</p><p id="Par27">The majority of popular plants for women’s health in the NYC Haitian community were not native to Haiti (82%). More than half of the popular plant species in the Haitian community were also food plants (60%), such as <italic toggle="yes">Abelmoschus esculentus</italic> (L.) Moench (<italic toggle="yes">kalalou</italic>, okra), <italic toggle="yes">Beta vulgaris</italic> L. (<italic toggle="yes">bètrav</italic>, beets), <italic toggle="yes">Daucus carota</italic> L. (<italic toggle="yes">kawòt</italic>, carrots), or <italic toggle="yes">Spinacia oleracea</italic> L. (<italic toggle="yes">epina</italic>, spinach). These are primarily native to Eurasia (including North Africa, Asia, and Europe) and South and Central America (Fig. <xref rid="Fig3" ref-type="fig">3</xref>). Of the popularly used taxa native to Haiti, the majority consist of non-food plants employed only for medicinal purposes (83%).<fig id="Fig3" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 3</label><caption><p>Geographic origin of popularly reported plants. Almost all Haitian participants knew at least one remedy for birth and puerperium (97%) and gynecological infections (93%). The NYC Haitian community generally did not distinguish between specific types of gynecological infections, which are therefore groped together in Additional files <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">1</xref> and <xref rid="MOESM2" ref-type="media">2</xref>. Dysmenorrhea (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 0.13; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.72), infertility (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 0.42; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.52), and contraception (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 0.51; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.47) were similarly important in both the NYC Haitian and Dominican communities. However, Haitian women reported using plants for several conditions more frequently than Dominicans, including birth and puerperium (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 9.3; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.002), gynecological infections (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 3.9; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.05), vaginal cleansing (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 38; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> =  &lt; 0.0001), menstruation (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 30; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> =  &lt; 0.0001), and ovarian cysts/fibroids (<italic toggle="yes">χ</italic><sup>2</sup> = 29; <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> =  &lt; 0.0001). For gynecological infections, Haitian participants frequently applied plants topically through washes, steams, douches, and baths (Table <xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>).
