These databases can be divided into four types: herb-related (HRDB), compound-related (CRDB), target-related (TRDB), and disease-related (DRDB) databases (<xref rid="T1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). Information on herbal properties and herbal ingredients can be obtained from HRDBs, such as TCMID (<xref rid="ref21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>), TCMSP (<xref rid="ref22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>), BATMAN-TCM (<xref rid="ref23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>), and SYMMAP (<xref rid="ref24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>). Most HRDBs provide not only information on herbal ingredients, but also ingredient-related targets, pathways, and diseases. For this reason, HRDBs have been mainly utilized for network pharmacology analysis of herbal medicine. The CRDB includes databases for compounds, compound-target interaction (CTI), and compound-induced transcriptome data. PubChem (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>) and SwissADME (<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>) contain information on physicochemical descriptors, pharmacokinetic properties, and ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) parameters of compounds, which can be used to calculate drug-likeness values of herbal compounds. SwissTargetPrediction (<xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>), STITCH (<xref rid="ref28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>), and Therapeutic Target Database (TTD) (<xref rid="ref29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>) provide known or predicted CTI information. Although high-throughput targeted assays have been developed to screen for drug targets, it is a fairly arduous task to identify binding targets on a genome-wide scale, in some cases for hundreds of herbal ingredients. Therefore, some CRDBs additionally provide information on CTIs predicted by using machine learning (<xref rid="ref30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>) or similarity (<xref rid="ref31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>) based on structures of compounds and targets. Among TRDBs, Uniprot (<xref rid="ref32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>) and GeneCards (<xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>) provide information on the sequences and functional roles of proteins/genes. KEGG (<xref rid="ref34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>), Reactome (<xref rid="ref35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>), and gene ontology (<xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>) provide sets of genes classified by their biological functions. These databases are utilized to perform functional enrichment analysis, such as GSEA (gene set enrichment analysis) (<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>). In addition, STRING (<xref rid="ref38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>) and Human Protein Reference Database (<xref rid="ref39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>) provide known PPI information. PPI information can be integrated with CTI to construct a target network in which potential drug targets are identified as interacted protein modules in the network (<xref rid="ref40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>). DRDB provides collections of genes and variants associated with diseases. DRDB is also widely used because one of the ultimate goals of systems pharmacology is to predict and evaluate therapeutic effects of drugs by exploring the relationship between drugs and diseases.</p></sec><sec sec-type="other"><title>COMPUTATIONAL APPROACHES FOR STUDYING HERBAL MEDICINE</title><p>With the introduction of polypharmacology, the paradigm of drug research has shifted from single-target to multi-target strategies, revealing the potential of multicomponent herbal medicines to treat a variety of multifactorial disorders (<xref rid="ref44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref><xref rid="ref45" ref-type="bibr"/>-<xref rid="ref46" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>). In line with this, various computational approaches have been introduced to identify targets, indications, and/or synergistic combinations of herbal medicines (<xref rid="T2" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>).</p><sec><title>Prediction of pharmacological targets</title><p>Network-based methods have been most widely applied to predict potential targets of herbs or herbal formulae. For example, Wang <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>. (<xref rid="ref47" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>) have predicted synergistic targets of four herbs, <italic toggle="yes">Radix Astragali Mongolici</italic> (RAM), <italic toggle="yes">Radix Puerariae Lobatae</italic> (RPL), <italic toggle="yes">Radix Ophiopogonis Japonici</italic> (ROJ), and <italic toggle="yes">Radix Salviae Miltiorrhiza</italic> (RSM) in cardiovascular diseases (CVD). They selected bioactive compounds of the four herbs by considering compounds’ drug-likeness and oral bioavailability. Targets of the compounds were then predicted based on their structural and physicochemical properties via a machine learning method (<xref rid="ref30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>). Finally, synergistic effects of RSM and other herbs were interpreted from target proteins shared by herbs in the herb-compound-target network.
