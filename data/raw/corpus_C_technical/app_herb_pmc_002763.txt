As causative agents of strife and illness, Lango culture recognizes a shade (<bold><italic toggle="yes">tipu</italic></bold>) originating in a person, and of non-human 'winds' (<bold><italic toggle="yes">yamo</italic></bold>)—elemental entities similar to the tiny beings of other cultures, which carry out the malign intentions of a <bold><italic toggle="yes">tipu</italic></bold> gone bad. In diagnosing occult ailments, chronic conditions with severe symptoms which can be traced to a known social conflict are the domain of shades, while passing ailments of less severe symptomology which cannot be traced to a social conflict are the domain of the winds [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>]. Of the <bold><italic toggle="yes">jok</italic></bold> manifestations as diseases with a known ritual cure, the known and established endemic diseases were ascribed to the local <bold><italic toggle="yes">Jok Lango</italic></bold>, but others to the foreign <bold><italic toggle="yes">Jok Nam</italic></bold> (god of the lake), ruler of diseases thought to originate from the Bantu peoples to the southwest [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>].</p><p id="Par38">Following Driberg by a generation, the anthropologist Hayley usefully classified Lango medical conditions into three broad categories, comprising inevitable diseases of childhood which also afflict adults; unexplained illnesses of a purely biotic origin (<bold><italic toggle="yes">two</italic></bold>), and those ascribed to a manifestation of Jok power, of which the latter included "psychic disturbances and virulent diseases such as plague"—<bold><italic toggle="yes">Jok</italic></bold> (or a <bold><italic toggle="yes">jok</italic></bold>) being identified as the cause as well as the potential cure of such afflictions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>]. Hayley was struck by the fact that epilepsy (<bold><italic toggle="yes">ekwinkwin</italic></bold>) was explicitly recognized as being of a purely natural origin (at the time, untreatable) and thus relegated to the second, <bold><italic toggle="yes">two</italic></bold> category and not the third—underlining the fact that not all types of unexplained medical phenomena were automatically ascribed to occult influence in traditional society [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>].</p><p id="Par39">By comparison, Teso culture has many names for the supreme being, including <bold><italic toggle="yes">Akuj</italic></bold> ('the one above,' associated with the sun), prime mover of all things good and evil; the healing god <bold><italic toggle="yes">Ejokit</italic></bold> (as 'the good'), and <bold><italic toggle="yes">Edeke</italic></bold>—the 'god of calamity' (or vengeance) and a bringer of sickness, plague, and veneral disease [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>]. The latter name was also known among the Jie from which the Iteso descended [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>]. It has been suggested that all events, including illnesses, have "a primary cause, Akuj, and a secondary cause, created agencies;" as a result, according to one traditional healer interviewed in 1979, it is unwise to rely on only one type of treatment for sickness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>].</p><p id="Par40">Like the Lwo <bold><italic toggle="yes">ajoka</italic></bold>, the Teso <bold><italic toggle="yes">emurwon</italic></bold> ('diviner-prophet') works with ethnomedicinal cures and with <bold><italic toggle="yes">ajokin</italic></bold> (pure spirits) to counter the activity of <bold><italic toggle="yes">acudan</italic></bold> (the witch or sorcerer) and malign intentions of the elemental spirits <bold><italic toggle="yes">iboro</italic></bold> (plural of <bold><italic toggle="yes">ibore</italic></bold>), corresponding to the Lwo <bold><italic toggle="yes">yamo</italic></bold>, which can be distinguished by an otherworldly smell, and are said to be active when the finger-millet is ripening [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>]. Like the Lwo <bold><italic toggle="yes">ajoka</italic></bold>, the Iteso <bold><italic toggle="yes">emurwon</italic></bold> receives their healing vocation from the spirit world (often after surviving a serious illness) but their phytomedicinal knowledge is transmitted from elder practitioners, and the institution is thought to predate the migration of the Iteso from their original homeland among the Jie [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>].
