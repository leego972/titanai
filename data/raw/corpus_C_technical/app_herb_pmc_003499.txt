Among them, 46 were key informants, primarily recognized traditional healers and elders, identified with the support of community leaders, development agents, and local administrators. The remaining 60 informants were recruited through snowball sampling in order to capture wider community-based knowledge regarding medicinal plant use, preparation, and transmission.</p><p id="Par29">The demographic profile of participants indicates that most informants were older adults, with the highest representation in the 51–80 age group, followed by individuals aged 31–50 and 18–30 years (Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>). Educational backgrounds also varied, although the majority of participants reported no formal schooling, while others had attended primary, secondary, or college-level education.</p><p id="Par30">These socio-demographic characteristics were documented to provide context for understanding patterns of knowledge transmission, cultural continuity, and potential generational shifts in ethnobotanical expertise, rather than solely for inferential statistical purposes.</p><p id="Par31">
