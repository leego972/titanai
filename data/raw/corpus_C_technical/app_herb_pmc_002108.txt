Antioxidant Capacity</title><p>As previously stated, antioxidant phytochemicals play an outstanding role in chemoprevention [<xref rid="B5-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>], including DNA-protective capacity [<xref rid="B33-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>], as well as in enhancing anti-inflammatory and other cytoprotective effects [<xref rid="B8-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B11-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B42-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>,<xref rid="B44-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>]. According to <xref rid="biomolecules-12-01649-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>, the radical assay sensitivity to detect differences between samples was ABTS &gt; DPPH &gt; ORAC in methanolic (hydrophilic antioxidants) hexane (hydrophobic antioxidants) extracts; the ABTS▪− radical was efficiently scavenged (µmol TE/g dw) by red (44.5) &gt; pink (36.9) &gt; white (19.6) PMG in methanolic extracts (ABTS-M) and white (10.0) &gt; red, pink (3.8) PMG in hexane (ABTS-H) extracts.</p><p>Pearson’s product–moment correlation analysis (<xref rid="app1-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="app">Figure S1 in the Supplementary Materials</xref>), evidenced a positive strong-to-moderate trend (r ≥ 0.74) between ABTS-M and total PC, flavonoids, carotenoids contents, and DPPH-M. However, ABTS-M demonstrated an inverse relationship (r ≥ −0.73) with ascorbic acid and tocol content, as well as the scavenging ability of DPPH and ABTS▪− radicals for hexanoic extract (DPPH-H and ABTS-H). Conversely, ABTS-H a showed strong positive (r ≥ −0.88; ascorbic acid, tocol and DPPH-H) and moderate (r ≥ −0.61) relationship with the rest of the assayed parameters. Yang et al. [<xref rid="B50-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>] studied thirteen PMG varieties cultivated in China (Zaozhuang), reporting a positive correlation between antioxidant capacity (DPPH radical) and total contents of PC, flavonoids, and hydrolyzable tannins (r ≥ 0.89) in both the pericarp (peel) and seeds (aryls with no juice), but not in juice. Similar results were also reported for Spanish cultivars, which also include “Wonderful” and “Mollar de Elch”’ [<xref rid="B40-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>].</p><p>The aforementioned apparent causal relationships (↑ [antioxidant species]: ↑ RSC; ↑ [hydrophilic antioxidants]: ↓ [lipophilic antioxidants]) are partially explained by the individual antioxidant capacity (radical-specific quenching) and the relative abundance of each antioxidant phytochemical extracted under hydrophilic (methanol—water; LogPow ~1.0) and hydrophobic (hexane; LogPow = 3.9) conditions [<xref rid="B27-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>]. Unlike DPPH and ABTS▪− radicals-based assays, the ORAC assay is considered more convenient to evaluate chain-breaking antioxidant activity within biological systems where reactive oxygen (ROS) and nitrogen species (NOS) are more relevant [<xref rid="B20-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>]. ROS plays a dual role in cancer by promoting cell proliferation–survival–adaptation to hypoxia and by triggering oxidative-stress cancer cell death, both cases handled with ROS-manipulation therapies [<xref rid="B51-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>]. However, this assay was less sensitive to discriminate PMG phenogenotypes, a fact that deserves a more detailed study aimed to uncover the fine-tuning mechanism exerted by each antioxidant specie.</p></sec><sec id="sec3dot5-biomolecules-12-01649"><title>3.5.
