# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

Urbanisation brings new health challenges resulting from ease of contagion, maintenance of disease due to high population densities and stress-related ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>]. Although biomedicine is often easily available in urban settings, traditional medicines can still be the most convenient and affordable health care resource [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. Similar to people in rural areas, urban dwellers can hold rich medicinal plant knowledge. Recent studies evidence the dynamism and adaptive nature of urban medicinal plant knowledge (e.g. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>]), challenging views that such knowledge is lost in cities.</p><p id="Par8">Since Balick et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>] noted a lack of ethnobotanical studies in urban areas, urban ethnobotany has flourished. Ethnobotanical surveys in cities have focused on issues such as the change of plant use by international immigrants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>] or the ethnobotanical diversity found in urban and peri-urban markets [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>] and home gardens [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>]. With few exceptions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>], these studies are set in Northern and Western countries. Cities in other parts of the world are equally dynamic plant knowledge hubs, and some have played an important role in the trade of medicinal plant material and knowledge historically. This is the case of cities along the silk and incense trade routes in the Middle East [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec3"><title>Women’s medicinal plant knowledge</title><p id="Par9">Several studies across the world have reported the pivotal role of women as holders of medicinal plant knowledge [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref>]. Medicinal plant knowledge is often women’s cultural domain because of the role women play in providing household care [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>]. It also stems from gendered labour and spaces, which affect plant resource harvesting and management [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>]. Women are often more knowledgeable about medicinal plant identification and use than are men [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>], and their knowledge can be epistemologically different [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>].</p><p id="Par10">Moreover, the documentation of traditional knowledge as part of plant biodiversity research has historically been gender-biased towards men, which can result in misleading and incomplete results [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. Both Howard [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>] and Pfeiffer and Butz [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>] claim that focus on male specialists, such as shamans and herbalists, has ignored a wealth of lay, female plant knowledge in ethnobotanical research. Historically, ethnobotanists have been predominantly male, which hampered their access to women’s knowledge in societies where men are granted greater public access than women [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. These considerations are particularly important in conducting ethnobotanical research in the Islamic world, where gendered spaces and networks are particularly strong. Women in urban Islamic contexts have so far gone unnoticed by ethnobotanical enquiry, although they are the main medicinal plant users in Saudi Arabian cities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Islam and medicinal plants</title><p id="Par11">In the Arabian Peninsula, where this study took place, the use of local plant diversity for medicines is part of the cultural heritage [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>] and is currently embedded in Islamic medicinal practices. Islamic medicine integrates ancient Greek medicine, which first arrived to the Islamic world through translations of the works of Hippocrates, Dioscorides and Galen [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>], with the teachings of the Prophet Mohamed (Hadith) referring to health, disease and medical treatment that became known as ‘The Medicine of the Prophet’ [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>].
