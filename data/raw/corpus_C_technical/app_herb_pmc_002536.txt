On the one hand, the concentrations of total phenols
(TP), total flavonoids, and flavan-3-ols in Golden delicious were
6.8 ± 1.2 mg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per 100 g of dw,
3.8 ± 0.6 mg catechin equivalent (CE)/ 100 g dw, and 2.6 ±
0.4 mg CE/ 100 g dw, respectively. On the other hand, these values
were 15.5 ± 3.2 GAE/ 100 g dw, 11.0 ± 3.1 mg CE/ 100 g
dw, and 7.5 ± 2.3 mg CE/ 100 g dw, respectively, for the Panaia
red variety, demonstrating that Panaia red is much richer in phenolics
compared to Golden delicious. The important biomolecules from apple
pomace also include triterpenoids, which are secondary metabolites
synthesized from isopentenyl pyrophosphate oligomers,<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref68">68</xref></sup> which present six isoprene units (C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>8</sub>).<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref56">56</xref></sup> Triterpene acids are found in apples,
particularly in the cuticular wax of the peels, with ursolic and oleanolic
acids being the most abundant.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref69">69</xref></sup> Pentacyclic
triterpenes such as ursolic acid have gained much attention from researchers
due to their antioxidant, antitumor, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial
properties.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref70">70</xref></sup> Pentacyclic triterpenoids,
in addition to their biological activity, have no obvious toxicity
and are therefore potential chemicals to be used in new medicinal
products.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref71">71</xref></sup></p><p>Apple peels contain anthocyanins,
flavonols, flavan-3-ols, dihydrochalcones,
and phenolic acids, which have been correlated with health-promoting
benefits.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref72">72</xref></sup> In fact, an apple peel is mainly
rich in phenolic compounds compared to other apple byproducts due
to their physiological function in protecting the fruit against ultraviolet
radiation.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref73">73</xref></sup> This is supported by several
studies showing that apple peel may have greater antioxidants and
antioxidant capacity than the pulp fraction or the whole fruit.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref74">74</xref>−<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref78">78</xref></sup> Eberhardt et al.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref79">79</xref></sup> reported the contents
of phenolics and flavonoids in apple with skin as 290 mg/100 g apple
and 142.7 mg/100 g apple, respectively; however, these values were
219.9 mg of phenolics and 97.6 mg of flavonoids per 100 g of apples
without skin. In another study conducted by Rupasinghe and Kean,<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref80">80</xref></sup> the apple skin of two cultivars (Idared and
Northern Spy) from the juice industry was reported to have over sevenfold
higher total phenolic content when compared with the pomace. In addition,
the skin of Idared apple was shown to have fivefold greater levels
of total phenolic compounds. Moreover, with regard to polyphenols,
quercetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-galactoside was predominant, followed
by quercetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-rhamnoside and quercetin-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-glucoside in the skin of two cultivars. Sun-Waterhouse
et al.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref81">81</xref></sup> measured and compared the bioactive
content and appearance attributes of juices and skin wastes of three
apple genotypes including white fleshed (WF), pink fleshed (PF), and
red fleshed (RF). The total extractable phenolic content of RF apple
was shown to be the highest, while PF had the least.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref81">81</xref></sup> Lončarić et al.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref82">82</xref></sup> recovered polyphenols from the peel of 12 traditional and 8 commercial
apple varieties by the Micro-Matrix Solid-Phase Dispersion extraction
method. They reported that the peel of traditional apples had higher
contents of all investigated polyphenols. They also calculated the
relative contribution of polyphenol groups and reported the major
contributors to the total polyphenolic content in traditional and
commercial apple varieties as nonflavonoids (28.6%) and flavanols
(46.2%), respectively. In addition to these, chlorogenic acid, procyanidin
B2, and epicatechin were reported to be the most abundant polyphenols
in traditional apple peel, while procyanidin B2 was reported in commercial
apple varieties.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref82">82</xref></sup></p><p>Apple seeds represent
high oil content, and oleic acid and linoleic
acid were reported to be the main fatty acids.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref83">83</xref>−<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref85">85</xref></sup> A high percentage
of unsaturated fatty acids makes apple seed oil nutritionally favorable,
having positive effects on lowering low-density lipoprotein (LDL)
cholesterol and preventing risks of cardiovascular diseases.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref83">83</xref></sup> In addition to fatty acids, apple seed also
contains a high proportion of polyphenols similar to other apple juice
industry byproducts.
