</p></sec><sec id="Sec17"><title>Use of fresh versus dried plant materials</title><p id="Par102">In the Karamara forest patches, medicinal plant species were utilized either fresh, dried, or in both forms, depending on plant part, availability, and intended application. The majority of plant preparations (87 species, 76.9%) relied on fresh materials, while 23 species (20.3%) were used in dried form, and 2 species (1.7%) were employed in both fresh and dried forms.</p><p id="Par103">Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Abutilon fruticosum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Acacia nubica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Dodonaea angustifolia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus globulus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Solanum coagulans</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic> were predominantly used fresh. Fresh materials, especially leaves, fruits, and latex, were preferred for treating wounds, digestive disorders, malaria, eye problems, and respiratory ailments, as they retain higher bioactive potency and are readily available for immediate use.</p><p id="Par104">Conversely, species such as <italic toggle="yes">Clerodendrum myricoides</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Coffea arabica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Datura stramonium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Myrsine africana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ricinus communis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Solanum incanum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Tamarindus indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic> were primarily used dried. Drying was mostly applied to roots, seeds, stems, and bark, which were subsequently boiled, crushed, or powdered for long-term storage, decoctions, or oral administration.</p><p id="Par105">Several species demonstrated dual usage, being applied either fresh or dried depending on seasonal availability and therapeutic purpose. Examples include <italic toggle="yes">Acacia nilotica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Argemone mexicana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Solanum coagulans</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Solanum incanum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe pirottae</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic>. This flexibility reflects the community’s adaptive knowledge in maximizing the efficacy and accessibility of MPs throughout the year.</p></sec><sec id="Sec18"><title>Dosage, Additives, and diagnostic practices in medicinal plant use</title><p id="Par106">In the Karamara forest patches, the dosage of herbal medicines varied according to the type of disease, the patient’s age, and overall health condition. Traditional practitioners determined doses using locally available measures, including liters, spoons, tea cups, coffee cups, water glasses, or even the palm of the hand, adapting quantities to the patient and the ailment’s severity.</p><p id="Par107">Diagnosis was primarily based on careful observation and patient consultation. Healers assessed symptoms through interviews with patients or their attendants, while visually examining changes in eyes, urine, skin color, tongue, and throat. Other indicators included body temperature, swelling, edema, coughing, bleeding, diarrhea, vomiting, presence of parasites, and sores, allowing practitioners to prescribe appropriate treatments and adjust dosages according to the duration and intensity of the illness.</p><p id="Par108">Herbal remedies were used with or without additives, depending on the preparation and desired effect.
