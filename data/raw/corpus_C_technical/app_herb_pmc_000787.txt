Introduction</title><p><italic toggle="yes">Hedera helix</italic> L. (Araliaceae), also known as common ivy or English ivy, has been traditionally used for the treatment of respiratory disorders [<xref rid="B1-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. The pharmacological data of <italic toggle="yes">H. helix</italic> extracts, including its bronchodilator, antibacterial, bronchospasmolytic, and expectorant effects, have supported its traditional use as a natural remedy for respiratory illness [<xref rid="B2-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="B3-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>]. Currently, it is one of the top-selling herbal respiratory medicines in many countries worldwide, and it is popularly used for the treatment of cough and cough-related problems [<xref rid="B4-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="B5-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. Bronchospasmolytic activity was exerted by hederacoside C, α-hederin, aglycone hederagenin, kaempferol and quercetin of <italic toggle="yes">H. helix</italic> extract [<xref rid="B6-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. Apigenin, kaempferol and quercetin significantly reduced the contraction of guinea-pig isolated ileum induced by prostaglandin E2 and leukotriene D4 [<xref rid="B7-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. The saponin from <italic toggle="yes">H. helix</italic> inhibited the terbutaline-stimulated internalization of the β2-adrenergic receptor in alveolar epithelial type-II cell line to explain its spasmolytic and β-mimetic effects [<xref rid="B8-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. Hederacoside C (HDC) is known as one of the primary constituents responsible for the therapeutic efficacy of <italic toggle="yes">H. helix</italic> extracts [<xref rid="B10-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>].</p><p>Unlike conventional drugs, herbal products are a complex mixture of bioactive constituents. As a result, their co-administration with prescription drugs may produce unexpected toxic or adverse consequences [<xref rid="B11-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. The main mechanisms underlying such interactions are via pharmacokinetic modulations such as inhibition or induction of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters. Among them, the inhibition of cytochrome P450 (CYP), a representative drug-metabolizing enzyme, is considered as one of the most frequent causes for herb–drug interactions [<xref rid="B11-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-molecules-22-01241" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>].
