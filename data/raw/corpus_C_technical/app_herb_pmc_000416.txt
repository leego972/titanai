medicines for pregnant women are rarely evaluated through randomised controlled/clinical trials (RCTs). Nonetheless, most of the reviews of herbal medicines during pregnancy are restricted to RCTs. The present review included non-experimental studies to assess a wider evidence base.</p></list-item><list-item><p>No restrictions were applied on the date of publication, location, study design and types of treatment (herbal medicine used).</p></list-item><list-item><p>There is lack of data on key outcomes (eg, maternal death and sepsis) and from low-income countries.</p></list-item><list-item><p>Some analyses did not have sufficient statistical power due to the inadequate number of studies and small sample sizes.</p></list-item></list></boxed-text><sec sec-type="intro" id="s1"><title>Introduction</title><p>Across the world, the use of unconventional or traditional medical therapies is very high.<xref rid="R1" ref-type="bibr">1–4</xref> These non-biomedical remedies are together referred to as complementary and alternative medicines (CAMs). WHO recognises the role of CAM of verified quality, safety and efficacy in ensuring universal access to healthcare.<xref rid="R5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref> As such, for the period between 2014 and 2023, WHO traditional medicine strategy focuses on harnessing the potential contribution of CAM in healthcare and promoting its safe and effective use.<xref rid="R5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref> Although this requires rigorous evidence on safety and efficacy of CAM, research in this area remains limited.<xref rid="R5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref> Herbal medicine or medicinal plant is one of the well-known CAM therapies that involve the use of plants or plant extracts for therapeutic motives.<xref rid="R6" ref-type="bibr">6</xref> As in the general population, the use of herbal medicines is common among pregnant women globally.<xref rid="R7" ref-type="bibr">7–10</xref> The estimated prevalence varies between regions and countries but ranges from 10% to 80%.<xref rid="R11" ref-type="bibr">11 12</xref> One of the common indications for herbal medicine use during pregnancy is prolonged labour or merely the desire to induce or augment labour for different reasons.<xref rid="R13" ref-type="bibr">13 14</xref> This practice is well documented and transcends cultural and generational boundaries.<xref rid="R14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref></p><p>From a medical perspective, induction of labour (IOL) changes the physiological processes associated with childbirth in ways that may increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as neonatal mortality, fetal distress, premature birth, haemorrhage, uterine rupture and caesarean section.<xref rid="R15" ref-type="bibr">15–17</xref> Because of this, WHO recommends that labour should only be induced in health facilities with the capacity for continual monitoring and emergency obstetric care.<xref rid="R18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref> The emphasis on facility-based IOL and close monitoring of pregnant women demonstrates the risks associated with the procedure.
