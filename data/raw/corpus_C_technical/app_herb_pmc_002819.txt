Several ethnobotanical studies have enumerated the plants used for various illnesses in India and elsewhere, especially wound healing [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">24</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B96">96</xref>] and skin diseases [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B97">97</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B98">98</xref>]. However when we compared these reports to the Malasars healers we found that they occasionally use different medicinal plants for the same illness category. To heal wounds, the Malasars used six plants (<italic toggle="yes">Achyranthes aspera </italic>Blume, <italic toggle="yes">Azima tetracantha </italic>Lam., <italic toggle="yes">Blepharis repens </italic>(Vahl) Roth, <italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia hirta </italic>L., <italic toggle="yes">Malaxis rheedii </italic>Sw., <italic toggle="yes">Trichodesma indicum </italic>(L.) R. Br.), of which all but one (<italic toggle="yes">Malaxis rheedii </italic>– high altitude species) are distributed in the plains and coastal areas. In this case personal preference may not be the reason for choosing these plants, but potential active ingredients for utilization in these plants for particular illnesses. Similarly, for skin diseases, three plants are used (<italic toggle="yes">Acalypha indica </italic>L., <italic toggle="yes">Lycopodium phlegmaria </italic>L. and <italic toggle="yes">Sphaeranthus indicus </italic>L.) of which all but <italic toggle="yes">Lycopodium </italic>are commonly found in the plains. In this case preference of availability may not be the key reason for constant utilization of this plant by the Malasars for skin diseases. However, on the plains, <italic toggle="yes">Acalypha indica </italic>is also used by traditional healers to treat scorpion bites and sore throats [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B23">23</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B30">30</xref>].</p><p>The Malasars are strong believers in spiritualism. They have a special way of dealing with illnesses brought along by evil. This is known as a culture bound syndrome, a folk illness that is specific to different cultures [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B99">99</xref>]. This is similar to '<italic toggle="yes">susto</italic>', within the latin population of Mexico, Guatemala and Texas. <italic toggle="yes">Susto </italic>is a folk illness, specifically a "fright sickness" with strong psychological overtones. The Malasars cure this category of illness using plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Abrus precatorius </italic>L., <italic toggle="yes">Crotalaria verrucosa and Selaginella rupestris </italic>(see mode of utilization in Additional file <xref ref-type="supplementary-material" rid="S1">1</xref>). The informant consensus factor for the spiritual illness category was quite high (F<sub>IC </sub>of 0.87). Weller et al [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B99">99</xref>] also reported that the treatment for <italic toggle="yes">susto </italic>involves praying for the individual, discussing the event that brought about the "sickness" and cultural rituals that involve 'drawing out the sickness' and 'restoring the lost essence'. Most treatments for folk illnesses can be found within cultural references. The Malasars treatment involves praying, communication with the evil sprit and the use of plants.</p><p>The Malasars traditional plant classification and nomenclature is complex and unique. During our ethnobotanical survey of the Malasars in the Velliangiri hills, we recorded the food and medicinal use of several ethnotaxa of <italic toggle="yes">Diplocyclos palmatus </italic>(L.) C. Jeffrey. In Tamil it is known as <italic toggle="yes">Iverali</italic>, which is based on it's morphology, meaning palm like leaves (I' = five; veralli = five fingers like leaf). The ethnotaxa were collected from different habitats of which some have utility as either medicine or healthy food. One of the ethnotaxa of <italic toggle="yes">Diplocyclos palmatus </italic>was identified by a Malasars healer as '<italic toggle="yes">Lingankatti'</italic>, which is only used for rheumatic pain. The name <italic toggle="yes">Lingankatti </italic>is derived from morphology of the fruit, which is deep reddish in colour, but more importantly this is also related to spiritual folk lore; <italic toggle="yes">Siva</italic>, the fire God of the holy hills represents red hot volcanic lava.
