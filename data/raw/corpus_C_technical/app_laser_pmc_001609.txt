For the remaining cases in the database, feedback on histopathological outcome was given after every 10–15 scans.</p><p>The diagnostic error rate, defined as the sum of false-negative and false-positive OCT results as a proportion of the total number of cases, was used as the criterion to assess diagnostic performance in this study, with histopathological diagnosis as reference standard.</p><sec id="sec1.1"><title>Training prior to the study</title><p>Before the start of the study, the OCT assessors received instructions on BCC diagnosing and subtyping with OCT by a representative from the manufacturer. Also they studied literature on OCT in dermatology and attended a convention on OCT (<xref rid="cit0019" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>). Approximately 20 OCT scans were assessed purely for educational purposes and to become familiar with the OCT device (scans not included in this study).</p><p>One of the OCT assessors had several years of clinical experience with diagnosis and treatment of BCC (including Mohs surgery) as a dermatology resident, and one had two years of experience in clinical dermato-oncology as a research fellow.</p></sec><sec id="sec1.2"><title>Learning curve analysis</title><p>A cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart was used to track performance over time and was constructed using an Excel spreadsheet (<xref rid="cit0020" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>). CUSUM is an analysis technique typically used for sequential monitoring of cumulative performance and detection of change in performance over time. CUSUM charts were originally developed for industrial process monitoring and are based on the classification of a product’s quality into 1 of 2 categories: “defective” or “non-defective” (<xref rid="cit0021" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>). The purpose is to detect changes in the proportion (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic>) of items in the “defective” category. It is necessary to pre-specify an acceptable failure rate (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic>) and an unacceptable failure rate (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic>). In the same manner, a CUSUM chart can be applied to evaluate the learning process in medical interventional and diagnostic techniques (<xref rid="cit0020" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>, <xref rid="cit0022" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>–<xref rid="cit0025" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). The outcome of the diagnostic technique (in this case OCT) has to be classified into “success” or “failure”. For construction of the CUSUM chart, the cumulative sum after each case is plotted against the index number of that case. For each failure, a certain score (<italic toggle="yes">s</italic>, see formula in Appendix S1<sup><xref rid="fn0003" ref-type="fn">1</xref></sup>) is added and for each success, a score (1 – <italic toggle="yes">s</italic>) is subtracted. The CUSUM is the running sum of a mixture of increments (with each failure) and decrements (with each success). A continuing descending curve indicates that successes occur more frequently than failures.</p><p>When the running sum exceeds a certain threshold boundary, this signals a critical change. The upper and lower limits represent the boundary above which performance becomes unacceptable (<italic toggle="yes">h</italic><sub>0</sub>) or below which performance becomes acceptable (<italic toggle="yes">h</italic><sub>1</sub>), respectively. These boundaries depend on the setting of <italic toggle="yes">p</italic><sub>0</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic><sub>1</sub>, but also on the setting of the false-positive or type I error (α, risk of falsely concluding that a trainee’s performance is unacceptable when it is not) and the false-negative or type II error (β, the risk of falsely concluding that a trainee’s performance is acceptable when it is not). The type I and type II error are conventionally set at 0.1, making <italic toggle="yes">h</italic><sub>0</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">h</italic><sub>1</sub> equal (<xref rid="cit0022" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>). For a detailed explanation see Appendix S1<sup><xref rid="fn0003" ref-type="fn">1</xref></sup>.</p><p>The primary endpoint in this study was the number of OCT assessments after which an adequate level of competence was achieved.
