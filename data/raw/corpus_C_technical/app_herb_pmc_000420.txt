In brief, of the 10 studies in the review, three were conducted in Iran, two in the USA and one each in South Africa, Israel, Thailand, Australia and Italy. In relation to the World Bank’s classification of countries by income, half of the studies were conducted in high-income countries and the other half in upper-middle-income countries (UMICs). No study from low-income countries (LICs) or lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) was included.</p><supplementary-material content-type="local-data" id="SP7" position="float" orientation="portrait"><object-id pub-id-type="doi">10.1136/bmjopen-2018-022499.supp7</object-id><label>Supplementary material 7</label><p><inline-supplementary-material xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="ss7" xlink:href="bmjopen-2018-022499supp007.pdf" mimetype="application" mime-subtype="pdf" content-type="local-data"/></p></supplementary-material><p>Three types of exposures were reported by the studies. An Australian study was concerned with exposure to raspberry leaf.<xref rid="R41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref> This is one of the common herbal remedies used during pregnancy that is believed to prepare the uterus for childbirth and thereby effectively reduce the length of labour.<xref rid="R14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref> In this study, exposure was self-reported by the participants as they were given raspberry pills by the nurses to take at home. Eight studies examined exposure to castor oil.<xref rid="R25" ref-type="bibr">25 34 42–47</xref> The oil is derived from the castor plant’s bean and is widely thought to have oxytocic properties.<xref rid="R44" ref-type="bibr">44 45</xref> In all the studies, pregnant women consumed 60 mL of castor oil, but in one study<xref rid="R43" ref-type="bibr">43</xref> the treatment was repeated in women who did not deliver within 1 week after the first dose. One study<xref rid="R48" ref-type="bibr">48</xref> assessed general exposure to herbal medicines, but there are indications in the report that they were for IOL.</p><p>Five of the included studies are RCTs and the remaining five are non-experimental, including cohort (3), case–control (1) and quasi-experimental (1) designs. The following pregnancy outcomes were reported by the included studies: onset of labour within 24 hours, caesarean section, haemorrhage, neonatal referral to nursery care, MSL, assisted vaginal delivery, stillbirth, neonatal death, maternal death and uterus rupture.</p></sec><sec id="s3c"><title>Outcome 1: onset of labour within 24 hours</title><p>Eight studies explored the onset of labour within 24 hours after the use of herbal medicine for IOL. Castor oil was the exposure or intervention in all the studies. As shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F2">figure 2</xref>, herbal medicine users were significantly more likely to give birth within 24 hours than non-users (RR 3.46; 95% CI 1.58 to 7.55). In the subgroup analysis by study design, similar results were observed among experimental studies, but there was no significant difference in onset of labour between users and non-users among the non-experimental studies (online <xref ref-type="supplementary-material" rid="SP8">supplementary file S8</xref>). Publication bias was not an issue (bias 3.23; 95% CI 0.48 to 5.97), but heterogeneity was significant (I<sup>2</sup>=90.2%, p≤0.001) and this was likely due to variations in study design and or setting.</p><supplementary-material content-type="local-data" id="SP8" position="float" orientation="portrait"><object-id pub-id-type="doi">10.1136/bmjopen-2018-022499.supp8</object-id><label>Supplementary material 8</label><p><inline-supplementary-material xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="ss8" xlink:href="bmjopen-2018-022499supp008.pdf" mimetype="application" mime-subtype="pdf" content-type="local-data"/></p></supplementary-material><fig id="F2" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Figure 2</label><caption><p>The use of herbal medicines for induction of labour and onset of labour.
