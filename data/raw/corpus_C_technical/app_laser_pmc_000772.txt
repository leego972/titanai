Subsequently, in 1961, Lipson et al. demonstrated that hematoporphyrin derivatives (HpD) accumulated in tumors and emitted fluorescence could be applied as a diagnostic tool [<xref rid="B10-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>].</p><p>Further, Dougherty et al. introduced PDT in the 1970s by observing complete mammary tumor remission in vivo using HpD combined with the red light [<xref rid="B11-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. A subsequent clinical study using 25 patients showed complete response in 98 out of 113 skin tumors, partial response in 13, and only two resistant tumors [<xref rid="B12-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. These findings led to the first approval of the PDT drug, Photofrin<sup>®</sup>, to treat bladder cancer in Canada in 1993 [<xref rid="B13-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. In 2001, Foscan<sup>®</sup> became the first second-generation PS agent commercially available for PDT, especially in head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) treatment. Ultimately, in 2012, silicon phthalocyanine (Pc) 4 entered a phase I clinical trial as the first topical PDT agent <bold>(</bold><xref ref-type="fig" rid="pharmaceuticals-14-00772-f001">Figure 1</xref><bold>)</bold> [<xref rid="B14-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>]. Currently, PDT is employed to treat a wide range of diseases, such as leishmaniasis, psoriasis, neovascular macular degeneration, cardiology, urology, immunology, ophthalmology, dentistry, and dermatology [<xref rid="B15-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>].</p><p>The distinctiveness of PDT relies on its highly selective mechanism of action. Utilizing two independently nontoxic components (i.e., a PS and light) to produce cytotoxicity within a tumor. There is a high level of tumor selectivity to PS due to increased tumor vasculature surface area, higher membrane permeability of cancer cells, and decreased lymphatic drainage [<xref rid="B16-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. An ideal PS remains inert until a light source is focused onto the intended area to “activate” the PS, boosting its selectivity over surrounding healthy tissues. Ultimately, a third intrinsic component for PDT, molecular oxygen, present in tissue’s extracellular and intracellular spaces, serves as the substrate for ROS formation. The generation of singlet oxygen and superoxide anions results in tumor cytotoxicity as both can directly react with and damage biomolecules such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [<xref rid="B17-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>].</p><p>Several studies have demonstrated PDT as a viable treatment option against early-stage esophageal dysplasia, lung, HNSC, anal, bladder, peritoneal ovarian, and non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSC) [<xref rid="B18-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. Despite the encouraging clinical results of PDT, some PSs themselves have been reported to have prolonged skin phototoxicity, low lesion selectivity, hydrophobic nature, aggregation proneness, poor bioavailability, high-dose requirements, adverse side effects, off-targeting, and development of drug resistance [<xref rid="B19-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B20-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="B21-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>]. The use of drug delivery systems (DDS) to overcome these shortcomings has been examined. In this context, PSs can be ideally delivered to therapeutic action sites while reducing adverse side effects [<xref rid="B21-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>]. Among DDS, the transdermal route stands out for dermatological applications. However, the inherent protective epidermic layer, the high molecular weight of some PSs (&gt;500 Daltons), and extremes of polarity remain a challenge for crossing the skin barrier [<xref rid="B22-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>].</p><p>Several innovative transdermal delivery systems (tDDS) have recently become available to study photobiology, improve drug penetration, enhance site-specific delivery, and increase therapeutic efficiency. This review aims to summarize current approaches focused on boosting the transdermal delivery efficacy of PSs for treating cutaneous conditions.</p></sec><sec id="sec2-pharmaceuticals-14-00772"><title>2. Transdermal Drug Delivery of PDT Agents</title><p>The skin barrier is a lipid layer along which individual molecules can migrate through diffusion. Although skin has a large surface area, transdermal drug delivery is challenging since it acts as a formidable barrier. Molecules can enter through keratinocytes (the transcellular pathway), the lipid matrix occupying the intercellular spaces (the intercellular pathway), hair follicles, sebaceous glands, or sweat glands (the transappendageal pathway) [<xref rid="B23-pharmaceuticals-14-00772" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>].
