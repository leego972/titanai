The basic concept of SS-OCT was also described in patents as early as 1991<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b49" ref-type="bibr">49</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b50" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b51" ref-type="bibr">51</xref></sup> and experimental studies in 1996 and 1997.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>,<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup> These techniques were used in the 1980s to measure fiber optics and photonics components.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b52" ref-type="bibr">52</xref><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b53" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b54" ref-type="bibr">54</xref></sup> The concept of frequency chirped coherent ranging and the signal-to-noise advantages were described in coherent laser literature in the 1980s<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b55" ref-type="bibr">55</xref></sup> but this sensitivity advantage was not translated to OCT until 2003.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref></sup> Many of the advantages of SS-OCT were described in 1995 by Chinn et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref></sup> and Golubovic et al.,<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup> but the sensitivity advantages were not realized and performance was limited by available laser technology, which was the major bottleneck at that time. Studies by Yun et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref></sup> in 2003 demonstrated high-speed SS-OCT in nonophthalmic applications with 19,000 A-scans per second and 13- to 14-μm axial resolution.</p><p>Because SS-OCT performance depends on the swept laser, advances in laser technology became critical. The development of Fourier domain mode locking (FDML) in 2006 by Huber et al.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b43" ref-type="bibr">43</xref></sup> provided a novel approach for breaking laser tuning speed limits. Early FDML lasers achieved record speeds of 370,000 A-scans per second in nonophthalmic applications.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b56" ref-type="bibr">56</xref></sup> This technology enabled multiple ophthalmic as well as endoscopic/catheter-based applications and ophthalmic imaging with greater than 1 million A-scans per second was demonstrated, a speed increase of greater than 100×.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b57" ref-type="bibr">57</xref></sup></p><p>In addition to research systems, commercial swept lasers also made critical advances. The commercial short cavity swept laser (Axsun Technologies, Billerica, MA, USA) enabled intravascular as well as ophthalmic applications. Retinal imaging with approximately 5- to 6-μm axial resolution at 1050 nm was demonstrated at 100,000 and 200,000 A-scan per second.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b58" ref-type="bibr">58</xref></sup> The vertical cavity surface emitting laser (VCSEL) improved both imaging speed and range. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f10">Figure 10</xref> shows wide-field retinal and choroidal imaging using SS-OCT at 580,000 A-scans per second.<sup><xref rid="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-b59" ref-type="bibr">59</xref></sup> A 12 × 12–mm region of the retina with 1000 × 1000 A-scans can be imaged in approximately 2 seconds. The high A-scan density enables high-resolution en face OCT images.</p><fig id="i1552-5783-57-9-OCT1-f10" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Figure 10</label><caption><p>Wide-field retinal and choroidal OCT imaging.
