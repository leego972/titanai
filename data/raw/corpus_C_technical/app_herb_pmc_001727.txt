The presence of the <italic toggle="yes">ortho</italic>-dihydroxy group in the phenolic ring (as in caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>) was suggested for the antioxidant activity of hydroxycinnamic acids toward LDL in vitro oxidation [<xref rid="B58-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>]. The radical scavenging activity of these hydroxycinnamic acids follows a similar mechanism to the flavonoids based on their ability to donate hydroxyl hydrogen and/or an electron to form resonance-stabilised intermediates. The scavenging mechanism is supported by (i) the <italic toggle="yes">ortho</italic>-dihydroxy phenyl configuration, which induces a metal ion chelation similar to that of flavonoids, (ii) the presence of –CH=CH–COOH groups in hydroxycinnamic acids that enhance the H-donating ability, and subsequent radical stabilisation due to the resonance-stabilised structure of α-β-unsaturated carbonyl [<xref rid="B50-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>,<xref rid="B59-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>].</p><p>For phenolic acids (hydroxybenzoic acid), the radical scavenging activity of the phenolic acids depends on the number and position of the hydroxyl (OH) groups and methoxy (–OCH<sub>3</sub>) substituents on the aromatic ring. The presence of π-π conjugation and <italic toggle="yes">para</italic>- and <italic toggle="yes">ortho</italic>-OH or –OCH<sub>3</sub> increases the possibility of forming resonance-stabilised intermediates after proton abstraction or electron donation. Benzoic acid shows no radical scavenging capacity (0 mM TEAC) because of its absence of a hydroxy group and the inability to form resonance-stabilised structures in comparison to its hydroxyl derivatives, such as gallic acid <bold>25</bold>, methyl gallate <bold>26</bold>, syringic acid <bold>28</bold>, vanillin <bold>29</bold>, protocatechuic acid <bold>30</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic <bold>31,</bold> and salicylic acid <bold>32</bold>, which have been identified across the antioxidant extracts of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic>. These hydroxybenzoic acids have been known to have substantial antioxidant activities. For instance, gallic acid <bold>25</bold> (3,4,5-trihydroxy-benzoic acid) was reported to have the most potent radical scavenging capacity (3.52 mM TEAC) because of the 3,4,5-trihydroxy configuration advantage and potent H-donating ability compared to protocatechuic acid <bold>30</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 1.15 mM), syringic <bold>28</bold> (4-OH, 3,5-OCH<sub>3</sub>, 1.39 mM), vanillic acid <bold>29</bold> (4-OH, 3-OCH<sub>3</sub>, 0.092 mM), and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic acid <bold>31</bold> (0.03–0.04 mM) [<xref rid="B52-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. The presence of these compounds, therefore, provided a sufficient phytochemical link regarding the antioxidant activities observed in the extracts from <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic>. </p><p>In summary, the presence of flavonoids, hydroxycinnamic acids, and hydroxybenzoic acids identified across the extracts (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>) from various parts of the plant has provided ample support for the antioxidant capacity of these extracts.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec3dot2-molecules-28-04396"><title>3.2. Antibacterial</title><p>Several authors evaluated the antibacterial activities of extracts from <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic>. The aqueous extract of flowers was shown to be active against phytopathogenic bacteria by Al-Huqail et al. [<xref rid="B12-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. The extract was active against <italic toggle="yes">Agrobacterium tumefaciens</italic> (MIC = 200 µg/mL), <italic toggle="yes">Enterobacter cloacae</italic> (MIC = 300 µg/mL), <italic toggle="yes">Erwinia amylovora</italic> (MIC = 300 µg/mL), and <italic toggle="yes">Pectobacterium carotovorum</italic> subsp (MIC = 100 µg/mL) (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref>). The minimum inhibition concentration (MIC) values were lower than the positive control (Tobramycin in 10 µg/disc). HPLC-based analysis of the extract revealed two main phenolic and flavonoid compounds (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref>). Among these (reported in % <italic toggle="yes">w</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">w</italic> of crude extract) were benzoic acid <bold>24</bold> (0.162), <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic acid <bold>31</bold> (0.014), syringic acid (0.006), and salicylic acid <bold>32</bold> (0.004). Hydroxycinnamic acids such as <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>36</bold> (0.042), ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> (0.007), caffeic acid <bold>35</bold> (0.003), <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> (0.002), ellagic acid <bold>44</bold> (12.17), and catechol <bold>45</bold> (6.54) were also identified.
