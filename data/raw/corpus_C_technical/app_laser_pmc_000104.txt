Gong et al<xref rid="bib15" ref-type="bibr"><sup>15</sup></xref> found that cryotherapy in a rabbit model simulates an environment similar to that of the fetal wound-healing environment, which is characterized by minimal inflammation, rapid re-epithelialization, and reduced collagen formation. Knott et al<xref rid="bib84" ref-type="bibr"><sup>84</sup></xref> found both reduced collagen formation with more organized structure after application of cryotherapy, along with decreased keratinization. Another study by Li et al<xref rid="bib85" ref-type="bibr"><sup>85</sup></xref> investigated differences in healing of skin wounds caused by burn and freeze injuries. It was found that a burn wound contained three times the amount of collagen as normal skin, and the freeze wound contained ¾ as much collagen as normal skin. The reduced formation of collagen is crucial to the preservation of postoperative voice quality. Due to its minimal scarring and positive postoperative voice outcomes, cryotherapy has the potential to become an important treatment for laryngeal diseases such as papilloma, glottic carcinoma, and both glottic and subglottic stenosis.<xref rid="bib86" ref-type="bibr">86</xref>, <xref rid="bib87" ref-type="bibr">87</xref>, <xref rid="bib88" ref-type="bibr">88</xref></p><p id="p0255">Cryotherapy has a wide range of clinical applications for laryngeal surgery involving the treatment of laryngeal cancers as well benign laryngeal lesions. A 1968 study by Gage<xref rid="bib89" ref-type="bibr"><sup>89</sup></xref> used cryotherapy on 50 patients with oral or oropharyngeal cancer and showed comparable survival rates to surgical excision. Further advancement of cryotherapy has led to its use as an adjuvant to laser surgery. A 2006 study by Knott et al<xref rid="bib87" ref-type="bibr"><sup>87</sup></xref> on early-stage glottic cancer demonstrated that endoscopic laser laryngeal surgery with adjuvant cryotherapy not only yielded excellent primary site control of the cancer, but also led to subjective and objective improvements in postoperative voice quality. In this study they administered cryotherapy to the wound margins after excision of the tumor by CO<sub>2</sub> laser and after confirmation of wound margins via frozen-section.</p><p id="p0260">Although emphasis has been placed on cryotherapy as a treatment for laryngeal cancer, several studies address the success of the use of cryotherapy as a treatment for papillomatosis.<xref rid="bib86" ref-type="bibr"><sup>86</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib90" ref-type="bibr"><sup>90</sup></xref></p><p id="p0265">Cryotherapy has the potential to be a medical treatment for laryngeal diseases resulting in minimal scarring and optimal postoperative voice quality and is therefore an important topic for future research. For one, future research should continue to look at the healing process of cryotherapy, exploring the healing environment produced and how it results in minimal scarring. Future research should also aim to explore how the use of cryotherapy could apply to other laryngeal diseases beyond cancer, while collecting more quantitative data regarding postoperative voice quality.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec6"><title>Conclusion</title><p id="p0270">Laryngologists today are faced with a difficult decision regarding treatment methods of laryngeal diseases. Through development of new technology and techniques, methods such as cold steel instrumentation, laser excision, photodynamic therapy, and cryotherapy must all be considered when deciding what is best for the patient. With each method having unique advantages and disadvantages, it is important for surgeons to have knowledge of each technique and its suitability for the situation at hand. Additionally, combinations of the above methods allow for mitigation of certain complications while preserving the overall benefits. Further advancement in cold instrumentation, laser technology, phototherapy, and cryotherapy will continue to refine the field of laryngology to improve outcomes while minimizing
