# PMC: adaptogen stress response HPA axis — Overview

Technical University (DTU) Food Institute’s risk assessments of <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic> (Ashwagandha) root [<xref rid="B52-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>,<xref rid="B53-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>], in which DTU identified possible thyroid and reproductive effects at high doses. These reports, which contributed to national-level restrictions and influenced regulatory positions in other countries, have been widely criticized by scientific and regulatory communities for methodological shortcomings and a lack of transparency [<xref rid="B56-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B127-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">127</xref>]. The Danish agency cited concerns about hormonal effects and lack of a well-defined safe dose, and then concluded that this focus was controversial. As noted by the Danish Veterinary and Food Administration, its report argued no safe dose could be proven with existing data, a conclusion some experts find unconvincing [<xref rid="B56-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B164-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">164</xref>]. Meanwhile, neither the EFSA [<xref rid="B165-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">165</xref>] nor the FDA has taken similar actions; Ashwagandha remains a permitted supplement in both regions [<xref rid="B164-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">164</xref>].</p><p>The views, scientific opinions, and conclusions drawn by Food and Drug authorities [<xref rid="B52-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>,<xref rid="B53-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>,<xref rid="B54-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>,<xref rid="B165-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">165</xref>,<xref rid="B166-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">166</xref>], dietary supplement manufacturers [<xref rid="B167-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">167</xref>,<xref rid="B168-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">168</xref>], experts in the field [<xref rid="B169-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">169</xref>,<xref rid="B170-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">170</xref>], distributors [<xref rid="B171-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">171</xref>], independent reviewers [<xref rid="B164-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">164</xref>], and researchers [<xref rid="B3-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="B35-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>] differ and are somewhat contradictory.</p><sec id="sec2dot5dot1-pharmaceuticals-18-01346"><title>2.5.1. Food and Drug Authorities</title><p>
<list list-type="bullet"><list-item><p>In April 2023, Denmark banned Withania, stating that it is impossible to find a safe dose given the current data. The ban was based on a finding in 2020 by the Danish Technical University (DTU) that ashwagandha may induce abortions and has a possibly harmful effect on thyroid and sex hormones [<xref rid="B52-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. This decision was based on only one study in rodents, which found that ashwagandha reduced sperm quality and quantity [<xref rid="B172-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">172</xref>], and one trial in humans suggested that ashwagandha might increase thyroid hormone levels [<xref rid="B173-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">173</xref>], which could potentially cause delirium, heart failure, and dehydration [<xref rid="B173-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">173</xref>].</p></list-item><list-item><p>In Report of the Expert Committee constituted by the Ministry of Ayush, Govt. of India. The Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences, the experts state that “<italic toggle="yes">Numerous safety studies consistently demonstrate that standardized Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root extract is safe for human consumption. The scientific data reveal that Ashwagandha root is well-tolerated across a wide range of doses, with no adverse outcomes reported in diverse demographic and clinical cohorts. Thus, the Expert Committee holds a strong view that the current evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of Ashwagandha root is robust, allowing it to be safely recommended and integrated into global health practices. This reinforces its standing as a safe herb in both traditional and contemporary health contexts; nevertheless, using correctly identified species is crucial, and stringent quality control should be carried out suitably in accordance with applicable regulatory norms. In view of its scientifically proven safety, the globally available and popular Indian herb Ashwagandha (Withania Somnifera) can be judiciously prescribed by the Ayurvedic physicians for its health benefits in humans</italic> [<xref rid="B54-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>].</p><p>

<italic toggle="yes">The report is not taking into consideration the different properties concerning function and safety between different plant parts of Ashwagandha</italic>
</p></list-item><list-item><label>○</label><p><italic toggle="yes">There is evolving evidence on the safety and efficacy of Ashwagandha published since 2020 (more than 400 papers), DTU report</italic> [<xref rid="B52-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">there are glaring instances of a lack of rigorous scientific scrutiny in the DTU risk assessment report, including conclusions drawn from publications of predatory journals”</italic> [<xref rid="B54-pharmaceuticals-18-01346" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>].</p></list-item></list>
</p></list-item><list-item><p>The National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM) studied whether herbal preparations containing <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic> are harmful to health, concluding that the herb can induce harmful effects in individuals who are sensitive to it.
