Oral administration is dominant, mainly for gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, parasitic infections, and fever, using decoctions, infusions, or juices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR109">109</xref>]. Topical application is the second most common, primarily for wounds, skin infections, inflammation, and bites, using species such as <italic toggle="yes">A. fruticosum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">D. angustifolia</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">O. lamiifolium</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>].</p><p id="Par192">Less frequent routes include ocular (<italic toggle="yes">A. pirottae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">S. coagulans</italic>) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>] and urogenital or anal applications (<italic toggle="yes">V. bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">H. linariifolium</italic>) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>]. Some species are versatile, administered through multiple routes depending on the ailment and plant part, such as <italic toggle="yes">A. pirottae</italic> (oral and ocular) and <italic toggle="yes">S. coagulans</italic> (oral and dermal).</p><p id="Par193">Thus, oral and dermal routes predominate in Somali traditional medicine, reflecting both therapeutic efficacy and cultural significance. The consistency of these administration patterns across global ethnomedicinal systems emphasizes a shared empirical reasoning in traditional healthcare. Understanding these practices preserves indigenous knowledge and informs future pharmacological validation and drug development.</p></sec><sec id="Sec34"><title>Use of fresh versus dried plant materials in Karamara forest patches</title><p id="Par194">In the Karamara forest patches, the majority of MPs were used in fresh form, while 20.3% were used dried, and a small proportion (1.7%) was employed in both forms. Fresh materials particularly leaves, fruits, and latex were preferred for treating wounds, digestive ailments, malaria, eye problems, and respiratory disorders. Commonly utilized species included <italic toggle="yes">A. fruticosum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. nubica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">V. tortilis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">D. angustifolia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">E. globulus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">O. lamiifolium</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Z. spina-christi</italic>.</p><p id="Par195">The preference for fresh plant parts reflects a widespread traditional belief that freshly harvested materials are more potent due to their higher content of active secondary metabolites, which may degrade during drying. Similar patterns have been documented across Ethiopia, where fresh leaves and latex are commonly used for both topical and internal treatments because of their perceived superior efficacy and immediate availability [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>].</p><p id="Par196">Dried plant materials were primarily used for roots, seeds, stems, and bark, which are often powdered, boiled, or crushed before administration. Drying facilitates storage, transportation, and preparation of decoctions or powders for long-term use. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">C. myricoides</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. arabica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">D. stramonium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">R. communis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">T. indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">W. somnifera</italic> were typically used in dried form. Comparable practices are reported in Kenya and Nigeria, where dried roots and bark are preferred for oral remedies targeting gastrointestinal and systemic disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].</p><p id="Par197">Certain species were used both fresh and dried, depending on seasonal availability and intended therapeutic purpose.
