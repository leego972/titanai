Nonetheless, the exhaustion of NK and T cells instead encourages tumor immune escape (<xref rid="B113" ref-type="bibr">113</xref>, <xref rid="B114" ref-type="bibr">114</xref>).</p><sec id="s3_1"><label>3.1</label><title>Current-day’ cancer immunotherapy</title><p>Cancer IOT has greatly evolved and now has various therapeutic approaches that are designed to mimic the natural antitumor immunity of the body in order to combat cancer and extend the patient’s life (<xref rid="B115" ref-type="bibr">115</xref>). The different therapeutic approaches are outlined below include IOT vaccines, adoptive cell therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors IOT, monoclonal antibodies IOT, and oncolytic virus therapy.</p><p>IOT vaccines: IOT vaccines for cancer treatment are developed to increase immune cells’ potential against cancer (<xref rid="B116" ref-type="bibr">116</xref>). They are divided into protein- and peptide-based vaccines (<xref rid="B117" ref-type="bibr">117</xref>), vector- and bacterial-based vaccines (<xref rid="B118" ref-type="bibr">118</xref>, <xref rid="B119" ref-type="bibr">119</xref>), nucleic acid (RNA, DNA, self-amplifying RNAs (saRNA), and mRNA)-based vaccines (<xref rid="B120" ref-type="bibr">120</xref>–<xref rid="B122" ref-type="bibr">122</xref>), and cellular (DC and whole cell)-based vaccines (<xref rid="B123" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>).</p><p>Adoptive cell therapies (ACTs): In ACT, the NK and T cells or other cells from the patient are grown and expanded through engineering or without engineering, then infused into the patient to fight cancer (<xref rid="B124" ref-type="bibr">124</xref>, <xref rid="B125" ref-type="bibr">125</xref>). The most common type of ACT is those derived from T-cells. The ACT based on T-cells can be accomplished by at least three unique T-cell approaches. Tumor-infiltrating-lymphocytes (TILs)-based ACT is the first approach, where endogenous TILs obtained from the patient tumors are grown <italic toggle="yes">ex vivo</italic> and injected into the patient (<xref rid="B125" ref-type="bibr">125</xref>, <xref rid="B126" ref-type="bibr">126</xref>). ACT, based on the fabricated T-cell receptor (TCR), is the second approach. This type of ACT helps recognize particular tumor antigens; however, it is restricted to Ags expressed by the major histocompatibility complex (<xref rid="B126" ref-type="bibr">126</xref>). ACT based on chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) is the third approach. Chimeric receptors comprising a domain for recognizing extracellular antigens, a signaling domain in the cytoplasm, and a transmembrane domain are engineered with T cells obtained from the patient, producing CAR T-cells that lock onto and eradicate the specific types of cancer (<xref rid="B127" ref-type="bibr">127</xref>, <xref rid="B128" ref-type="bibr">128</xref>).</p><p>Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs): Immunotherapy ICIs derived medication can boost tumor cell immune-mediated clearance procedure and obstruct the co-inhibitory signal pathways to reactivate immunity against the tumor (<xref rid="B129" ref-type="bibr">129</xref>, <xref rid="B130" ref-type="bibr">130</xref>). For example, some types of cancerous cells excessively secrete on their surface the programmed death-ligand (PD-L1) in order to avoid immune surveillance. This causes the activation of the “cytotoxicity brake” to continuously working, leading to T cell exhaustion and positive PD-L1 cancer cell survival (<xref rid="B131" ref-type="bibr">131</xref>). Also, PD-L1 and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) (<xref rid="B132" ref-type="bibr">132</xref>), T-cell immunoglobulin mucin-3 (TIM-3) (<xref rid="B133" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>), lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) (<xref rid="B134" ref-type="bibr">134</xref>, <xref rid="B135" ref-type="bibr">135</xref>), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) (<xref rid="B132" ref-type="bibr">132</xref>) are frequently used immune checkpoint proteins.</p><p>Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) IOT: mAbs are large-molecular-weight glycoproteins that are fabricated by B cells.
