These plants are valued not only for their medicinal effectiveness but also because their parts bulbs, leaves, seeds, bark, and fruits are relatively easy to harvest, process, store, and transport, enhancing their commercial viability. Similar trends have been observed in Tanzania’s Kariakoo Market, where non-woody herbs, seeds, and fruits dominate trade due to their durability and economic potential, providing income for vendors while meeting high community demand [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR182">182</xref>]. Likewise, in Western Kenya, <italic toggle="yes">Azadirachta indica</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Aloe</italic> species were among the most traded MPs, highlighting the regional importance of widely recognized and culturally accepted species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR154">154</xref>].</p><p id="Par136">Marketability in Sheikh District appeared to be influenced by several factors, including frequency of use for common ailments, simplicity of preparation, shelf life, and cultural acceptance. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> bulbs are widely sold due to their use in treating colds and flu, while fruits and roots of <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic> are traded for gastrointestinal and respiratory conditions. Conversely, plants with highly perishable parts, such as fresh leaves or latex (<italic toggle="yes">Aloe pirottae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>), are less frequently marketed and are typically collected directly from the wild when needed. This pattern mirrors other Ethiopian market studies, where plants intended for rare, acute, or specialized conditions are often accessed through traditional healers rather than through commercial outlets [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>].</p><p id="Par137">In Sheikh markets, leaves and fruits were the most commonly sold plant parts, followed by seeds, roots, and latex. This preference reflects practical considerations related to storage durability and transportation, a trend consistent with broader African markets where dried leaves and fruits dominate herbal trade due to their lower perishability [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>]. Roots and bark, despite their therapeutic significance, are less frequently traded due to sustainability concerns and the labor-intensive nature of their collection.</p><p id="Par138">The study also found that many MPs sold in Sheikh markets are traded primarily for non-medicinal purposes, such as food, spices, or household use. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Capsicum annuum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Lepidium sativum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Zingiber officinale</italic> are commonly marketed for culinary purposes, with medicinal applications considered secondary. This dual-use pattern has been documented in other East African and Ethiopian markets, where MPs often enter trade under the guise of edible or aromatic products [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR128">128</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR133">133</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR154">154</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR183">183</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR184">184</xref>].</p><p id="Par139">Although only a minority of MPs appear in formal markets, their trade contributes to household incomes, particularly for rural vendors, women traders, and traditional practitioners who rely on herbal commerce as a supplementary economic activity. Similar economic roles have been reported in urban African markets, where the sale of MPs supports livelihoods and underscores the continued importance of traditional medicine in primary healthcare [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>]. Additionally, species such as <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> also participate in broader regional and global herbal markets, reflecting increasing demand for natural and complementary therapies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR182">182</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR185">185</xref>].</p><p id="Par140">However, commercialization raises critical conservation concerns. Much of the traded material is harvested from wild populations, and unsustainable collection could threaten biodiversity and deplete valuable plant resources. Similar challenges have been documented in Tanzania, highlighting the need for regulated trade, sustainable harvesting practices, and conservation awareness to safeguard both ecological integrity and community well-being [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR182">182</xref>].</p><p id="Par141">Hence, while most MPs in Sheikh District are primarily used for subsistence healthcare, a meaningful fraction possesses commercial value.
