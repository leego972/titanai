# PMC: phytochemical bioactive compound anticancer — Overview

ref-type="bibr" rid="ref245">245</xref></sup> Ismail et al.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref247">247</xref></sup> reported the TPC of cantoloupe seeds as 285 mg GAE/100 g extract, where total flavonoids content was 162 μg/100 g extract. The highest phenolic content was found in cantaloupe leaf as 2640 mg GAE/100 g extract, followed by the stem (1025 mg GAE/100 g extract), skin (470 mg GAE/100 g extract), seed, and flesh (168 mg GAE/100 g extract). Cantaloupe leaf was also
the richest in total flavonoid content, 6970 μg/100 g extract. They also reported a high antioxidant activity in the leaf and stem
of cantaloupe fruit. For honeydew seeds, Zeb<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref248">248</xref></sup> reported 81.2 mg/100 g of total phenolics, which is much lower than
that of others discussed above. When the phenolic profile was investigated,
nine phenolics were identified, including caffeic acid, vanillic acid
derivatives, quercetin-3-rutinoside, ellagitanins, derivatives of
syringic, and ellagic acid. On the one hand, Galia melon seeds also
contained lower amounts of total phenolics (57.2 mg catechin/100 g
dw); on the other hand, watermelon seed is reported as a rich source
of phenolics, ∼969.3 mg catechin/100 g dw.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref249">249</xref></sup> As for golden melon (aka Cucurmis melo var. inodorus Canary)
seed, phenols, steroids, and flavonoids were the most abundant phytochemicals
representing 29.93 mg/100 g, 22.63 mg/100 g, and 20.67 mg/100 g of
the seed, respectively.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref250">250</xref></sup> Even though
golden melon seed contain some bioactives, the amount is still below
the other melon cultivars above. However, what we can conclude from
these studies is melon seeds exhibit a good potential of extracting
phytochemicals for valorization in different industries. In addition
to phenolics, melon seed oil also exhibited similar properties of
vegetable oils, including soybean and sunflower oils.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref251">251</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref252">252</xref></sup> Therefore, melon seeds may be great alternatives for the production
of novel functional foods with a wide range of benefits.</p><p>Besides
seeds, the juice, peel, and pulp of the Piel de Sapo cultivar (<italic toggle="yes">Cucumis melo</italic> var. inodorus H. Jacq.) were investigated with
respect to their phenolics, carotenoids, and vitamin C content. The
results showed that peels and seeds contained similar amounts of phenolics,
and they presented the greatest values, 364 μg/g and 393 μg/g,
respectively. On the other hand, the phenolic content of melon juice
was found as 211 μg/g, and the pulp has 221 μg/g of phenolics,
which is a valuable byproduct of juice processing. They also found
similar values of vitamin C for juice, pulp, and peel, where seeds
had lower values. Although there are some phenolics and vitamin C
present, any type of carotenoid was not identified in Piel de Sapo
melon.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref253">253</xref></sup> Unlike Piel de Sapo, cantaloupe
melon contained 68.92 mg/g carotenoids in its pulp and 49.9 mg/g in
its juice; however, total phenolics in the juice and the pulp was
significantly lower than Piel de Sapo (95.35 μg/g and 101.9
μg/g, respectively).<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref244">244</xref></sup></p><p>As
for peels and seeds, they have contributed almost the half of
the phenolics and antioxidant activity, and peel itself contained
31% of the TPC, which is comparable with the phenolics in juice (35%).<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref244">244</xref></sup> Thanks to the high phenolic content of melons,
peels are good sources of gallic acid, ellagic acid, and kaempferol
along with other phenolics found in the seeds (ferulic acid, kaempferol,
and gallic acid).<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref254">254</xref></sup> Watermelon peel is
reported to be the highest phenol-containing byproduct among different
kinds of fruit peels including pawpaw, orange, pineapple, banana,
apple, mango, and pomegranate,<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref1">1</xref></sup> and the
TPC of watermelon peels has been reported as 335 mg catechin/100 g
dw by Duda-Chodak and Tarko (2007);<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref249">249</xref></sup> it
mainly contained gallic acid, catechin, ellagic acid, and kaempferol,
which have been reported to have antiviral, antimutagenic, anticarcinogenic,
and cytotoxic effects, along with a shade of cinnamic acid, ferrulic
acid, chlorogenic acid, rutin, and some others.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref255">255</xref>−<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="ref257">257</xref></sup></p><p>Various studies in the literature have evaluated the bioactive
compounds in wastes of different melon cultivars, and they all agreed
on the presence of valuable bioactive compounds, which are promising
sources of natural food additives. However, there are limited studies
on melon processing wastes; therefore, utilization of melon juice
processing byproducts should be investigated in a deeper and more
detailed sense to clarify possible market opportunities.
