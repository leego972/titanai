Multimodal Fusion Approaches and Research Gaps</title><p>Given that fundus photographs and OCT scans each provide complementary information about glaucoma, an important question is whether combining them can lead to even better diagnostic performance. In clinical practice, ophthalmologists often examine the optic disc (via fundus exam) and OCT analysis together before deciding if a patient has glaucoma [<xref rid="B5-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>].</p><p>Fundus images show the optic disc cupping, pallor, and peripapillary signs, whereas OCT quantifies layer thickness and structural loss [<xref rid="B29-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. <xref rid="sensors-25-04337-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref> shows images of an eye with no diseases (a,b) and of an eye with glaucoma (c,d) from each modality (fundus and OCT).</p><p>To our knowledge, this is the first study to leverage one-to-one correspondence between fundus and OCT images for every eye, allowing us to extract and fuse complementary surface- and depth-based features. By using matched pairs graded by senior ophthalmologists at Bangladesh Eye Hospital, our approach captures both optic nerve head morphology and retinal nerve fibre layer structure synchronously, enhancing the model’s ability to detect subtle, early glaucomatous changes that might be missed when modalities are considered independently.</p><p>However, until recently, most deep learning studies treated these modalities separately. One reason is the lack of publicly available datasets with paired fundus and OCT images for the same eyes [<xref rid="B30-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. Another challenge is architectural: how to fuse two very different image modalities in a network. Early attempts to bridge modalities did so indirectly. <xref rid="sensors-25-04337-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref> shows a summary of prior deep learning studies for glaucoma detection in the last few years which used either fundus or OCT as input for glaucoma detection.</p><p>Multimodal glaucoma detection has started to emerge in the last couple of years. One recent study by Hwang et al. (2025) introduced a multimodal CNN that processes time-matched fundus photographs, OCT scans, and visual field maps together for glaucoma detection [<xref rid="B31-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>]. Their architecture contained three parallel CNN branches (one for each modality) whose learned feature embeddings were concatenated and passed to a fusion network (multi-layer perceptron) to output glaucoma vs. normal classification [<xref rid="B31-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>]. Trained on a dataset of 706 eyes with all three modalities available, the multimodal model outperformed single-modality models significantly. Notably, on their clinical test set, the fundus-only model had AUC 0.57 and the OCT-only model, 0.74; the performance was likely lower here due to the real-world, heterogeneous data, whereas the fused model reached AUC 0.86 [<xref rid="B31-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>]. This demonstrates that even when individual modalities are noisy or limited, combining structural (OCT) and functional (field) information with fundus images yields a much more robust predictor.</p><p>A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Ling et al. (2025) reported consistently high performance of deep learning models for glaucoma detection across imaging modalities [<xref rid="B32-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>]. Specifically, in their review, algorithms using fundus photographs achieved a pooled sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 93%, with an area under the ROC curve (AUROC) around 0.90 [<xref rid="B32-sensors-25-04337" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>].
