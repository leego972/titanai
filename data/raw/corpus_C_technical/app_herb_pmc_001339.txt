The study identified multiple mechanisms of action, including membrane depolarization, intracellular pH reduction, decreased bacterial protein content, DNA cleavage, and cytoplasmic leakage, confirming its ability to effectively inhibit bacterial growth [<xref rid="B113-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">113</xref>].</p><p>Finally, a comparative study of <italic toggle="yes">A. spinosus</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Tridax procumbens</italic> revealed significant antimicrobial properties against various bacterial strains, including antibiotic-resistant ones. Hydroethanolic extracts from <italic toggle="yes">T. procumbens</italic> exhibited the highest activity, with inhibition zones ranging from 9 to 31 mm, except against <italic toggle="yes">C. albicans</italic> 1581. Meanwhile, <italic toggle="yes">A. spinosus</italic> inhibited 11 of the 15 tested strains, with inhibition zones between 7 and 17.5 mm. MIC values ranged from 0.39 to 3.12 mg/mL, with <italic toggle="yes">T. procumbens</italic> showing superior potency, underscoring its potential for therapeutic applications [<xref rid="B114-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">114</xref>].</p><p>These studies collectively reinforce the potential of <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus</italic> spp. as a natural source of antimicrobial agents with applications in both medicine and food preservation. Antimicrobial activity varies depending on the species, extract type, and growth conditions, suggesting that future research should focus on optimizing their use by identifying and characterizing the most relevant active compounds.</p></sec><sec id="sec7dot2-nutrients-17-01749"><title>7.2. Antioxidant Activity</title><p>The antioxidant potential of <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus</italic> spp. is attributed to their diverse phytochemical composition, which includes vitamins, tocopherols, polyphenols, and squalene, compounds known for their ability to counteract oxidative stress [<xref rid="B115-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">115</xref>,<xref rid="B116-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">116</xref>]. Several studies have explored the antioxidant properties of amaranth, analyzing its bioactive compounds, processing effects, and mechanisms of action.</p><p>Cooking methods significantly influence the antioxidant activity of amaranth grains. A comparative study between boiling and roasting revealed that roasting enhances the total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant properties, whereas boiling leads to higher starch hydrolysis, but reduces antioxidant potential. Specifically, roasted intact grains exhibited a TPC of 662.78 mg GAE/100 g sample, compared to a 470.21 mg GAE/100 g sample in boiled grains, underscoring the potential of roasting to improve amaranth’s antioxidant capacity [<xref rid="B89-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">89</xref>].</p><p>The antioxidant potential of four <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus</italic> spp.—<italic toggle="yes">A. viridis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. spinosus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. tricolor</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">A. lividus</italic>—was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS assays.
