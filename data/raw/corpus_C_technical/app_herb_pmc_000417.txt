Nonetheless, with herbal medicine-induced labour, monitoring of women is often out of the question due to self-prescription.<xref rid="R2" ref-type="bibr">2 19</xref> So, the use of herbal medicines for IOL is likely to be riskier and it is plausibly an important factor influencing adverse pregnancy outcomes in settings where its use is common.</p><p>In vitro studies have confirmed that some of the herbal medicines used during pregnancy have oxytocic properties.<xref rid="R13" ref-type="bibr">13 20</xref> For instance, a study in Nigeria found that several plants that are used to facilitate childbirth in the country significantly induced muscle cell contractility.<xref rid="R13" ref-type="bibr">13</xref> However, safety is the main concern as many of the herbal medicines are believed to be poisonous and may contribute to maternal and neonatal mortality as well as morbidity.<xref rid="R21" ref-type="bibr">21 22</xref> To date, there is mixed evidence from population-based studies regarding the efficacy and safety of herbal medicines for IOL<xref rid="R23" ref-type="bibr">23–25</xref> and yet available data have not been systematically evaluated and synthesised to provide the rigorous evidence necessary to inform decisions. Lack of high quality and consistent data on efficacy and safety of herbal medicines makes recommendations and regulations challenging.<xref rid="R5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref> Consequently, we conducted a systematic review to explore the effectiveness and safety of herbal medicines for IOL.
