Of these patients, 1 had hypopigmentation from both AFXL-dPDT and dPDT, 1 only from dPDT treatment, and for 1 patient the side with hypopigmentation was not defined.</p></sec></sec><sec sec-type="discussion" id="sec3"><title>DISCUSSION</title><p>These results confirm the efficacy of AFXL-dPDT in a randomized setting with, to our knowledge, the highest number of patients published to date. Complete clearance at 6 months was achieved for 50% of patients in AFXL-dPDT, which was significantly higher than for dPDT alone (30%). Two previous studies on AFXL-dPDT reported CC values; in the first on 46 patients, 72% achieved CC in artificial AFXL-dPDT (<xref rid="CIT0013" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>), and in the other on 12 patients 76% achieved CC in natural AFXL-dPDT, and 64% in ndPDT alone (<xref rid="CIT0015" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>). The CC of the current patients was slightly lower compared with these studies; however, the current follow-up was longer (6 vs 3–4 months) (<xref rid="CIT0013" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>–<xref rid="CIT0016" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>). The patients recruited to the current study were mostly severely photodamaged and difficult-to-treat; 73% of the current patients had had previous field treatments for AKs (<xref rid="T0001" ref-type="table">Table I</xref>) and 9% were immunosuppressed.</p><p>Regarding lesion-specific clearance of the current study, 86% of lesions in AFXL-dPDT, and 70% of lesions in dPDT cleared completely. In a previous study on 18 patients, the LSC-results were comparable to those of the current study; 81% of lesions receiving AFXL-ndPDT cleared completely (<xref rid="CIT0014" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>). However, they did not present clinically relevant patient-specific clearance results.</p><p>To our knowledge, only 1 previous RCT on adPDT (<xref rid="CIT0006" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>) has been published, in which adPDT with an operating room light-emitting diode (LED) light is compared with ndPDT. In the half-side comparative study on 22 patients, the amount of AKs reduced equally on both treatments (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.21); by 62% in ndPDT and 68% in adPDT at 1 month. In the current study, the outcomes of adPDT and ndPDT were also similar (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &lt; 0.09), although a trend towards superior CC rates for ndPDT was seen in AFXL-dPDT (<xref rid="T0002" ref-type="table">Table II</xref>). Overall, adPDT appears to be as effective as ndPDT in the treatment of AKs.</p><p>AFXL creates microscopic channels in the tissue, which aids the absorption of photosensitizer and, in addition, has direct toxic effects on cells (<xref rid="CIT0011" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>). AFXL increases treatment efficacy (<xref rid="CIT0011" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>) and possibly also the treatment response for thicker AKs. In the current study, grade I lesions and grade II–III lesions achieved similar clearance in AFXL-dPDT (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.84); however, these clearance rates were also similar in dPDT (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> = 0.10). Although statistically insignificant, the proportion of completely cleared lesions of grade I (87%) compared with grade II–III (85%) were similar in AFXL-dPDT, whilst in dPDT a higher proportion of grade I lesions cleared completely (73%) compared with grade II–III lesions (63%).</p><p>To further increase the clinical relevance of the study, the outcomes of AFXL-dPDT were additionally compared with cPDT in our previous study branch, in which we compared cPDT with pulsed-dye laser-mediated PDT in a half-side comparative manner on 59 patients with an equal recruitment and inclusion process (<xref rid="CIT0018" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>).
