An analysis of the altitudinal gradient was made in 1000 m intervals as 60 – 1000 m, 1000 – 2000 m, 2000 – 3000 m, and 3000 – 4000 m.</p></sec></sec><sec><title>Results and discussion</title><sec><title>History and plant usage</title><p>Plants have been one of the most important sources of food and medicine since the dawn of human civilization. Archaeological evidence of 60,000 year-old Neanderthal burial grounds in Shanidar, Iraq, points to the use of plants like Marshmallow, Yarrow and Groundsel, which are still used in contemporary folk medicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B33">33</xref>]. Evidence for the medicinal use of <italic toggle="yes">Papaver somniferum</italic>, the opium poppy, dates back 8000 years [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B34">34</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B35">35</xref>]. Concomitantly, the earliest written record of plants used as medicine originating from the Himalayas are found in the 6,500 year old texts of the Rigveda [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B36">36</xref>], followed by Atharveveda (2000-1000 BC) and Auryveda (600-100 BC) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B37">37</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B38">38</xref>]. The oldest account recording the uses of 278 Nepalese medicinal plants is 'Saushrut Nighantu,' written in 878 AD (935 BS). Later 'Nepali Nighantu,' an elaborated encyclopedia with information on the traditional knowledge of 750 plant species was published by the Royal Nepal Academy in 1969 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B39">39</xref>].</p><p>Until the middle of the 19<sup>th </sup>century, plants were the main therapeutic agents used by humans, and even today almost 80% of the world population rely to some extent on medicinal plants for their primary healthcare needs. The use of nearly 3000 plant species as food during the course of human civilization has been documented, but only about 150 species have been cultivated [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B40">40</xref>] and less that 10 plant species are meeting over 90% of the world food demand [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B41">41</xref>]. Human survival still can not be imagined without plants. The importance of plants is substantial and reflected in the large variety of products such as food, fiber, fodder, vegetables, medicinal plants, and aromatic plants. The contribution of medicinal plants in Nepal is important [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B42">42</xref>]. A total of 1012 useful plants are documented for Nepal [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>], of which 554 (54.74%) have ethnomedicinal in properties. An review of research conducted in the Nepal Himalayas indicated that nearly 40% of the studies were related to medicinal plants and ethnomedicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B43">43</xref>], underlining that ethnomedicine is utmost important in the Nepal Himalayas.</p></sec><sec><title>Medicinal plants and their distribution</title><p>About 60% of the world population and 60–90% of the population of developing countries (80% in Nepal [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B44">44</xref>], 70% in India, 80% in Pakistan, 65% in Sri Lanka, 90% in Bangladesh, 85% in Burma, and 60% in Indonesia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B45">45</xref>] rely on traditional medicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B46">46</xref>], and about 85% of the traditional remedies for primary health care are derived from plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B47">47</xref>].</p><p>In the world, 10–18% of all plant species are used medicinally [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B48">48</xref>] while in Nepal medicinal plants account about 20–28% of the local flora [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B49">49</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B54">54</xref>]. The data for India and China is significantly higher i.e. 44% and 29–41% respectively [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B55">55</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B56">56</xref>] (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T1">1</xref>). The present analysis found that an average of 56% of higher plants were ethnobotanically important, and 54% were used as ethnomedicine in the Nepal Himalayas (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T2">2</xref>). This indicates that there are more ethnobotanically and ethnomedicinally important plant species in the Nepal Himalayas than previously estimated.
