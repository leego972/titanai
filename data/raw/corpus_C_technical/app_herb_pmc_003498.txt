Local communities further distinguish vegetation into three land-cover types: mountain and highland vegetation, coastal plain vegetation, and plateau vegetation. These categories are consistently recognized throughout the district and guide the collection, management, and cultural use of medicinal plants.</p><p id="Par20">Mountain and highland areas are characterized by evergreen, shrub-dominated vegetation growing mainly on rocky black soils locally known as carro-madow. Natural woody vegetation is widespread, while irrigated agriculture remains limited. Common MPs found in this zone include <italic toggle="yes">Albizia anthelmintica</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Aloe citrina</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Cadia purpurea</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Cordia gharaf</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Dodonaea angustifolia</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Olea somaliensis</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Terminalia prunioides</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par21">The coastal plain and piedmont zones represent low-lying arid environments with sandy, nutrient-poor soils and minimal rainfall. Vegetation is sparse and dominated by drought- and heat-adapted species such as <italic toggle="yes">Balanites latifolia</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Cissus rotundifolia</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Neltuma juliflora</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Solanum obbiadense</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus hamur</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par22">Plateau landscapes cover the largest proportion of the study area and are mainly composed of red sandy soils that are highly prone to erosion. Vegetation in this zone consists largely of thorny trees, shrubs, and herbaceous species. Dominant medicinal taxa include several <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia</italic> species (<italic toggle="yes">V. bussei</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> V. mellifera</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> S. senegal</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> V. tortilis</italic>), along with <italic toggle="yes">Aloe somaliensis</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Calotropis procera</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Commiphora myrrha</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Cordeauxia edulis</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Euphorbia spp.</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Momordica charantia</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Senna italica</italic>. These ecological patterns are consistent with previous vegetation assessments conducted in Somaliland [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par23">

</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Reconnaissance survey, study site, and informant selection</title><p id="Par24">A preliminary reconnaissance survey was conducted from 1 January to 1 February 2023 to obtain baseline information and to support the systematic selection of representative study sites within Sheikh District. During this phase, initial data were gathered from the Sheikh District Office of Agriculture and complemented through field consultations with local elders, kebele leaders, forest guards, and other knowledgeable community members. These discussions were essential for understanding locally recognized vegetation patterns, settlement characteristics, and the presence of active traditional healing practices.</p><p id="Par25">Sheikh District was purposively selected for the present ethnobotanical investigation because of its relatively high plant diversity, the strong reliance of indigenous communities on MPs for primary healthcare, and the limited availability of formal conservation or management initiatives aimed at protecting these culturally important plant resources.</p><p id="Par26">Based on recommendations from community elders and local administrative leaders, together with considerations of vegetation cover, ecological variation, and agroclimatic conditions, 12 kebeles were purposively chosen from the 27 kebeles found in the district. These included: Galooley, Gidhays, Girni, Gugux, Hundisa, Kalabaydh, Kalasharka, Kulmiye, Laalays, Shacabka, Suuq-sade, and Tuulo-dhibiijo. These settlements were identified as areas where traditional medicinal knowledge remains actively practiced and where medicinal plant availability is relatively high. The geographic distribution of these kebeles is presented in the study area map (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>), and detailed settlement-level information is provided in the corresponding location table.</p><p id="Par27">Informants were selected using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling methods, following established ethnobotanical research approaches [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>].
