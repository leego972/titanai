# PMC: optical coherence tomography clinical application — Overview

GRIN lens was added to the inside of a plastic ear speculum tip that attached to a commercial RCA-based video otoscope without modifying any of the internal components. Modifying an existing commercial product to expand functionality also greatly hastened the development of this system. The lens properties were chosen to focus light through the ear canal and onto and through the TM of a human subject. An LCI system that utilized a laptop for control and processing was constructed and placed into a portable cart for use in the otolaryngologist specialist office (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="f5">Fig. 5</xref>). This system provided single A-scans at a rate of 35 kHz through the TM and into any content within the middle ear (effusion and biofilm). Modified and reprinted with permission.<xref rid="r71" ref-type="bibr"><sup>71</sup></xref></p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="JBO-022-121715-g005.jpg"/></fig></sec><sec id="sec3.5"><label>3.5</label><title>First-Generation Integrated Handheld Probe for Primary Care</title><p>Considering the regular use of both the otoscope and ophthalmoscope in the primary care office, it was realized that it would be possible to expand imaging to include the eye (both anterior and posterior), as well as the skin and oral cavity. A system with this expanded capability could improve upon not only the ophthalmoscope or otoscope but also provide a more comprehensive point-of-care imaging system for all the tissue sites commonly examined in primary care. While the ophthalmoscope can be used for this purpose, it only provides a surface view of the retina, and is difficult to use effectively. With the depth-resolved capabilities of OCT, an integrated system could provide more comprehensive ocular exams, or early screening for diabetic retinopathy (DBR), which slowly causes patients to lose their vision.<xref rid="r73" ref-type="bibr"><sup>73</sup></xref> Vision loss occurs through either the leakage of fluid into the macula, which eventually causes macular edema (nonproliferative DBR), or from the growth of new vessels on the retina with resultant scar tissue that restricts vision, and may eventually lead to a detached retina (proliferative DBR). In either case, early detection of these microstructural changes could allow for more immediate preventative action to be taken, mediated through drugs or other more invasive treatments. An expanded system would also potentially allow for examination of common skin diseases and screening for cancers, such as melanoma or basal cell carcinoma (BCC).<xref rid="r74" ref-type="bibr"><sup>74</sup></xref> Oral cancer and dental screening could also benefit patients, as oral caries and gingivitis alone pose a large financial burden and are extremely common.<xref rid="r75" ref-type="bibr"><sup>75</sup></xref></p><p>This portable and multisite primary care imaging system and handheld probe, shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="f6">Fig. 6</xref>, enabled optical characterization of these different sites with interchangeable attachments to the probe tip. An interchangeable adapter used a modified cutout that simulated the device head on a commercial otoscope, such that it would be possible to use standard otoscope tips for TM imaging. To observe the skin, oral cavity, or cornea, a metal standoff and eye cup were used, if applicable, which also had the same fitment. To image the retina, an additional adjustable lens and eyecup was developed, along with a flip mirror in the reference arm to have the proper (longer) pathlength. Suitable dispersion compensation to improve image quality was also added. This initial system and probe design lacked a way to visualize the real-time acquired video and OCT scans on the handheld probe, and had the optical patch cable exposed near the grip, both of which increased the difficulty of using the probe. Although the tradeoffs from the combination and implementation of this hardware were apparent during use, this system and handheld probe was the first of its kind.</p><fig id="f6" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Fig. 6</label><caption><p>Schematic diagram and photograph of the first-generation primary care portable OCT system and handheld scanner.
