This, along with a higher preference for biomedical services amongst the younger generation, could result in the erosion of medicinal plant knowledge. Ethnobotanical knowledge erosion has been observed in the Middle East both among herbalists [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>] and the general population [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>]. The diffusion of non-local knowledge about medicinal plants through mass media is characteristic to urban settings [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>] and has a homogenizing effect on oral pharmacopoeias [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>]. Mass media often disseminates information on the uses and properties of commercial plants, increasing their visibility [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR66">66</xref>] and, alongside availability factors, could also contribute to explain the high proportion of food and spices used among the Meccan women interviewed.</p><p id="Par36">Mass media is also used in Saudi Arabia to communicate biomedical education programs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>]. Although these campaigns may be necessary, they favour biomedical knowledge over traditional therapies. Loss of ethnomedicinal and ethnobotanical knowledge may result from different treatment preferences between generations. Higher preference for biomedical treatments among younger Meccan women reflects the same trend among rural Arabic populations elsewhere in Saudi Arabia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>]. Although Press [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>] argues that the disregard of faith and the role of family in biomedical diagnosis and treatment are often sufficient to hamper the utilisation of biomedicine, Ghazanfar notes that in the Arabian Peninsula, ‘modern and traditional medicine may be tried [simultaneously], or if one fails the other will be tried – but where modern medicine achieves results, traditional medicine tends to disappear’ [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>, p. 1]. Biomedicine in Islamic countries integrates faith and has a religious viewpoint on caring [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>], but people may still prefer home remedies for treating minor ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>] as observed in this study. Even when biomedicine is growing and herbal remedies may be in decline, medicinal plant use still plays an important role in urban health care [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>].</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec20"><title>Conclusion</title><p id="Par37">We join Emery and Hurley [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR67">67</xref>] in highlighting the vibrant botanical knowledge and practices in urban areas. Women in Mecca are the primary household health carers and hold a singular, lay body of medicinal plant knowledge to treat mostly common ailments. Plant availability in shops and markets, as well as religious texts, seem to play an important role shaping the urban medicinal flora of women in Mecca; we highlight the important medicinal role in urban environments of foods, spices and traded plants in general. Much of this knowledge had not yet been documented, and gender and geographical biases in research may account for the under-representation of urban women’s knowledge in Saudi Arabian medicinal plant literature.</p><p id="Par38">However, medicinal plant knowledge among Meccan women may be eroded and changed with the spread of new knowledge through mass media and preference for biomedical care. Documentation efforts are urgent for the preservation of the diversity of medicinal plant knowledge in the Arabian Peninsula. We propose that scientifically rigorous ethnobotanical and ethnomedicinal research ‘acknowledging the sociocultural heterogeneity within the community being researched’ [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>, p. 242] in Islamic settings can be achieved by teams with both female and male ethnobiologists. Al-Sodany et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>] have reported that medicinal plants in rural Saudi Arabia are vastly under-documented, but so far, ethnobotanical enquiry of women’s medicinal plants has been even more overlooked.</p></sec></body><back><glossary><title>Abbreviations</title><def-list><def-item><term>MW</term><def><p id="Par5">Meccan women</p></def></def-item><def-item><term>NA</term><def><p id="Par6">Not available</p></def></def-item></def-list></glossary><ack><title>Acknowledgements</title><p>We are truly thankful to the 32 adult women who participated in this study and for sharing their time and knowledge.
