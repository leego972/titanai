Ashwagandha demonstrates sleep-promoting and circadian-modulating properties with evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies, but further long-term trials are necessary to confirm the durability and safety of these effects.</p><p>Anti-inflammatory pathways</p><p>Ashwagandha exhibits both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, making it a promising intervention for disorders driven by oxidative and inflammatory stress [<xref rid="REF48" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>]. These effects are primarily mediated through modulation of NF-κB and Nrf2 signaling pathways, which are critical for immune balance and redox homeostasis [<xref rid="REF49" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>]. Withaferin A, a major bioactive constituent, inhibits NF-κB activation by preventing IκBα degradation and blocking NF-κB p65 translocation to the nucleus. This leads to reduced expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and COX-2 [<xref rid="REF50" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>]. Concurrently, ashwagandha activates the Nrf2-ARE pathway, which upregulates antioxidant enzymes including HO-1, SOD, and GPx [<xref rid="REF51" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>]. Experimental data reinforce these mechanisms. In vitro studies with neuronal and immune cells show that ashwagandha lowers intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), stabilizes mitochondrial membranes, and enhances cell survival under oxidative stress [<xref rid="REF22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>]. Animal studies further demonstrate protection against glial activation, blood-brain barrier disruption, and neuroinflammation in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced neurotoxicity models [<xref rid="REF5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. These actions are particularly relevant where the nervous and immune systems intersect, such as in neurodegenerative and autoimmune disorders. Ashwagandha’s ability to simultaneously regulate transcription factors and inflammatory mediators underscores its dual benefit for both immune and neural cells [<xref rid="REF14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>]. Because cytokines, ROS, and NF-κB serve as shared mediators between these systems, such regulation is crucial in conditions involving immune-neural dysfunction [<xref rid="REF35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>]. Despite robust preclinical evidence, clinical validation is limited. Large-scale human trials are still needed to confirm efficacy, optimize dosing, and translate these findings into therapeutic strategies for inflammatory and oxidative stress-related disorders. Ashwagandha modulates NF-κB and Nrf2 signaling to reduce inflammation and strengthen antioxidant defenses. Across trials, results are generally supportive, though study designs vary in dosage, formulation, duration, and participant demographics. One RCT enrolled 64 chronically stressed adults who received 600 mg/day of KSM-66 root extract for 60 days, resulting in a 27.9% decrease in serum cortisol and significant reductions in stress scores compared with placebo [<xref rid="REF33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. In patients with generalized anxiety disorder, ashwagandha treatment for six weeks reduced HAM-A scores by 56% of lorazepam’s effect, but without sedation [<xref rid="REF34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>]. Sleep-related benefits were demonstrated in a study of 150 adults with insomnia, where 600 mg/day of root extract for eight weeks significantly improved sleep onset, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency (p &lt; 0.01) [<xref rid="REF45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>]. Cognitive benefits have also been reported: in individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), 300-500 mg twice daily for eight to 12 weeks enhanced memory, executive function, and processing speed [<xref rid="REF30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>].</p><p>Formulation and dosing patterns vary across trials. Most clinical studies use KSM-66 (aqueous root extract) or Sensoril (root-leaf blend with higher withanolide content) [<xref rid="REF17" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="REF18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. KSM-66 is primarily studied for stress and endurance outcomes, while Sensoril has been tested for metabolic and cognitive endpoints.
