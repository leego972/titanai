Volatile Oil</title><p>The volatile oil extracted from the roots and the aerial parts (stems and leaves) of <italic toggle="yes">H. japonicum</italic> were analyzed by GC-MS, and thirty-two and forty-three constituents were identified, respectively. As the results show, there was obvious chemical variability in the volatile oil composition: the contents of six chemotyppes including hydrocarbons, alcohols (phenols), aldehydes/ketones, acids, esters and amines were 31.92%, 11.47%, 9.95%, 0.56%, 40.03% and 4.13% in the roots, respectively, and 62.16%, 8.12%, 2.72%, 1.24%, 18.96% and 5.75% in the aerial parts, respectively [<xref rid="B25-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. The main volatile components that represented more than 5% of the total volatile oils are summarized in <xref rid="molecules-19-10733-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>.</p></sec><sec><title>2.6. Metallic Elements</title><p>Fifteen rare earth elements in wild <italic toggle="yes">H. japonicum</italic> materials were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The results showed that the concentrations of La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Yb, Tm, Lu and Y ranged from 6 to 14522 ng/g, and among them the concentrations of La, Ce and Nd were higher than 2,000 ng/g [<xref rid="B26-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>]. In addition, the contents of other five metallic elements, Zn, Cu, Pb, Cr and Cd were measured by flame atomic absorption spectrophotmetry (FAAS) [<xref rid="B27-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>].</p></sec></sec><sec><title>3. Pharmacology</title><sec><title>3.1. Antioxidant Activity</title><p>The antioxidant effect of <italic toggle="yes">H. japonicum</italic> is one of the most prominent effects due to its responsibility for many of the other activities. The aqueous extracts of <italic toggle="yes">H. japonicum</italic> have been demonstrated to have obvious antioxidant activity by molybdenum reduction, DPPH scavenging, β-carotene bleaching inhibition and lipid peroxidation inhibition methods with values of 37.28 ± 0.54 μg/mg, IC<sub>50</sub> = 77.7 ± 5.6 μg, 83.18% and 95.38%, respectively. By reducing the generation of hydroxyl radicals, the aqueous extract effectively reduced the oxidative damage of the DNA [<xref rid="B28-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>].</p><p>Quercetin-7-O-α-<sc>l</sc>-rhamnoside is one of main flavonoids in <italic toggle="yes">H. japonicum</italic>. Oral treatment with quercetin-7-O-α-<sc>l</sc>-rhamnoside (0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 mg/kg) in bile duct ligation-injured liver fibrosis rats showed increases of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) level, and a decrease of malondialdehyde (MDA) content in liver. <italic toggle="yes">In vitro</italic>, inhibiting the overexpression of ROS and GSH depletion is a very important reason for quercetin-7-O-α-<sc>l</sc>-rhamnoside to attenuate L-02 cell injury induced by glycochenodeoxycholic acid [<xref rid="B29-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>].</p></sec><sec><title>3.2. Hepatoprotective Activity</title><p>Total bilirubin (TBIL), alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) in serum are three markers for liver function. After intraperitoneal administration with ethanol and ethyl acetate extract of <italic toggle="yes">H. japonicum</italic> (0.2, 0.6 and 1.8 g raw materials/kg) to rats with acute liver injury induced by D-aminogalactose, the levels of ALT and AST in all treatment groups were reduced remarkably when compared to those in the model group [<xref rid="B30-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. The aqueous extract (4.5 g raw material/kg) exhibited an obvious effect by decreasing AST, ALT and TBIL levels in serum of mice with liver injury induced by CCl<sub>4</sub>, indicating its hepatoprotective effects [<xref rid="B31-molecules-19-10733" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>].
