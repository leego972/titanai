Variability in Phytochemical Compositions of A. saligna</title><p>Plants biosynthesise secondary metabolites for survival and defence. The outcome of biosynthesis is influenced by environmental factors, including soil, salinity, temperature, and geographical variations [<xref rid="B30-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. Environmental stresses, such as drought and high salinity, can stimulate the signalling pathway in the plant’s cells to accumulate bioactive compounds in order to respond to severe conditions. For instance, <italic toggle="yes">D</italic>-pinitol <bold>48</bold> (methoxy inositol) was found in Australian <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> [<xref rid="B26-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>]. This suggested that plants grown in water shortage and high salinity areas produced the compounds as osmoprotectants to overcome the osmotic stress. A study by Streeter et al. [<xref rid="B31-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>] confirmed that <italic toggle="yes">D</italic>-pinitol <bold>48</bold> (methoxy inositol) in stressed soybean leaves was around 40% higher in concentration than in the well-watered groups. </p><p>Moreover, studies on drought and salinity stresses showed significantly elevated levels of gallic acid <bold>25</bold> and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> in <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic>. For example, the leaves harvested from the Albahah region of Southwest Saudi Arabia yielded (reported in % of the total content) gallic acid <bold>25</bold> (0.0054) and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> (0.0008) [<xref rid="B17-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. In contrast, the leaves collected from the Orman Botanical Garden in Giza, Egypt yielded a much higher content of gallic acid <bold>25</bold> (19.2) and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> (6.4) [<xref rid="B29-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. The Albahah region is a dry area with an annual mean rainfall of 142.6 mm/year [<xref rid="B32-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>] and a medium to high soil salinity [<xref rid="B33-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>], while the Giza site is an arid area estimated to have 1.2 mm/year of annual rainfall [<xref rid="B34-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>] and a high soil salinity [<xref rid="B35-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>]. These findings suggest the influences of abiotic stresses in phenolic biosynthesis.</p><p>Furthermore, plants have been known to produce high concentrations of flavonoids and glycosides when they experience environmental stresses such as low or high temperatures, high intensity of light, high salinity, and drought [<xref rid="B36-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>]. These flavonoids were identified (with variable quantities) in the extracts from various parts of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> plants from Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Tunisia, South Africa, and Australia, wherein the plants shared similar growing conditions and environments.</p><p>Our data in <xref rid="app1-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="app">Supplementary Table S1</xref> revealed variations in the quantities and types of polyphenolic compounds. It is noteworthy that three main groups (flavonoids, cinnamic acids, and benzoic acids) of compounds were commonly identified (with variable quantities) across the plant parts, geographic origins (growing conditions), and polarity of the extraction solvents and analytic methods. Although most articles did not mention the collection period and phenological stage, the abiotic factors could influence the extracts’ chemical composition outcomes. These commonly identified compounds (with variable concentrations) across actives extracts (<xref rid="app1-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="app">Supplementary Table S1</xref>, <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref> and <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-f002" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>) are </p><list list-type="simple"><list-item><label>(i)</label><p>flavonoids—isosalipurposide <bold>1,</bold> quercetin <bold>3</bold>, kaempferol <bold>22</bold>, rutin <bold>6</bold>, and naringenin <bold>42</bold>;</p></list-item><list-item><label>(ii)</label><p>hydroxycinnamic acids—caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>36</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold>, and ferulic acid <bold>38</bold>; and</p></list-item><list-item><label>(iii)</label><p>benzoic acid <bold>24</bold> and hydroxybenzoic acids such as gallic acid <bold>25,</bold> salicylic acid <bold>32,</bold> ellagic acid <bold>44,</bold> syringic acid <bold>28</bold>, and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic acid <bold>31</bold>.</p></list-item></list><p>In summary, richer chemical diversity has been observed among compounds in the leaf and flower extracts than in bark extracts.
