The heart wood of pine tree was used for lighting of the street at night and ignition of firewood at home in olden days where there was no supply of electricity and kerosene. The resins extracted from pine wood are used as adhesives. Popular traditional beer, locally known as "bhangchang", was prepared from rice (<italic toggle="yes">Oryza sativa </italic>L.), finger millet (<italic toggle="yes">Eleusine coracana </italic>Geartn.), maize (<italic toggle="yes">Zea mays </italic>L.) and buckwheat (<italic toggle="yes">Fagopyrum esculentum </italic>Moench). "Bhangchang" has traditionally been used and served in all festive occasions, birth and marriage ceremonies. A diverse knowledge system exists among the Monpa women to prepare the nutritionally rich foods and fermented beverages, which play an important role in their day to day socio-cultural and spiritual occasions. A Monpa woman uses some of the wild plants as anti-microbial and they believed that these plants are responsible for the healthy growth of yeast during the process of fermentations. During the field study, we have documented the use of young leaves and twigs of certain species like <italic toggle="yes">Piper betle</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Solanum indicum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Buddleja asiatica </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">Hedyotis scandens </italic>as common growth supplements during the preparation of fermentation starter cultures containing brewer's yeast (locally called <italic toggle="yes">phamzas</italic>). The most frequently cited species were <italic toggle="yes">Buddleja asiatica </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">Hedyotis scandens</italic>. They believed that consumption of rice beer is good for health and act as a remedy for various ailments may be attributed to medicinal properties of the herbs used in the preparation of starter cultures.</p></sec><sec><title>Ethno-veterinary plants</title><p>A few plants were used to improve the health state and growth of livestocks. The leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis sativa </italic>was given to the cattle and goat to cure dysentery and diarrhoea (These diseases were identified by the presence of watery stool and blood). The stem of wild <italic toggle="yes">Musa paradisica </italic>L., was regularly given to cattle particularly during pregnancy to enhance the yield of milk. A paste powder obtained from the whole plant of <italic toggle="yes">Plantago major </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">Ageratum conyzoides </italic>are commonly tied to the affected portions of cattle and goat to relieve from severe pain and inflammations. <italic toggle="yes">Gymnocladus assamicus </italic>ripe pods are soaked in water and used as disinfectant for cleaning wounds and parasites like leeches and lice on the skin of livestocks. The fully ripe pods soaked in water are used as soap for bathing because it does not cause harm to soft skin and burning sensation to eyes. Leaves are used as green manure in agricultural field crops. <italic toggle="yes">Gymnocladus assamicus </italic>is a critically rare and endangered plant species and also endemic to the north-eastern region of India [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B29">29</xref>].</p></sec><sec><title>Ichthyotoxic and fish feed plants</title><p>Community seasonal fishing and hunting are of great economic activities of many tribal people including Monpa ethnic group in addition to agriculture.
