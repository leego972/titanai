Trees were the most dominant growth form, followed by shrubs and herbs, whereas climbers were least represented. This pattern reflects both the ecological structure of the district’s semi-arid and montane landscapes and the adaptive strategies of indigenous communities in selecting locally available plant resources for healthcare.</p><p id="Par87">The predominance of woody growth forms is consistent with findings from many ethnobotanical studies conducted across East Africa, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, particularly in environments where trees and shrubs constitute the backbone of natural vegetation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>]. Such cross-regional similarities suggest that ecological availability strongly shapes medicinal plant selection, but also that woody taxa often hold enduring cultural and therapeutic value across diverse pastoral and agro-pastoral societies.</p><p id="Par88">Trees represented the largest proportion of MPs in Sheikh District, indicating a strong reliance on long-lived woody perennials. Trees are especially valued because they remain available throughout the year, even during dry seasons, and provide multiple usable parts such as leaves, bark, roots, fruits, and seeds. This versatility allows healers and households to treat a wide range of ailments using a single species. Similar cultural preferences for trees have been reported in Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Cameroon, where woody species are frequently regarded as central to traditional medicine due to their broad therapeutic roles and symbolic importance within local healing systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR79">79</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR83">83</xref>]. Comparable patterns have also been documented in South Asia and South America, highlighting the widespread significance of woody MPs in indigenous healthcare traditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR84">84</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR86">86</xref>].</p><p id="Par89">Several culturally prominent medicinal trees recorded in Sheikh District, such as <italic toggle="yes">Albizia anthelmintica</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Azadirachta indica</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Vachellia tortilis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, are widely recognized across Africa and Asia for their medicinal versatility, nutritional contributions, and socio-economic value [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR87">87</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>]. Their prominence in local ethnomedicine suggests that these species function as keystone resources supporting both health and livelihood resilience.</p><p id="Par90">Shrubs constituted the second most dominant growth form, reflecting their ecological abundance and resilience under drought and grazing pressure in semi-arid environments. Shrubs are often easily accessible and commonly used in remedies for gastrointestinal, dermatological, and respiratory conditions. Comparable patterns have been observed in Ethiopia, Sudan, and Tanzania, where shrub-dominated vegetation supports a wide range of medicinal applications, particularly through the use of bark, latex, or aromatic resins [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR90">90</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>].</p><p id="Par91">In Sheikh District, species such as <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Commiphora myrrha</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Euphorbia polyacantha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus hamur</italic> illustrate the importance of resinous and latex-producing shrubs in managing wounds, skin infections, and respiratory ailments. Similar uses have been reported in Somalia and the broader Middle East, suggesting shared ethnomedicinal traditions shaped by comparable dryland ecologies.</p><p id="Par92">Herbs, although less represented than trees and shrubs, were mainly used to treat common and acute ailments such as colds, inflammation, and minor infections. Herbaceous species are often preferred for household-level remedies because they are easy to collect, regenerate quickly, and can be prepared fresh with minimal processing. Similar findings have been documented in Uganda, Kenya, Europe, and Asia, where herbs are frequently associated with short-term treatments and everyday healthcare practices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR97">97</xref>].
