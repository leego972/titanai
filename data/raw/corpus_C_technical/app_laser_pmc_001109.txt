Akiyama et al.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0076" ref-type="ref">76</xref> used OCT and OCT‐A to demonstrate that persistent vitreous attachment at the optic disc (that is absence of posterior vitreous detachment) is essential to the growth of neovascularisation at the optic disc, with neovascularisation proliferating along both sides of the posterior vitreous interface, and that neovascularisation arises from the neuroretinal rim or outside the optic disc margin rather than from the optic cup. Similarly, Vaz‐Pereira et al.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0074" ref-type="ref">74</xref> found that 79 per cent of neovascularisation of the retina involved proliferation along the outer surface of the posterior vitreous interface, arising from areas of ischaemic retina with persistent vitreous attachment. Within the posterior pole, vitreous and pre‐retinal haemorrhage likely results from tractional forces and shear stress exerted on the area of neovascularisation bound to the retinal circulation.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0074" ref-type="ref">74</xref>
</p><p>Prior to the emergence of OCT‐A, evaluation of early retinal ischaemia in DR was limited to ophthalmologists with access to fluorescein angiography. Current generation OCT‐A enables rapid, non‐invasive visualisation of areas of reduced capillary perfusion, enlargement and distortion of the foveal avascular zone (FAZ) and pruning of the arteriolar branches (Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0004" ref-type="fig">4</xref>B). OCT‐A has also shown capillary impairment associated with intra‐retinal microvascular abnormalities, cotton wool spots or anomalous vascular loops and thus may be useful in staging disease severity.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0065" ref-type="ref">65</xref> Areas of capillary non‐perfusion appear to be better delineated on OCT‐A than with fluorescein angiography or clinical examination.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0061" ref-type="ref">61</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0073" ref-type="ref">73</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0077" ref-type="ref">77</xref> Capillary closure may occur in early stages of DR, worsening as DR progresses; thus measurements such as FAZ size could potentially represent biomarkers for DR progression.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0061" ref-type="ref">61</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0077" ref-type="ref">77</xref> Krawitz et al.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0078" ref-type="ref">78</xref> found a strong correlation between acircularity of the FAZ and the presence of DR.</p></sec><sec id="cxo12847-sec-0014"><title>Key points</title><p>
<list list-type="bullet" id="cxo12847-list-0002"><list-item id="cxo12847-li-0004"><p>DR is characterised by a diverse series of specific ophthalmoscopic signs. The appearance of these signs using OCT and OCT‐A has been well‐described in the literature; however, the specific application of OCT in eyes without DR on clinical examination has not been fully determined.</p></list-item><list-item id="cxo12847-li-0005"><p>OCT is an indispensable adjunct for the detection and management of all sight‐threatening DMO.</p></list-item><list-item id="cxo12847-li-0006"><p>
<italic toggle="yes">Limitations</italic>: OCT and OCT‐A for the assessment of intra‐retinal microvascular abnormalities and neovascularisation is presently limited by field of view. The application of OCT and OCT‐A to DR assessment in treatment‐naïve patients is evolving and may, in the future, include routine evaluation of the posterior vitreous interface and FAZ.</p></list-item></list>
