Preference ranking exercises further highlight the targeted use of species like <italic toggle="yes">C. kelleriana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">L. inermis</italic> for malaria and other priority health conditions, echoing patterns observed in Asia and South America, where community preferences strongly influence medicinal plant selection [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR84">84</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>]. Together, these observations emphasize the central role of MPs as primary healthcare resources and emphasize the need to integrate indigenous knowledge into broader health strategies.</p><p id="Par244">MPs also play a critical role in food security. Several species, including <italic toggle="yes">C. edulis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">M. stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">T. indica</italic>, serve both nutritional and therapeutic functions. This dual purpose enhances dietary diversity, particularly during periods of scarcity, and strengthens community resilience a pattern documented in Ethiopia, India, China, and Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR118">118</xref>]. The nutritional and medicinal multifunctionality of these plants illustrates the interdependence of healthcare and food security in semi-arid forest ecosystems and highlights opportunities for policies that simultaneously promote nutrition and health outcomes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR122">122</xref>].</p><p id="Par245">From an environmental perspective, the community demonstrates adaptive and sustainable management practices, including selective harvesting, home garden cultivation, and the protection of high-value species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR123">123</xref>]. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">C. myrrha</italic> are harvested in ways that preserve leaves, fruits, or latex while avoiding destructive extraction of roots or bark. Such practices align with strategies observed in Kenya, Nigeria, and Sudan, where traditional ecological knowledge mitigates threats like agricultural expansion, overgrazing, and fuelwood collection [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>]. Home garden cultivation, in particular, mirrors global approaches in Asia, Europe, and the Americas that aim to maintain plant availability while conserving wild populations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>]. These findings highlight the vital role of ethnobotanical knowledge in shaping adaptive environmental management strategies that can inform conservation and ecosystem planning in dryland forests.</p><p id="Par246">Culturally, MPs in Karamara are deeply woven into spiritual practices, rituals, and social cohesion. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">D. stramonium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">P. granatum</italic> are used in traditional ceremonies and protective rituals, reflecting the interconnection between ethnomedicine and cultural identity. The transfer of knowledge within families and selective sharing practices ensures the preservation of both medicinal efficacy and cultural heritage, a pattern also reported in Ethiopia, Pakistan, China, and South America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR123">123</xref>]. Maintaining these cultural practices safeguards indigenous knowledge systems while reinforcing communal health and social networks.</p><p id="Par247">These findings have important implications for public health and environmental policy in the Karamara forest region. Incorporating ethnomedicinal knowledge into primary healthcare programs can increase access to culturally appropriate treatments, reduce reliance on limited pharmaceutical resources, and strengthen community resilience.
