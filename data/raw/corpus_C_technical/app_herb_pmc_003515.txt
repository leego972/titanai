Such multipurpose use, while signaling pharmacological potential, may also increase conservation pressure, as widely used species often face greater harvesting intensity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>].</p><p id="Par113">Thus, the findings demonstrate that communities in Sheikh District rely extensively on MPs and diverse plant parts to address a broad spectrum of health challenges. The strong alignment between local practices and ethnomedicinal patterns reported globally underscores both the shared foundations of indigenous healing knowledge and the culturally specific adaptations shaped by dryland environments.</p><p id="Par114">These results provide important baseline evidence for future pharmacological validation, conservation planning, and sustainable healthcare strategies, while emphasizing the urgent need to preserve both medicinal plant diversity and the indigenous knowledge systems that continue to play a vital role in primary healthcare in Somaliland and beyond.</p></sec><sec id="Sec17"><title>Modes of preparation, formulation, and conditions of use of medicinalplants</title><p id="Par115">The ethnobotanical survey in Sheikh District showed that indigenous communities employ a diverse range of preparation and formulation techniques for MPs. These practices reflect long-standing cultural knowledge, accumulated experimentation, and practical adaptation to local environmental conditions. Preparation choices were not random but were strongly influenced by the plant part used, the ailment being treated, and the intended route of administration. Among the 54 recorded species, the most frequently reported methods included crushing, grinding or powdering, maceration, boiling/decoction, and direct application of fresh plant material (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">3</xref>).</p><p id="Par116">Crushing was the most common method, particularly for leaves, roots, and bark, where plant material is pressed to obtain juices or pastes for oral intake or topical application. Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe citrina</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> were often prepared in this way, either used directly or mixed with water. This technique allows rapid release of active compounds and is especially suitable for treating acute conditions requiring immediate intervention. Similar reliance on crushing has been widely documented across East Africa, India, and Pakistan, suggesting that this practice represents a culturally shared and highly accessible approach in many indigenous medical systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR162">162</xref>].</p><p id="Par117">Grinding or powdering was frequently applied to seeds, bark, and dried leaves. In Sheikh District, species such as <italic toggle="yes">Sesamum indicum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia reficiens</italic> were dried, powdered, and administered orally or applied externally to wounds. Powdering not only improves storage and transportability but also facilitates blending with other medicinal ingredients, reflecting local awareness of formulation strategies. Comparable preparation patterns have been reported from Uganda, Nigeria, and Cameroon, indicating that communities in different cultural contexts often converge on similar solutions for maximizing medicinal plant utility [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR129">129</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR131">131</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR163">163</xref>].</p><p id="Par118">Maceration, commonly performed in water or milk, was used especially for delicate plant parts such as roots and seeds. Plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Rotheca myricoides</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic> were prepared in this way to allow gradual extraction of therapeutic compounds while minimizing chemical alteration. This approach reflects a nuanced understanding of plant potency and patient safety, particularly for remedies intended for internal use. Similar culturally careful maceration practices have been widely reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, and India [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR164">164</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR165">165</xref>].</p><p id="Par119">Boiling and decoction were primarily employed to extract bioactive compounds from tougher plant materials such as bark, stems, and mature leaves. In Sheikh District, decoctions prepared from <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Sterculia africana</italic> were commonly used for respiratory illnesses and febrile conditions.
