Though, Abu-Rabia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>] reported that knowledge of plants of nomadic pastoralists in Middle East countries originated from the sayings of Prophet Muhammad’s on health and illness: ‘<italic toggle="yes">The Medicine of the Prophet</italic>”. Also, the study conducted around Debre Libanos monastery has shown that the knowledge of the people varies based on the distance from the Monastery since religion is one of the aspects of variation in TK among the different societies in Ethiopia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR68">68</xref>].</p><p id="Par37">The medicinal plants reported by the people in Yalo Woreda are also reported by ethnobotanical studies conducted in semi-arid and arid regions in Ethiopia. Most of the medicinal plants are drought resistant, and many of them are a member of <italic toggle="yes">Acacia-Commiphora</italic> Woodland [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR68">68</xref>]. The medicinal plants were collected from natural vegetation since the majority (95%) of the population is pastoralists, and the rest are urban dwellers without a home garden. In Ethiopia, the majority of the traditional medicines are prepared from plants collected from the wild [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>]. The culture of conservation of medicinal plants in the home garden is not practiced in many regions of Ethiopia. Conservation of the medicinal plants is a requirement since the recurrent drought imposed by climatic changes has an impact on the natural vegetation. The other threat to the vegetation of the study area is the spread of invasive species such as <italic toggle="yes">P. juliflora</italic> that are replacing the plants with cultural values and changing vegetation to monotype bushes and forests. The people in Djibouti and Borena pastoralists areas reported a similar scenario where encroachment of invasive plants has resulted in a loss of valuable plants and degradation of the rangeland that indicates the necessity of further additional ethnobotanical studies in Afar region [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>].</p><p id="Par38">The medicinal plants reported in Yalo Woreda are also reported by other studies conducted in Afar and other areas where the Afar people are inhabitants. Bahru et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>] reported fourteen plant species and Beche et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>] reported ten plant species in the studies conducted around the Awash National Park. Meragiaw [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>] reported twelve plant species in a study carried out in Aba’ala Woreda; Seifu et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>] reported nine plant species in a study conducted in Chifra District; and Belayneh et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>] reported 14 plant species in a study carried out in pastoral and agro-pastoral communities in Erer Valley of Babile Woreda. The survey conducted in the region of Randa, Djibouti reported 46 plant species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>].
