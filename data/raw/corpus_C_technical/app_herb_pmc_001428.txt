The oil content variability is attributable to genetic variances in <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> species, environmental conditions, and agricultural practices [<xref rid="B33-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. Among all the <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> species, <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic>, rapeseed, is one of the most important sources of edible oil. According to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA report, January 2015), <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic> is the second largest produced oilseed crop worldwide with 71.94 million MT and the third largest source of vegetable oil. Indeed, the average oil content of <italic toggle="yes">B. rapa</italic> (47.30%) and <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic> (46.40%) was significantly higher compared to that of <italic toggle="yes">B. carinata</italic> (40%) and <italic toggle="yes">B. nigra</italic> (37.68%). Additionally, the general composition of oils from different <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds consists of seven major fatty acids: palmitic (C16:0), stearic (C18:0), oleic (C18:1), linoleic (C18:2), linolenic (C18:3), eicosanoic (C22:0), and erucic (C22:1) acids. <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. rapa</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">B. carinata</italic> naturally accumulate high amounts of monounsaturated fatty acids, mainly erucic acid (C22:1ω9) (40–50%), while other species such as <italic toggle="yes">Sinapis alba</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">B. nigra</italic> have a moderate content of this fatty acid (<xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>).</p><p>This oil’s high concentration of erucic acid (about 40%) renders it unsuitable for human consumption [<xref rid="B42-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>]. Thus, the removal of erucic acid is required before rapeseed oil can be consumed by humans. The low-erucic-acid and low-glucosinolate cultivar is called “canola” in North America and “double-low” or “00” rapeseed in Europe. The oil extracted from double-low rapeseed is generally recognized as safe by the FDA [<xref rid="B43-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>]. It was demonstrated that mature oilseed rape seeds oil from double-low quality oilseed rape (low in erucic acid (up to 2% in consumption seeds) and low glucosinolate level (up to 25 μmol/g of seeds)) contained 60% monounsaturated oleic acid (C18:1), 30% polyunsaturated fatty acids (20% linoleic acid (C18:2) and 10% linolenic acid (C18:3)), 2% eicosenoic acid (C20:1), 7% saturated fatty acids (mainly palmitic (C16:0) and stearic acids (C18:0)), and 1% other acids [<xref rid="B44-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>]. As a result, double-low oil contained a high amount of oleic and linoleic acids, making it significantly healthier [<xref rid="B45-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>]. Oleic acid is suitable for low-cholesterol diets, for frozen food preparation, and to improve the stability of cooking and frying oils [<xref rid="B46-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>]. Moreover, linoleic (omega-6) acid reduces cholesterol and triglyceride levels and improves the viscosity of blood cells [<xref rid="B33-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. Linoleic acid (omega-6) and α-linolenic acid (omega-3) are essential fatty acids [<xref rid="B47-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>]. Furthermore, the low level of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) is beneficial for human health as their high consumption reduces the risk of cardiovascular diseases [<xref rid="B48-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>], while oils with a high polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content are more sensitive to lipid oxidation [<xref rid="B49-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec3dot2-molecules-27-06008"><title>3.2. Proteins, Minerals, and Secondary Metabolites</title><p>Several research studies have been undertaken to assess the chemical composition of the seeds of <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> genus species. In addition to their high oil content, <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds exhibited a high content of carbohydrates, fat, dietary fiber, and proteins, especially in <italic toggle="yes">B. juncea</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. napus</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">B. rapa</italic> seeds [<xref rid="B50-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>,<xref rid="B51-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>] (<xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref>). Indeed, there is a growing trend to isolate proteins from <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds, e.g., from canola seed meal to be used in human food [<xref rid="B52-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>] and as a coproduct of oil recovery [<xref rid="B53-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>].
