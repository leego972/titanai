Locals report that <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>bush tea is calming and warms the blood.</p><p>The literature contains various mentions of tropical peoples' medicinal use of <italic toggle="yes">Lippia </italic>ssp. (see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B80">80</xref>] for genus review); however, there are few ethnographic reports for <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>specifically. People of San Felipe, Yaracuy State, Venezuela use <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>for upset stomach [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B81">81</xref>]. In Montserrat and Trinidad people use a <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>infusion for colds [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B71">71</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B82">82</xref>], and also for influenza in Trinidad [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B71">71</xref>], and sore throat in Monserrat [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B82">82</xref>]. It is an antispasmodic across Venezuela [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B83">83</xref>].</p><p>African and American tropical peoples most usually use Lippia species to treat respiratory disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B80">80</xref>], for stomach problems and as sedatives ([<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B80">80</xref>] and [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B84">84</xref>] for review of <italic toggle="yes">L. alba </italic>in South America). For example, Jamaicans use <italic toggle="yes">L. alba </italic>for insomnia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>], and Brazilians use <italic toggle="yes">L</italic>. <italic toggle="yes">alba </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">L</italic>. <italic toggle="yes">geminata </italic>as sedatives [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B85">85</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B86">86</xref>], and Nigerians likewise drink an infusion of <italic toggle="yes">L</italic>. <italic toggle="yes">geminate </italic>as a sedative and relaxing remedy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B87">87</xref>].</p><p>Haitians use a plant with the common name <italic toggle="yes">dité </italic>(c.f. <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>in Dominica), for what the researchers identify as "nerves"[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B88">88</xref>], which may relate to a similar or identical condition as fright. Though this research identifies Haitian <italic toggle="yes">dité </italic>as <italic toggle="yes">Thymus vulgaris</italic>, it may even be <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>('false thyme") rather than (<italic toggle="yes">T. vulgaris</italic>) ("thyme") as this misidentification is common in my experience. At any rate, thymol is the main constituent in both <italic toggle="yes">T. vulgaris </italic>and <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera</italic>, and the effects of the two plants should thus be somewhat comparable. European use of thyme tracks perfectly with the Caribbean use of <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>bush tea for fright. For example, in England, the "most important reason for drinking thyme tea has been to calm the nerves: the plant is a well-known sedative ([<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B89">89</xref>]:224)." Further, Scots and English of Suffolk County used it to prevent bad dreams [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B89">89</xref>].</p><p>Pharmacologically, <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>demonstrates bioactivity in brine shrimp assays [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B90">90</xref>], and as extracted essential oil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B91">91</xref>], and in vapor phase <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>shows highly potent antimicrobial activity against Gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria, molds and yeast [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B92">92</xref>]. Bacterial strains are highly sensitive to <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>oil, and fungi and yeast strains are even more sensitive than bacteria to the action of the oil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B91">91</xref>].</p><p><italic toggle="yes">L. micromera</italic>'s leaf oil is high in cavacrol (42.2%)[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B93">93</xref>]. Cavacrol performs diverse pharmacological activities (see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B94">94</xref>]including inhibiting gastrointestinal spasms and contractions in rats [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B94">94</xref>]and rendering bacteria non-infective (by affecting flagellin such that cells become nonmotile and unable to adhere to epithelial cells) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B95">95</xref>].</p><p>The painkilling ability of <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>remains uninvestigated, however oregano oils- which, like lippia oils are very high in cavacrol- demonstrate analgesic activity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B96">96</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">Lippia adoensis </italic>is analgesic for mice [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B97">97</xref>].</p><p><italic toggle="yes">L. micromera</italic>'s essential oil (as opposed to leaf oil only, above) is abundant in thymol (33.7%) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B91">91</xref>].
