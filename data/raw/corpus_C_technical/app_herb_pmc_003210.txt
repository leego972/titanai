</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec17"><title>Discussion</title><sec id="Sec18"><title>Edible, traded and Muslim medicinal plants</title><p id="Par30">Urbanisation is often considered an aspect of modernisation that leads to the erosion of medicinal plant knowledge [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>], but urban contexts may have vibrant medicinal plant use traditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>]. In the Middle East, urban male herbalists are acclaimed for their specialist medicinal plant knowledge [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>]. In this study, we evidence a rich body of female, lay medicinal plant knowledge, supporting the observation by Elolemy and AlBedah [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>] that women commonly use herbal therapies in Saudi Arabian cities. Although only 32 Meccan women participated in this study, more than 100 medicinal plants were documented to treat a wide range of health complaints. The knowledge held by women in cities is markedly different from knowledge previously documented, with 41% of the plants cited by Meccan women not in the literature we sourced. This points to the under-documentation of knowledge of medicinal plants in Saudi Arabia but cannot be attributed to male versus female knowledge or urban versus rural knowledge in the absence of further studies. Existing studies may also exclude foods, spices and culinary herbs from ethnobotanical listings of medicinal plants, making less visible knowledge held by women and contributing to the difference we find between published studies and our results.</p><p id="Par31">A third (32%) of the medicinal plants used by the Meccan women interviewed are food plants. Salient food plants cited in this study include onion, celery, cabbage, coriander, lemon, olive oil and dates (Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>). The use of food plants as medicines by urban populations is widespread [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>] and may be due to the easy access to these plants. Medicinal foods are also an important feature of the Mediterranean medical tradition, observed specifically in the Greek Hippocratic texts that influenced Dioscorides’ <italic toggle="yes">Materia Medica</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>], which in turn influenced Arabic medicinal texts [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>]. Specific health beliefs associated with foods have also been observed in Arabia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>]. Along with food plants, many medicinal plants reported are spices (17%), which have played a double role as flavouring and medicinal products since the Middle Ages [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>]. The most salient medicinal plants identified here are also all spices (<italic toggle="yes">Trigonella foenum-graecum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cuminum cyminum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Pimpinella anisum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cinnamomum verum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Zingiber officinale</italic>). Spices are both grown in the Middle East and imported from Africa and Southeast Asia (including plants from the Zingiberaceae, Piperaceae, Theaceae, Costaceae, Fucaceae and Musaceae families).
