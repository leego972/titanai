Indigenous knowledge often guides sustainable harvesting, seasonal use, and species selection, and its loss can accelerate biodiversity decline and weaken community-based conservation practices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>].</p><p id="Par8">In eastern Ethiopia, remnant forest ecosystems such as the Karamara forest patches play a vital role in sustaining both biodiversity and traditional livelihoods. These forests support the health systems of the Somali community, whose ethnomedicinal practices reflect long-term adaptation to dryland and semi-arid environments. However, the Karamara forest patches are increasingly threatened by agricultural expansion, overgrazing, charcoal production, and recurrent droughts linked to climate variability. The growing demand for medicinal plants, particularly those harvested for roots, bark, or whole plants, poses additional pressure on already fragmented habitats and places several culturally important species at risk.</p><p id="Par9">Although Ethiopia is widely recognized for its ethnobotanical richness, research efforts have been unevenly distributed across regions. Most ethnomedicinal studies have focused on Oromia, Southwest Ethiopia, and the Amhara regions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>], while Somali communities remain poorly represented in the national ethnobotanical literature. This imbalance limits understanding of how medicinal plant knowledge varies across ecological zones and cultural settings, particularly in dryland systems where environmental constraints strongly influence plant availability and use. The Karamara forest therefore offers a valuable opportunity to document and interpret ethnomedicinal knowledge within a Somali cultural and ecological context that has received little scientific attention.</p><p id="Par10">Beyond documentation, contemporary ethnobotanical research increasingly emphasizes the need to identify culturally important and ecologically vulnerable species to support ethnopharmacological research and conservation planning. Quantitative tools such as use value (UV), informant consensus factor (ICF), and fidelity level (FL) have become widely used for evaluating the relative importance, reliability, and therapeutic consistency of medicinal plant use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>]. When combined with careful voucher specimen collection and taxonomic verification, these approaches allow ethnobotanical data to contribute meaningfully to biodiversity conservation and medicinal plant prioritization.</p><p id="Par11">The documentation and analysis of ethnomedicinal knowledge also align closely with global development priorities. Traditional medicine supports affordable and culturally appropriate healthcare, contributing to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 on good health and well-being. At the same time, conserving medicinal plant resources and their habitats advances SDG 15 on life on land, while plant-based livelihoods can enhance household resilience and support poverty reduction under SDG 1 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>]. In dryland regions such as eastern Ethiopia, where ecological vulnerability and health challenges often intersect, these linkages are particularly important.</p><p id="Par12">Within this broader ethnobotanical and ethnoecological context, the present study seeks to address clear gaps in knowledge concerning Somali ethnomedicine in eastern Ethiopia. By combining qualitative documentation with quantitative analysis, the study aims to generate evidence that is relevant not only for cultural preservation but also for ethnopharmacological prioritization and conservation planning.</p><p id="Par13">Specifically, this study investigates the medicinal plant knowledge of the Somali community inhabiting the Karamara forest patches of eastern Ethiopia. It aims to: (i) document a vouchered and taxonomically verified inventory of medicinal plant species and examine how ethnomedicinal knowledge is distributed across socio-demographic groups; (ii) analyze patterns of plant part use, preparation methods, and routes of administration, and compare these findings with reports from other Somali-inhabited areas of Ethiopia and the Horn of Africa; (iii) evaluate the cultural importance and consensus of medicinal plant use using standard quantitative ethnobotanical indices; and (iv) assess the implications of this knowledge for strengthening primary healthcare systems, guiding conservation priorities, and supporting sustainable management of MPs in dryland forest ecosystems.</p><p id="Par14">The study is guided by the following hypotheses: (i) medicinal plant knowledge is unevenly distributed across age groups and healing specialization, with elders and traditional healers possessing significantly greater knowledge than younger community members; (ii) medicinal species harvested for destructive parts such as roots, bark, and whole plants face significantly higher conservation risk than species harvested for renewable parts such as leaves and fruits; (iii) a core group of medicinal species and therapeutic applications in Karamara shows strong cross-regional similarity with other Somali communities, while forest-dependent uses reflect local ecological adaptation; and (iv) high reliance on locally available MPs is positively associated with household health resilience and pro-conservation attitudes, strengthening the link between indigenous medical systems, biodiversity stewardship, and sustainable development goals related to health and ecosystems.</p></sec><sec id="Sec2"><title>Materials and methods</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Description of the study area</title><p id="Par15">The study was conducted in the Karamara forest patches, situated in the Jijiga District of the Somali Regional State, eastern Ethiopia (Fig.
