See above</p></table-wrap-foot></table-wrap></p></sec><sec id="Sec7"><title>Results and discussion</title></sec><sec id="Sec8"><title>Discussion</title><p id="Par25">Only five species (Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>) are mentioned explicitly as medicinal plants in the Bible: Fig (<italic toggle="yes">Ficus carica</italic>), Nard (<italic toggle="yes">Nardostachys jatamansi</italic>), Hyssop (<italic toggle="yes">Origanum syriacum</italic>), “Balm of Gilead” (<italic toggle="yes">Commiphora</italic> sp.) and Mandrake (<italic toggle="yes">Mandragora officinarum</italic>) (Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>). Twenty-seven species come under the category “Plants which are mentioned in the Bible and are known as medicinal in Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia” (Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>). Thirteen species are included as “Plants which are not cited in the Bible but mentioned as medicinal in the Talmud and/or Egypt and/or in Mesopotamia” (Table <xref rid="Tab3" ref-type="table">3</xref>). Six plants are classified under various patterns (Table <xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>).</p><p id="Par26">At least 18 medicinal plants (Tables <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>–<xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>), in addition to those in the Bible, are mentioned in the Talmud and or Mishna, most of which (15) are known also in Egypt and Mesopotamia, while three are only from Egypt. Since most of the post-Biblical citations are from the Babylonian Talmud, one may consider it as having influenced the local medicine of Babylonia (where this Talmud was written) rather than reflecting Biblical reality. The data indicating that all these species were also known from Egypt, strengthen the idea that the post-Biblical literature was not biased to Mesopotamian plants.</p><p id="Par27">Seven of the suggested species (Table <xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>) are not mentioned in the Bible or in the Talmud but were recorded as medicinal plants from Egypt, as well as from Mesopotamia; it is logical to assume that they can be included as BMPs.</p><p id="Par28">About 60% of our suggested BMPs are foreign species; 40% are indigenous, 60% are imported, 30% are domesticated (each plant can belong to more than one group). The main sources of foreign imported species are East Asia (11%), Southwest Asia (11 %), West and South Asia (8%) and Arabia (4%).</p><p id="Par29">The high proportion of imported plants (as medicinal materials) shows indirect evidence of prolific import in Biblical times [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>].
