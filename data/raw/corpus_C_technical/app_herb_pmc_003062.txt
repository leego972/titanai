Species such as <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Opuntia ficus-indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Tamarindus indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus dubius</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic> were frequently cited as both food and medicine, reflecting their dual nutritional and therapeutic value. In addition, fodder-providing trees such as <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Acacia nilotica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Acacia senegal</italic> indirectly strengthen food security by sustaining livestock, which forms the backbone of local livelihoods. These findings indicate that MPs are not used solely for healthcare but are deeply integrated into broader household survival and nutritional strategies.</p><p id="Par163">From an environmental perspective, most medicinal species are harvested from wild forest patches, shrublands, and riverine habitats, showing a strong dependence on natural ecosystems. Multipurpose species such as <italic toggle="yes">Boswellia rivae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Ficus</italic> spp., and <italic toggle="yes">Pistacia aethiopica</italic> provide overlapping medicinal, ecological, and economic functions, including soil stabilization, microclimate regulation, wildlife habitat, and resin or fuel production. However, species harvested for roots, bark, and resin were consistently perceived as being under higher harvesting pressure and declining availability, particularly in fragmented forest patches. In contrast, species harvested mainly for renewable parts such as leaves, fruits, and flowers showed greater local sustainability. These results demonstrate a clear link between harvesting strategy, ecosystem degradation, and conservation risk.</p><p id="Par164">Medicinal plant use in Karamara is also strongly embedded in cultural identity and social organization. Many species carry ritual, spiritual, and symbolic significance alongside their therapeutic functions, particularly incense-producing taxa such as <italic toggle="yes">Boswellia</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora</italic>, cosmetic plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Lawsonia inermis</italic>, and culturally protected trees such as <italic toggle="yes">Ficus</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus</italic>. Ethnomedicinal knowledge is predominantly held by elders and traditional healers and is transmitted orally across generations. However, informants widely reported a decline in youth participation in traditional healing practices due to formal education, urban influence, and increased reliance on pharmaceutical medicine. Together, these results show that MPs in the Karamara forest patches simultaneously support public health, food security, environmental stability, and cultural continuity, while also facing growing pressure from ecological degradation and weakening knowledge transmission systems.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec30"><title>Discussion</title><sec id="Sec31"><title>Diversity and growth forms of medicinal plants in Karamara forest patches</title><p id="Par165">The Karamara forest patches harbor a remarkable diversity of ethnomedicinal plants, comprising 85 species across 40 families. Fabaceae was the most dominant family, followed by Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Lamiaceae, and Burseraceae, consistent with ethnobotanical surveys across Ethiopia and East Africa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>]. Fabaceae’s prevalence likely reflects both ecological abundance and pharmacological potential, making it accessible and culturally significant. The prominence of these families may also reflect shared ethnobotanical knowledge and chemical diversity, highlighting their potential for pharmacological screening and novel therapeutic discovery [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>].</p><p id="Par166">Tropical forests like Karamara serve as reservoirs of traditional knowledge and natural resources, contributing to roughly one-quarter of modern pharmaceuticals [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>]. The high representation of Fabaceae and Asteraceae aligns with their prominence in Ethiopian flora [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref>], while studies elsewhere emphasize the ecological and medicinal importance of Asteraceae [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>].
