In Vitro Citototoxicity Assays</title><p>The cytotoxic activity (MTT assay) of PMG samples against normal (retinal) and cancer (breast, lung, colorectal) cell lines is depicted in <xref rid="biomolecules-12-01649-f005" ref-type="fig">Figure 5</xref>.</p><p>Despite an apparent red &gt; pink &gt; white trend (all but A549 cells; <xref rid="biomolecules-12-01649-f005" ref-type="fig">Figure 5</xref>), all PMG hydroalcoholic extracts are moderately active (IC<sub>50</sub>&gt; 200 μg.mL<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup>) on the assayed cell lines since IC<sub>50</sub> values &lt; 10, 11–99 and 100–500 μg.mL<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> are customarily considered as very strong, strong, and moderate cytotoxic [<xref rid="B28-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Moreover, at given concentrations, they exhibited growth-promoting effects in both normal ARPE [pink (12.5–50.0, ~11%), white (12.5, ~6%)] and cancer cell lines (LS180, 7–47D &gt; A-549, MDA-MB-231). It has been reported that raw food-derived extracts sometimes induce proliferation in both normal and cancer cell lines, particularly at low concentrations [<xref rid="B65-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">65</xref>,<xref rid="B66-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">66</xref>,<xref rid="B67-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>]. The lack of bioactivity (IC<sub>50</sub> &gt; 200 μg.mL<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup>) of raw fruit extracts has been also reported for pecan nut in A549, LS 180 and ARPE-19 cells [<xref rid="B65-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">65</xref>] and PMG juice in MDA-MB-231 [<xref rid="B66-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">66</xref>]. Conversely, pure gallic acid from PMG (cv. Kabul; IC<sub>50</sub> ~ 15 μg.mL<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup>) and mango (cv. Ataulfo; IC<sub>50</sub> ~ 50 μg.mL<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup>) peels inhibit A549 and LS180 cell growth [<xref rid="B46-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>,<xref rid="B67-biomolecules-12-01649" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>]. The observed low cytotoxic effect (IC<sub>50</sub> &gt; 200 μg.mL<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup>) can be ascribed to low extract concentration, short exposure time, and the co-existence of cell growth-promoting substrates (e.g., free sugars). Lastly, certain cellular events suggestive of cytotoxicity (e.g., pro-apoptotic events such as cell shrinkage/rounding, nuclear fragmentation/chromatin condensation, membrane ‘blebs’) could have occurred at the upper assayed concentration (200 mg.dl<sup>−1</sup>); these were not documented in this study (e.g., by optical microscopy), a fact that deserves to be explored soon.</p></sec></sec><sec sec-type="conclusions" id="sec4-biomolecules-12-01649"><title>4. Conclusions</title><p>In this study, the in vitro assays indicated that the antioxidant capacity (ABTS, alcoholic &gt; hexane extract) and phytochemical fingerprint [ascorbic acid, tocols, carotenoids, organic acids, and PC (relative abundance/chemical nature)] of PMGs is phenogenotype-specific [red &gt; pink &gt; white], although their in vitro cytotoxicity was the same (IC<sub>50</sub> &gt; 200 μg.mL<sup>−1</sup>) against normal (retinal) and cancer (breast, lung, colorectal) cell lines. On the other hand, chemometric analyses showed that ellagic (pink) &gt; gallic (white) &gt; vanillic (red) acids may interact with multiple molecular targets in the cancer continuum (initiation–progression). So, by employing a comprehensive and multidisciplinary stepwise experimental design [in vitro assays (several cell lines, high-throughput chemical characterization) + in silico studies (foodinformatics)], this study documents the differential anticancer potential of three PMG fruits of different flesh (aryl) colors [red (cv. Wonderful), pink (cv. Molar de Elche), white (cv. Indian)], although such anticancer potential could be more effective in nutraceutical formulations such as fruit concentrates. The practical application of the evidence reported here may result in the formulation of novel PMG fruit-based OTC nutraceuticals, highlighting their phenogenotype (varietal)-specific phytochemical value as a market differentiation element, increasing the PMG fruit market share currently held by PMG (’Wonderful’) dry concentrates producing reconstituted juices and medicinal syrups.</p></sec></body><back><ack><title>Acknowledgments</title><p>The authors thank the support in research infrastructure to the institutional authorities (UAQ, UACJ, UNAM).</p></ack><fn-group><fn><p><bold>Publisher’s Note:</bold> MDPI stays neutral with regard to jurisdictional claims in published maps and institutional affiliations.</p></fn></fn-group><app-group><app id="app1-biomolecules-12-01649"><title>Supplementary Materials</title><p>The following supporting information can be downloaded at: <uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.mdpi.com/article/10.3390/biom12111649/s1">https://www.mdpi.com/article/10.3390/biom12111649/s1</uri>: Table S1: a.
