Sinapic acid, 3,4-di-hydroxybenzoic acid, ferulic acid, and rutin identified from <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> seeds were strongly associated with the antioxidant capability [<xref rid="B108-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">108</xref>]. Quercetin, the major representative of the flavonol subclass, shows antioxidant potential by scavenging free radicals and chelating transition metal ions [<xref rid="B131-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">131</xref>]. Furthermore, kaempferol is also characterized by a remarkable antioxidant potential [<xref rid="B132-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">132</xref>].</p><p>Because various oxidants have distinct recovery processes, several assays are employed to assess the total antioxidant capacity of samples. The seeds have a high antioxidant potential, as evaluated by their ability to reduce and chelate metals, reduce lipids, and scavenge free radicals. 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical-scavenging activity and the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) are the most widely used tests for evaluation of the antioxidant capacity of plant samples. The antioxidant activities of different mustard seeds as reported in the relevant literature are summarized in <xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t005" ref-type="table">Table 5</xref>. In vitro antioxidant activity has been the subject of numerous studies for different <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> species seeds. Several reports have highlighted the strong antioxidant potential of seeds in terms of positive DPPH radical-scavenging activity, Fe<sup>2</sup>⁺-chelating effect (FRAP), oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC), and [2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)] (ABTS) radical-cation-scavenging activity. As indicated in <xref rid="molecules-27-06008-t005" ref-type="table">Table 5</xref>, the cold-press oil of <italic toggle="yes">Sinapis alba</italic> (white mustard) and <italic toggle="yes">B. nigra</italic> (black mustard) seed extract are characterized by a higher inhibition of DPPH with 94.24% and 89.25%, respectively [<xref rid="B78-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">78</xref>], followed by <italic toggle="yes">B. juncea</italic> with 83.17% [<xref rid="B81-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">81</xref>]. The antioxidant potency of <italic toggle="yes">B. juncea</italic> seeds has been largely reported [<xref rid="B62-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>,<xref rid="B81-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">81</xref>,<xref rid="B133-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>,<xref rid="B134-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">134</xref>,<xref rid="B135-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">135</xref>]. Furthermore, it has been reported that the dichloromethane extract of <italic toggle="yes">B. rapa L.</italic> (Turnip) seeds exhibited a potent in vivo antioxidant effect through the inhibition of the hydroxyl radicals responsible for oxidative damage to DNA at 1000 μg/mL concentration by phenylethylbrassinin and 3-phenylpropionitrile [<xref rid="B123-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>]. Recently, special emphasis was placed on the antioxidant potential of broccoli (<italic toggle="yes">B. oleracea. var. italica</italic>), especially its seeds [<xref rid="B77-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">77</xref>,<xref rid="B136-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">136</xref>] and their isolated compounds such as sulforaphane [<xref rid="B79-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">79</xref>]. The antioxidant potential of different <italic toggle="yes">Brassica</italic> species seeds and their bioactive compounds was evaluated in animals to better highlight and understand their mechanism action. An in vivo investigation on female albino rats exposed to cadmium chloride, CdCl<sub>2</sub>, showed that the aqueous extract of <italic toggle="yes">B. nigra</italic> seeds prevented the oxidative stress of cadmium chloride and its toxicity in hematological parameters and lung tissue [<xref rid="B137-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">137</xref>]. Moreover, a mustard-seed-enriched diet improved the antioxidant status in mice by increasing plasma antioxidant enzyme activity and dose-dependently reducing lipid peroxidation [<xref rid="B138-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">138</xref>]. Moreover, the administration of broccoli and seed-purified glucoraphanin proved effective to reduce oxidative stress promoted by a high-fat diet (HFD) in mice by raising superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities [<xref rid="B139-molecules-27-06008" ref-type="bibr">139</xref>].
