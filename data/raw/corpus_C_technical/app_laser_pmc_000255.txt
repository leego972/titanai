The preclinical evidence on photoimmunotherapy and nano-photoimmunotherapy in combating cancer resistance is also discussed.</p></sec><sec id="s2"><label>2</label><title>Photodynamic therapy</title><p>PDT is a photochemical therapeutic procedure that utilizes laser irradiation at a defined wavelength to stimulate the transfer of photoelectrons to nearby oxygen molecules, thereby generating singlet oxygen, which is lethal to cancerous cells (<xref rid="B19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>). This therapeutic procedure is less invasive, suitable for treating squamous cell carcinoma, and offers good spatiotemporal selectivity (<xref rid="B20" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>). Apart from being directly lethal to cancerous cells, PDT can stimulate inflammatory reactions that encourage the creation of tumor-associated antigens from the remnants of cancerous cells, leading to immunogenic cell death (<xref rid="B21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>).</p><sec id="s2_1"><label>2.1</label><title>Principle of photodynamic therapy</title><p>Light irradiation of photosensitizer (PS) stimulates photon absorption and the excitation of the PS to the singlet state (S<sub>1</sub>), where there is a shift of electrons to an orbital with higher energy (<xref rid="f1" ref-type="fig">
<bold>Figure 1</bold>
</xref>). At this state (a short-lived state and typically not stable), the PS may go back to the ground state (S<sub>0</sub>) through the conversion of its energy to fluorescence or heat. This feature is so handy for applications in monitoring and diagnostic procedures (<xref rid="B22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>). Alternatively, intersystem crossing may happen, resulting in the PS being excited to a triplet state (T<sub>1</sub>). The PS in the T<sub>1</sub> can convey energy via phosphorescence or collide with different molecules, creating reactive chemical species through two kinds of molecular reactions. T<sub>1</sub> PS can also react with different types of solvents or organic substrates to transfer a single proton or electron, forming cation species or radical anions, respectively. Mostly, reactions of the PS with electron donor substrate occur, forming PS that eventually reacts with oxygen, generating radical superoxide anion. This is classified as a type I reaction. Yet, a type II reaction could take place if the PS at T<sub>1</sub> directly reacts with oxygen in its ground state (<sup>3</sup>O<sub>2</sub>) by transferring energy to generate singlet oxygen (<sup>1</sup>O<sub>2</sub>), noted as reactive oxygen species (ROS) (<xref rid="B22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>, <xref rid="B23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>). The molecular products of PDT, such as superoxide anions and singlet oxygen, will promote cytotoxicity since both products can even react directly with biomolecules like nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, leading to their degradation (<xref rid="B24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>). The superoxide anions that are created from type I reactions are non-damaging, especially in biological systems. Despite that, these superoxide anions can participate in reactions that generate hydrogen peroxide. A Fenton reaction may also occur where the superoxide anion instead reacts with hydrogen peroxide, forming highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. These radicals are ultimately capable of reducing hydrogen atoms in biomolecules or adding to the side of biomolecules with double bonds. For example, reactions of fatty acids with hydroxyl radicals can generate a hydroxylated product, which is also a radical, thus originating a reaction chain involved in lipid peroxidation and consequently causing damage to the cell (<xref rid="B22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>, <xref rid="B25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Moreover, ROS can directly damage lipids, proteins, and DNA in cancerous cells, causing alterations in ion transportation and cellular metabolism and an imbalance in homeostasis. The mitogen-activated protein kinase is stimulated in response to induced oxidative stress. Also, numerous cytokines and mediators involved in cell death processes such as autophagy, necrosis, and/or apoptosis are released. These processes or pathways could occur simultaneously and are not mutually exclusive with the same cell population. Different important parameters could contribute to the occurrence of a particular type of cell death pathway. These parameters include drug dose, PS intracellular distribution, light dose (illumination total time and fluence rate), the cell type being investigated, and the available amount of oxygen (<xref rid="B15" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>). Cell death following PDT activation is typically induced via autophagy, necrosis, and/or apoptosis (<xref rid="B26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>–<xref rid="B28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>). However, some newly identified cell death processes are linked with PDT outcomes, including paraptosis (typified with elevated cytoplasmic vacuolization that does not involve nuclear fragmentation or caspase activation) (<xref rid="B27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>, <xref rid="B29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>), pyroptosis (inflammation-induced cell death) (<xref rid="B30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>), ferroptosis (iron-dependent lipid peroxidation-driven cell death) (<xref rid="B31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>), and necroptosis (controlled cell death mimicking attributes of necrosis and apoptosis) (<xref rid="B32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>).
