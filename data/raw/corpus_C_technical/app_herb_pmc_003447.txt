During the survey, only a few species, such as <italic toggle="yes">H. rueppelli</italic> (locally called <italic toggle="yes">Taseta</italic>), <italic toggle="yes">N. tabacum</italic> (locally known as <italic toggle="yes">Bao tumako</italic>), <italic toggle="yes">R. chalepensis</italic> (locally referred to as <italic toggle="yes">Chirata</italic>), and <italic toggle="yes">E. kebericho</italic> (locally called <italic toggle="yes">Kebercho</italic>), were observed being sold for their medicinal value. Informants interviewed at the market indicated that selling medicinal plants in legal markets was uncommon in the community. Instead, healers typically prepared and sold these plants from their homes. This practice reflects the local preference for gathering these plants themselves or seeking treatment directly from local healers rather than purchasing them. Additionally, cultural beliefs within the community suggested that selling medicinal plants could diminish their healing potential and lead to a loss of indigenous knowledge. Both medicinal practitioners and community members felt that activities related to traditional medicine should remain private and confidential. The market observation further noted that the plants available for sale were primarily intended for food, spices, stimulants, aromatics, and beverages, rather than for medicinal purposes.</p></sec><sec id="Sec37"><title>T-test analysis of medicinal plant knowledge between key and general informants</title><p id="Par57">R software was utilized to conduct a t-test to examine the differences in TMPK between key informants and general informants.
