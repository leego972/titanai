Common names for <italic toggle="yes">Gossypium barbadense </italic>cultivates include Extra Long Staple, Pima, South American, Creole, Sea Island, Egyptian cottons. The species accounts for about 5% of the world's cotton market and is prized for having the longest, highest quality fiber [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B67">67</xref>]. In Dominica plants grow cultivated and as escapes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B68">68</xref>].</p><p>Bwa Mawegans do not trim <italic toggle="yes">G. barbadense </italic>trees and they grow to around three meters tall in the village. The large, usually red-tinged leaves (40 cm) are alternate, on long petioles (at least 10 cm), and have five triangular lobes. The inflorescence is ivory to yellowish, and the cotton around its seeds is grayish, and darker gray on the red-leaved trees. Though the single species has a range of coloration, Dominicans have separate common names for the red-tinged plants with dark fiber, and green-leaved plants with light fiber: <italic toggle="yes">kouton nué </italic>(black cotton) and <italic toggle="yes">kouton blan </italic>(white cotton). In freelists, kouton nue was a much more frequent response, though some individuals specifically mentioned <italic toggle="yes">kouton blan </italic>in addition to or instead of <italic toggle="yes">kouton nué</italic>. There are five mature red-tinged trees growing near people's houses in the village, and I only know of one large fully-green-leaved tree. There are also several smaller, less visible shrubs in various home gardens.</p><p>If a Bwa Mawegan wants to make <italic toggle="yes">kouton nué </italic>tea, he asks one of his neighbors who has a <italic toggle="yes">kouton nué </italic>tree for some leaf. This is a small request, as the tree's leaves are so large. One can make at least three servings by boiling one lobe from one leaf (torn up) for around three minutes. Villagers with fright drink a cup of this tea once or twice a day until their fright subsides. As with all fright treatments, villagers say that bush tea made with <italic toggle="yes">kouton nué </italic>relaxes a person and "warms" his "frozen blood."</p><p>Ethnographic information on the medicinal use of this plant, aside from the cotton fiber, is relatively scanty, but most uses relate to relieving pain or treating coughs and respiratory trouble. In North America, the Alabama and Koasati Indians used the roots of <italic toggle="yes">Gossypium </italic>ssp. to make a tea to ease childbirth (Moerman 1998). A decoction from the bark of cotton roots was an official U.S. drug used from 1863 to 1950 to stimulate menstrual flow and aid contractions during labor. Northern Peruvians use <italic toggle="yes">G. barbadense </italic>topically for wounds (Bussmann and Douglas 2007), and Jamaicans use it for hemorrhoids [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>]. Yucatan Maya[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B69">69</xref>] and people of St. Kitts[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B70">70</xref>] drip juice of the flower bud into the ear for earaches. Venezuelans (Chiossone V. 1938), Trinidadians [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B71">71</xref>], Jamaicans [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B72">72</xref>], and people of St.Kitts and Nevis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B70">70</xref>] drink an infusion of <italic toggle="yes">G. barbadense </italic>leaves and flowers for bronchial and pulmonary problems, and colds and flues. Cubans boil the seeds to make a decoction for bronchial trouble [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B73">73</xref>]. South Carolinians [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B74">74</xref>] and ancient Maya [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B69">69</xref>] used the gossypium roots for asthma. Maya of Chunhuhub (Quintana Roo) Mexico use the <italic toggle="yes">G. hirsutum </italic>plant for snakebites, and grind the seeds for a headache poultice (Anderson Eugene N, with Cauich JC et al. 2003). Trinidadians favor a treatment of cotton seeds (of various species) for deworming dogs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B75">75</xref>]. Several peoples across the Guiana shield use <italic toggle="yes">Gossypium </italic>ssp. leaves medicinally (DeFilipps, Maina et al. 2004). They are a backache treatment throughout the area. French Guianese and several native Guiana groups (Caribs and Arawaks in Surinam; Palikur and Wayapi in French Guiana, Patamona in Guyana) boil <italic toggle="yes">G. barbadense </italic>leaves to treat high blood pressure, and pain, and apply macerated leaves to control itching. In the Guianas, <italic toggle="yes">Gossypium </italic>treats pain and stress in some accord with the Dominican use for fright.</p><p>In comparison to the abundant laboratory research on agricultural aspects of cotton, pharmacological research pales.
