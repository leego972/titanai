Ethnomedicinal Uses of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic></title><p>The different uses of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> are summarized in <xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>, including data about herbal preparation and countries where such practices are applied. Information on phytochemicals is summarized in <xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref> and associated pharmacological properties are discussed separately. Ethnomedicinal uses of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> in <xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref> are validated by bibliography shown in <xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref> and pharmacological properties of the species discussed in <xref ref-type="sec" rid="sec5-molecules-22-02128">Section 5</xref> of the manuscript.</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Euclea natalensis</italic> has been recorded in Angola, Botswana, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ethiopia, Kenya, Malawi, Mozambique, Namibia, Somalia, South Africa, Swaziland, Tanzania, Zambia and Zimbabwe. The species is found in arid and rocky habitats, termite mounds, dune bush, open grassveld, thickets, forests, forest margins, river banks and swamps, with altitude ranging from sea level to about 1600 m above sea level [<xref rid="B5-molecules-22-02128" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>,<xref rid="B22-molecules-22-02128" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>,<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-02128" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. It is a shrub or small to medium-sized dioecious tree. The roots, bark, twigs and leaves of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> exhibit several medicinal applications and used to treat or manage various human diseases and ailments throughout the distributional range of the species (<xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). The roots were the most used plant parts (83.3%), followed by bark and leaves with 6.7% each, leaf sap (3.3%) and twigs (1.7%). A total of 51 ethnomedicinal uses of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> are documented in the literature (<xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>) from eight countries in tropical Africa. The country with the highest number of ethnomedicinal uses is South Africa with 30 ethnomedicinal uses based on 11 literature records, followed by Tanzania with 15 uses and six literature records, Kenya with 11 uses and five literature records, Mozambique with five uses and five literature records and Malawi with five uses and one literature record (<xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). Literature records show high degree of consensus for at least 12 major diseases and ailments, which include abdominal pains, antidote for snake bites, chewing sticks or toothbrush, diabetes, diarrhoea, malaria, mouthwash, purgative, roundworms, stomach problems, toothache and venereal diseases (<xref rid="molecules-22-02128-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). In some cases, different parts of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> are mixed with plant parts of other species forming an herbal concoction or formula. For example, in South Africa, roots of <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> are boiled with roots of <italic toggle="yes">Capparis tomentosa</italic> and thorns of <italic toggle="yes">Gymnosporia heterophylla</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Phoenix reclinata</italic> and tied to a sharp instrument which is then stabbed into the chest of a patient suffering from pleurisy and pleurodynia [<xref rid="B8-molecules-22-02128" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B14-molecules-22-02128" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>]. In South Africa, <italic toggle="yes">E. natalensis</italic> is an ingredient of herbal concoction called <italic toggle="yes">imbiza</italic> also containing roots of <italic toggle="yes">Polygala fruticosa, Raphionacme</italic> spp., bulbous roots of <italic toggle="yes">Crinum</italic> spp. and <italic toggle="yes">Cyrtanthus obliquus</italic> and root barks of <italic toggle="yes">Zanthoxylum capense, Capparis tomentosa</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Rauvolfia caffra</italic> used to purify the blood [<xref rid="B8-molecules-22-02128" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>].
