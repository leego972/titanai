BY-NC</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec sec-type="intro" id="section1-0954411914543963"><title>Introduction</title><p>Optical coherence tomography (OCT)<sup><xref rid="bibr1-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">1</xref></sup> is a technique that uses principles of low-coherence interferometry to achieve images with micrometer resolution and millimeter penetration depth. While OCT was developed initially to investigate the retina,<sup><xref rid="bibr2-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="bibr3-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">3</xref></sup> it is nowadays used for several biomedical applications including, besides ophthalmoscopy, teeth, skin, and endoscopy.</p><p>Even though OCT has been first applied to in vitro studies, now is essentially used for in vivo investigations, as the challenge is to deliver images in real time—with video-frame capability. The modern OCT technology has evolved from time-domain (TD) OCT to spectral domain (SD) that offers higher acquisition speed and better sensitivity. SD-OCT has been developed along two directions to interrogate the spectrum at the output of the OCT interferometer, using either a broadband source and a spectrometer or a swept source (SS) and a photodetector.<sup><xref rid="bibr4-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="bibr5-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">5</xref></sup> OCT has progressively expanded toward other fields, such as non-destructive characterization of materials,<sup><xref rid="bibr6-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">6</xref></sup> art conservation,<sup><xref rid="bibr7-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">7</xref></sup> and profilometry.<sup><xref rid="bibr8-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">8</xref></sup>
</p><p>In parallel with the development of OCT systems and applications, an expanding area of research is involved in moving the technique from the laboratory to the clinical environment.<sup><xref rid="bibr9-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">9</xref></sup> This specific activity, as well as the material and art applications mentioned before, implies the use of handheld scanning probes to allow for the examination of different parts of the body or a larger sample.<sup><xref rid="bibr10-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">10</xref><xref rid="bibr11-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr"/>–<xref rid="bibr12-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">12</xref></sup> This work concerns the development of various handheld-based near-infrared optical imaging devices.<sup><xref rid="bibr13-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">13</xref></sup> This is itself part of a more general trend of having and using handheld probes in portable instruments,<sup><xref rid="bibr14-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">14</xref></sup> not only in OCT but also in other techniques, such as Raman spectroscopy,<sup><xref rid="bibr15-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">15</xref></sup> or other types of applications.<sup><xref rid="bibr16-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">16</xref></sup> These researches include devices developed for surgical procedures<sup><xref rid="bibr17-0954411914543963" ref-type="bibr">17</xref></sup> and applied in a dedicated medical environment.
