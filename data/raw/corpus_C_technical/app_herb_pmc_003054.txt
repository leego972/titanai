While ICF is widely applied in ethnobotanical studies, several authors, including Leonti, have emphasized that this index has important conceptual and methodological limitations. In particular, ICF does not measure pharmacological efficacy and may be influenced by sample size, informant selection, and cultural transmission processes rather than therapeutic effectiveness. Accordingly, in this study ICF is interpreted cautiously as a descriptive indicator of shared cultural knowledge and local agreement, rather than as evidence of medicinal efficacy.</p><p id="Par116">Human ailments were grouped into seven categories based on the nature of the condition, affected body parts, symptoms, and perceived causes (Table <xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>): dermatological disorders (wounds, ringworm, skin infections, burns), gastrointestinal disorders (gastritis, stomachache, constipation, diarrhea, intestinal worms), respiratory disorders (cough, common cold, influenza, chest pain), malaria, dental and oral disorders (toothache, mouth problems), urogenital and reproductive disorders, and other ailments (kidney problems, hypertension, snake bites, poisoning, effects of evil spirits, epilepsy).</p><p id="Par117">ICF values ranged from 0.60 to 0.85, indicating varying degrees of agreement among informants. Dermatological disorders showed the highest level of consensus, suggesting strong sharing of knowledge and commonly recognized remedies for skin-related conditions within the community. Gastrointestinal disorders ranked second, reflecting widespread recognition of plant-based treatments for digestive problems. Respiratory disorders and malaria exhibited moderate consensus, indicating the use of multiple species with less uniformity. Dental, oral, and other ailment categories showed lower levels of agreement, while urogenital and reproductive disorders recorded the lowest ICF values, suggesting greater variability in plant selection and possibly more specialized or restricted knowledge associated with these conditions.</p><p id="Par118">Fidelity Level (FL) analysis was used to examine the degree to which specific medicinal plant species were consistently associated with particular ailments by informants (Table <xref rid="Tab5" ref-type="table">5</xref>). Similar to ICF, FL has recognized limitations and should not be interpreted as a measure of clinical effectiveness. In this study, FL is applied as an indicator of ailment-specific emphasis within local ethnomedicinal knowledge systems.</p><p id="Par119">FL values ranged from 0.434 to 1.0, reflecting differences in the consistency of use for particular ailments. Three species—<italic toggle="yes">Euphorbia abyssinica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Cyphostemma adenocaule</italic> showed maximum FL values, indicating that informants consistently associated these species with wound healing, hypertension, and malaria, respectively. These results reflect strong cultural agreement on their primary uses, rather than confirmed pharmacological efficacy.</p><p id="Par120">Several other species, including <italic toggle="yes">Datura stramonium</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Jatropha aceroides</italic>, also exhibited relatively high FL values, indicating focused use and local recognition for specific conditions. In contrast, <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-christi</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic> displayed moderate FL values, suggesting that while they are preferentially used for particular ailments, they are also applied across a broader range of health conditions.</p><p id="Par121">Lower FL values were observed for <italic toggle="yes">Aloe megalacantha</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic>, reflecting their multipurpose use across several ailment categories. Such patterns highlight the adaptive and flexible nature of traditional healthcare practices, where certain culturally important species are valued for their broad applicability rather than ailment-specific specialization.</p><p id="Par122">
