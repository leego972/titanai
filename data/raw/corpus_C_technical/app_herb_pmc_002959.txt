Even though her focus was on entomology and her illustrations revolved around her discussion of metamorphosis, Maria Sibylla Merian, for many years, was seen as an artist rather than the scientist she was. In a 1997 catalog of an exhibition of Merian's work [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR109">109</xref>], it is said that her work should be read as artistic and not scientific since she was not an academic and did not have the necessary qualifications to be the author of a scientific work: “Merian was not a scholar. She was a painter, an embroideress, a dealer in paints, a teacher, a housewife, and a mother and a lover of nature. She came from a family of craftsmen, had not studied at a university, knew little Latin and basically had none of the qualifications required of an author of a scientific work.”</p><p id="Par39">She was seen by many as a skilled artist, but when she published her work <italic toggle="yes">Metamorphosis Insectorum Surinamensium</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>], she made it clear that she saw herself as a naturalist. Like many others, she had no formal academic training and studied in Amsterdam's collections and library and read and cited the work of Leeuwenhoek, Moffet, Swammerdam, Goedart and other distinguished scientists [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>]. In addition to having devoted great care and effort to correlating her work with that of other naturalists, such as Willem Piso [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>]. For each plant, she gave an indigenous, Dutch, and Latin name prior to Linnaeus's taxonomy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>]. For example, the illustration 21 refers to <italic toggle="yes">Passiflora laurifolia</italic> L. (Passifloraceae) and Merian reported its vernacular name as “marquiaas” and also “passiebloem”. She also annotated the names given by Marcgraf (“Murucuia Guacu”) and Piso (“Murucuia quarta”) (Fig. <xref rid="Fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref>).<fig id="Fig4" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 4</label><caption><p>Illustration 21 (left) and description (right) about <italic toggle="yes">Passiflora laurifolia</italic> L. in Maria Sibylla Merian’s <italic toggle="yes">Metamorphosis Insectorum Surinamensium</italic> (1705) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>]</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="MO4" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="13002_2023_589_Fig4_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par40">The ultimate test for claiming that Maria Sibylla Merian is a scientist is the fact that plant species can be identified from her illustrations. The number of identifications is extraordinarily high, proof of the accuracy of her work and concern for scientific rigor rather than just an aesthetic issue, Merian's accuracy even surpasses that of several later naturalists [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>]. The ultimately botanical identification was made by the botanists Tinde van Andel, Hajo Gernaat, Auke Hielkema and Paul Maas published in the 2016 facsimile edition of <italic toggle="yes">Metamorphosis Insectorum Surinamensium</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>].</p><p id="Par41">After the publication of her diary by the Russian Academy of Science [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>], historians began to examine her contribution as a naturalist. They found out that, at the age of 13, Merian began observing the metamorphosis of the silkworm, reporting its entire life cycle starting from the egg. When she published her book on European caterpillars [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>], the spontaneous generation of insects was still well accepted by academics and she wrote in her preface: “all caterpillars are born from eggs”. She made discoveries independently and at the same time (sometimes even before) as other renowned scientists such as Redi, Malpighi and Swammerdam [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>]. Her observations helped to discredit the spontaneous generation of insects [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>].</p><p id="Par42">Merian was also one of the first naturalists to accurately describe the complete metamorphosis of amphibians [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>] as is possible to see in Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5</xref>. Her description is not as detailed as that by Leeuweenhoek and Swammerdam (who are credited), but she probably did not have access to a microscope like they did—again an informal ban aiding the obliteration of a female scientist [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].
