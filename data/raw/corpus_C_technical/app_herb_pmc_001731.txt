SAR of Antibacterial Flavonoids Identified in <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic></title><p>Comprehensive reviews by Farhadi et al. [<xref rid="B82-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">82</xref>], Shamsudin et al. [<xref rid="B83-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>], and studies reported within the reviews revealed that different flavonoid structural configurations could exhibit different antibacterial activities. The SAR suggested that ring A and hydroxy groups at positions C-5 and C-7 were required for antibacterial activity against <italic toggle="yes">S</italic>. <italic toggle="yes">aureus</italic> strains (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-f007" ref-type="fig">Figure 7</xref>) [<xref rid="B84-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">84</xref>]. The presence of hydroxylation (number and position) on the B ring could influence the antimicrobial activities of these compounds, such as quercetin <bold>3</bold> (with 3′,4′-dihydroxy), kaempferol <bold>22</bold> (with 4′-hydroxy), and galangin (without a hydroxy at C-4′), as shown in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-f008" ref-type="fig">Figure 8</xref>. Kaempferol <bold>22</bold> was found to be the most active inhibitor of <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> DNA gyrase (MIC = 25 μg/mL), followed by quercetin <bold>3</bold> (MIC = 36 μg/mL), and the least active was galangin (MIC = 53 μg/mL), which demonstrated that 3′-OH decreased activity and emphasised the importance of 4′-OH in the ring B [<xref rid="B77-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">77</xref>]. However, kaempferol <bold>22</bold> (4′-OH) was less active against Gram-positive bacteria than galangin [<xref rid="B85-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">85</xref>].</p><p>Xu and Lee [<xref rid="B86-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">86</xref>] investigated 38 plant-derived flavonoids against antibiotic-resistant bacteria such as MRSA, multidrug-resistant <italic toggle="yes">Burkholderia cepacian,</italic> and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE); they showed that myricetin <bold>10</bold>, kaempferol <bold>22</bold>, quercetin <bold>3</bold>, and luteolin <bold>19</bold> were active against MRSA. In contrast, only myricetin <bold>10</bold> was found to be active on multidrug-resistant <italic toggle="yes">B. cepacian</italic> and VRE with MIC values of 32 and 128 μg/mL, respectively. The results highlight the importance of 3′,4′, and 5′-OH of ring B against multidrug-resistant bacteria. Furthermore, it was suggested that the C2=C3 double bond could also be necessary for the activity when the activity of quercetin <bold>3</bold> was compared with that of (+)-catechin <bold>14</bold>.</p><p>The 3-OH in ring C is considered essential for the activity; for instance, quercetin <bold>3</bold> (MIC<sub>50</sub> = 36 μg/mL) was found to be more active against <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> (DNA gyrase inhibition) than luteolin <bold>19</bold> (MIC<sub>50</sub> 67 μg/mL), which lacks the 3-OH [<xref rid="B77-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">77</xref>]. In addition, the presence of glycosylic at C-3 also significantly improved antibacterial activities compared to the 3-OH counterpart [<xref rid="B87-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">87</xref>]. </p><p>The antibacterial properties of the identified compounds in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref> and their discussed MOA and SAR explain and provide sufficient support for the antibacterial activities observed in the crude extracts of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic>.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec3dot3-molecules-28-04396"><title>3.3.
