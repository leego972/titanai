For example, the prevalence of diabetes in Brazilian adults has increased since the 1980’s [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>], and both <italic toggle="yes">Momordica charantia</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Bauhinia</italic> species are employed to treat it in Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">Bauhinia</italic> species are also used to treat high cholesterol, kidney diseases, and high blood pressure [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>]. Although recovered by our survey, these plants were not documented before at the VOP, most likely due to their absence or lack of popularity during the time frame of the previous survey [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Furthermore, differences in plant inventory, especially the higher proportion of introduced species in our study compared to Van den Berg’s 1960–1980’s study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>], may be influenced by environmental factors. Forest degradation and deforestation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>] caused by logging [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR66">66</xref>], cattle ranching [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR67">67</xref>], and fires [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR68">68</xref>] prompt a reduction in the availability of medicinal and native hardwood species, pushing a demand for introduced medicinal species to treat human diseases [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>]. However, the most popular medicinal species in Belém listed by Shanley and Luz [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>] partly overlap with our survey, but also partly with Van den Berg [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. This implies that it is yet unknown what the impacts of deforestation are on the availability of medicinal plants at the VOP.</p><p id="Par25">Van den Berg [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>] initiated her market study in 1965, and it continued for two decades over a time span in which there was more intact rainforest habitat compared to the present [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>]. However, our shorter study resulted in a more diverse medicinal plant species composition. We attribute this variation to our different methodological approaches due to our differing aims: while her aim was to document the most frequently occurring species, our aim was to find out the overall medicinal species diversity of the market. Because of these different approaches, a direct comparison of the species composition between studies remains problematic. However, this is inherent to studying ethnobotanical inventories over time and space [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>]. When comparing such historical ethnobotanical data, the only manner to overcome disparities in historical survey quality across studies is to clearly acknowledge differences in methodology. Our study highlights the importance of repeating market surveys, the necessity of establishing standard methodologies, and the exact documentation of the applied methodologies to ensure comparable results in future studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec12"><title>Plant names preserved</title><p id="Par26">Of the species our survey shared with Van den Berg [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>], the majority of vernacular names overlapped, indicating that there has been little change in names over three decades at the VOP. The small proportion that did not overlap could, again, be related to shifts in health perceptions and the occurrence of diseases over time. For example, in our study <italic toggle="yes">Cissus verticillata</italic> was called, among other names, <italic toggle="yes">insulina</italic>, which could be due to a higher occurrence of diabetes in Belém than at the time of Van den Berg’s study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>]. However, such differences in associated names were minor. This is in line with our expectations, as plant names tend to present a remarkable continuity over time, even for centuries, as was previously found in Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>] and neighboring Suriname [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>]. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Acmella oleracea</italic> was named <italic toggle="yes">jambu</italic> in our survey, and we traced back this name to circa 375 years ago in the <italic toggle="yes">Historia Naturalis Brasiliae</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>], where it was documented for the same species, in a slightly different spelling (<italic toggle="yes">nhambí</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">nhambu</italic>).</p><p id="Par27">Substantial transformations in social and environmental factors are needed for plant names to change.
