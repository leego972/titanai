Global average production hovers around 120 million metric tons, whereas in 2023, the production reached 139 million metric tons, due to the significant delay in the reporting of the data, with India, China, and Indonesia being the top producers, accounting for approximately 40% of global output [<xref rid="B100-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>]. Reflecting its significance, the market value in 2023 approached US$140 billion [<xref rid="B100-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>].</p><p>Beyond fresh consumption, bananas find versatile applications, including dried fruits, snacks, smoothies, ice creams, breads, flours, wines, and ingredients for functional foods [<xref rid="B121-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>]. Notably, there has been a recent surge in utilizing this fruit as a functional food ingredient due to its low digestibility of carbohydrates (both starch and non-starch), rendering it a highly compelling dietary staple [<xref rid="B123-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>].</p><p>Banana plants yield a fruit bunch only once in their lifetime, resulting in considerable waste generation from harvesting to post-processing [<xref rid="B121-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>]. For every ton of harvested bananas, approximately 100 kg of fruit are discarded, generating around 4 tons of waste, comprising 160 kg of stems, 480 kg of leaves, and 3 tons of pseudostems [<xref rid="B124-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">124</xref>]. Pseudostems resemble a trunk and are composed of overlapping leaf sheaths that provide support to the plant [<xref rid="B125-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">125</xref>]. Most of this waste is typically returned to the field, while in the processing industries, banana peels represent the most significant residue, accounting for about 35–50% of the fruit’s weight. Annually, approximately 8 million tons of these residues are generated in this industry, highlighting their considerable utilization potential [<xref rid="B100-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">100</xref>]. In some instances, banana peels can be repurposed as organic fertilizer and animal feed, due to their low tannin content and high fiber content [<xref rid="B126-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">126</xref>].</p><p>Banana peels, like other residues, exhibit high potential for reuse due to their richness in organic compounds, including lipids, fibers, carbohydrates, and proteins, serving as an excellent source of bioactive compounds, as shown in <xref rid="molecules-31-00996-t006" ref-type="table">Table 6</xref> [<xref rid="B121-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>,<xref rid="B123-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>,<xref rid="B124-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">124</xref>]. Several studies have identified over 40 compounds, categorized into four subgroups: phenolic acids, flavonols, flavan-3-ols (the largest subgroup), and catecholamines [<xref rid="B123-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>]. Moreover, banana peels demonstrate significant radical scavenging activity and reducing capacity compared to avocado, papaya, passion fruit, watermelon, and melon [<xref rid="B127-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">127</xref>].</p><p>These bioactive compounds exhibit significant potential for various applications, including the agri-food, cellulose and paper, energy, fiber, and bioplastic industries [<xref rid="B123-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">123</xref>,<xref rid="B126-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">126</xref>,<xref rid="B132-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">132</xref>]. Investigations by Ardila et al., 2024 [<xref rid="B133-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>], produced very high crystalline cellulose indices (up to 67.9%) from the extraction of pseudostem fibers through ultrasound-assisted extraction with NaOH. These produced fibers are comparable to, or even higher, than industrial fibers in terms of crystalline structure and cellulose content [<xref rid="B133-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>]. Particularly, these fibers, especially from the pseudostem, are being utilized as reinforcement in epoxy composites and raw materials in the textile industry [<xref rid="B134-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">134</xref>]. Banana stems are used as flavorings [<xref rid="B125-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">125</xref>].</p><p>The reviewed studies summarized in <xref rid="molecules-31-00996-t007" ref-type="table">Table 7</xref> present the latest applications and explore properties of biologically active compounds from banana waste in the agri-food industry, such as fertilizers or biopesticides.</p></sec><sec id="sec5dot4-molecules-31-00996"><title>5.4.
