Recently, cases of PAMM during pregnancy were described [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. Like our patient, the three women were described as healthy and had an otherwise uncomplicated pregnancy. One was in her first and two were in their second trimester of pregnancy, like our patient.</p><p id="Par35">OCTA was successfully performed in all three cases. Reperfusion was seen in cases 1 and 2, which could be considered a surrogate marker for better functional outcome in terms of a smaller scotoma due to a smaller atrophic area.</p><p id="Par36">In pregnancy, preexisting conditions may deteriorate owing to hormonal, hematologic, metabolic, cardiovascular, and immunologic changes that induce retinal vascular occlusive events [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. Associated risk factors for events of retinal vascular occlusion are primary antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, high factor VIII, and low protein S [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>]. Also, factor V Leiden mutation has been reported in a case of bilateral retinal vein occlusions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. Combined CRVO and CLRAO can occur with increased D-dimer level [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>]. However, physiologic changes during pregnancy may play a role. Hormonal alterations increase angiopoietic factors, such as progesterone [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>]. Cardiac output and heart rate are physiologically increased [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>]. Physiologic hypercoagulability and thrombophilia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>] may also play a role in the pathogenesis of retinal vessel occlusion.</p><p id="Par37">Our patients all had otherwise uncomplicated pregnancies, and laboratory blood tests did not reveal any defects or alterations. Two of the three women (case 2 and 3) possessed a PFO. This is a congenital heart defects with a short-circuit connection between the right and left atrium and is found in around 30% of all healthy adults [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>]. Though the majority of patients with PFO are asymptomatic and do not possess an increased risk for developing a stroke, it is likely that other risk factors (such as hypercoagulability) need to be present for a stroke to occur [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>]. It was found that PFO-related strokes occur mainly during the first two trimesters [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>]. In cases 2 and 3, who both presented PFO, the occlusive event occurred during this time. Post-ischemic management of PFO consists of secondary prophylaxis (antiplatelet therapy) and closure in large-sized PFO shunts [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>]. The treatment for retinal vascular occlusions is empirical, and should be performed in close cooperation with hemostaseologists, cardiologists, and obstetricians. We treated all cases with 100 mg acetylsalicylic acid orally, and patient 2 was recommended by cardiologists to undergo interventional PFO closure after pregnancy. Unlike the other two cases, the pathogenesis in case 1, that is, CRVO associated with CLRAO, is not thrombotic or embolic (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>). This also reflects the fact that the internal examination was unremarkable. Nocturnal hypotension, being a risk factor, was ruled out. Antiplatelet therapy as secondary prophylaxis was initiated.</p></sec><sec id="Sec6"><title>Conclusion</title><p id="Par38">OCTA is a valuable option to noninvasively diagnose and analyze the retinal perfusion status in pregnancy when fundus fluorescein angiography should be avoided.
