# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

<italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>for fright, some Dominican women use it to induce menstruation and labor, throughout labor, and after childbirth. To make a medicinal bush tea, villagers use two average stalks (containing around 24 leaves) to one liter of water. Some people make a decoction, boiling the tea for a couple of minutes, while others make an infusion by steeping <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>for several minutes. Villagers say that one can feel the calming, "melting" effects of <italic toggle="yes">go dité </italic>"bush tea" immediately. One cup of tea may be enough to cure a person of an acute attack of fright. A chronic fright sufferer takes one cup a day, or a few cups a week, usually alternating with the abovementioned herbs.</p><p>This species is used medicinally elsewhere (For a review of <italic toggle="yes">Plecthranthus </italic>ethnobotany see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B105">105</xref>]) Peoples of North, East and Central Africa, Asia, South America, the Caribbean, and the Pacific use it for digestive problems and skin conditions including burns, wounds and allergies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B105">105</xref>]. Like some Dominicans [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B106">106</xref>], Brazilians in Bahia use ground <italic toggle="yes">P. amboinicus </italic>leaves topically to treat skin ulcers [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B107">107</xref>]. In the Amazon and India the leaves of <italic toggle="yes">P. amboinicus </italic>treat urinary diseases [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B107">107</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B109">109</xref>]. Asians and South Americans use it for fevers [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B105">105</xref>], and Cubans use it as a bronchodilator and general cold and respiratory remedy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B73">73</xref>]. Schultes and Hoffman find that a related species (<italic toggle="yes">Plectranthus scutellarioides </italic>(L.) R. Br. [as <italic toggle="yes">C.blumei</italic>]) has psychoactive properties and has magico-religious significance for the Mazatec in Mexico as "divinatory" [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B110">110</xref>]. Most concurrent with its Dominican use as a fright treatment are other uses of <italic toggle="yes">P. amboinicus </italic>as a relaxant or antispasmotic, and to treat pain. Cubans prescribe it for epilepsy and convulsions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B111">111</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B112">112</xref>] and for earaches [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B73">73</xref>]. Kenyans use it for stiff necks and backaches [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B113">113</xref>]. Asprey and Thornton report historic uses of <italic toggle="yes">P. amboinicus </italic>in India (by its former <italic toggle="yes">Coleus aromaticus</italic>) to treat cronic cough, asthma, epilepsy and other convulsions, and also note that it had an intoxicating effect([<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B72">72</xref>] and [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B114">114</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B115">115</xref>] in [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B72">72</xref>]). Trinidadians and Jamaicans also use it for coughs, asthma, epilepsy and convulsions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B116">116</xref>].</p><p>So far in pharmacological laboratory screening, <italic toggle="yes">P. amboinicus </italic>shows antimicrobial activity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B112">112</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B117">117</xref>]. It demonstrates antiviral activity against vesicular stomatitis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B118">118</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">Herpes simplex </italic>virus-1 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B119">119</xref>], and it inhibits HIV activity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B120">120</xref>]. A decoction from <italic toggle="yes">P. amboinicus </italic>leaves demonstrates anti-epileptic effects [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B121">121</xref>], and, in experiments on rats, the decoction indicates simultaneous actions similar to those of tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B122">122</xref>].</p></sec></sec><sec><title>Epidemiology of fright</title><p>Rates of fright are fairly low. The frequency of fright is such that during any given field season that I have been in this village, there may be community knowledge of one to three individuals (out of about 130 adults) suffering fright -- sometimes nobody is frightened, or sometimes a family of ten or so may be. The irregular and fairly uncommon nature of the illness makes it hard to asses at the community level.</p><p>Further, finding about the epidemiology of fright in Dominica is not a simple task because people do not like to talk about their own fright experiences. Dominican culture has a strong Christian ethic of "neighbor loving," such that if one resident is publicly ill or troubled, his neighbors must try to help the sufferer. And, sufferers do receive plenty of outside attention. And yet, from the sufferer's view, it is his social responsibility to keep unpleasantness to himself, or within his immediate family, so as not to oblige his neighbors, who have other responsibilities.
