</p></sec><sec id="Sec26"><title>Indigenous knowledge transfer and medicinal plant collection</title><p id="Par152">The majority of informants reported that most ailments are treated at the family level using self-prepared remedies. When necessary, community members may consult other knowledgeable individuals with minimal or no cost. Notably, a substantial proportion of informants approximately 80% and 75% in different parts of the district indicated that they kept their medicinal plant knowledge secret, and its transfer was largely restricted to family members. Typically, the knowledge holder (often a father or mother) selects a child usually a son based on specific personal criteria for knowledge inheritance. Transfer of knowledge to individuals outside the family generally required substantial compensation. Only a small number of practitioners openly taught medicinal plant use to all family members, while some were unwilling to pass on their knowledge even within the household. Interestingly, about 10% of informants reported that their knowledge was revealed through dreams, reflecting spiritual dimensions of ethnomedicine.</p><p id="Par153">Most informants (80%) expressed concern that vertical transmission of traditional medicinal knowledge is declining, primarily due to the younger generation’s reduced interest, influenced by acculturation and modernization. According to 90% of respondents, the transfer of indigenous plant knowledge occurs orally rather than through written documentation, underscoring the fragility of this knowledge system.</p><p id="Par154">The timing of medicinal plant collection varied considerably among practitioners and was largely guided by individual beliefs.
