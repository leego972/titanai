2026</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1"><title>Background</title><p id="Par5">Ethnobotany explores the long-standing and often intimate relationships between people and plants, focusing on how local knowledge, cultural values, and ecological experience shape plant use, management, and conservation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>]. Across generations, indigenous societies have developed sophisticated systems for recognizing, classifying, and utilizing plants based on their habitat, form, availability, and practical value [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. Such knowledge is not static; rather, it evolves through continuous interaction with changing environments and social conditions, forming a living cultural heritage closely tied to local ecosystems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>].</p><p id="Par6">Traditional medicine represents one of the most visible and enduring expressions of ethnobotanical knowledge. Through ethnomedicine, communities interpret illness and health in ways that integrate physical, social, and spiritual dimensions, drawing on locally available biological resources [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>]. In many parts of the developing world, traditional medicine continues to serve as the primary and, in some cases, the only accessible form of healthcare [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>]. This is particularly true in Ethiopia, where limited access to modern medical facilities, especially in rural and pastoral areas, has sustained a strong reliance on plant-based remedies. It is estimated that 80–90% of the population depends on traditional MPs for primary healthcare needs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>]. Ethiopian healing traditions are deeply embedded in cultural belief systems and extend beyond the treatment of disease to encompass mental well-being, social harmony, and spiritual balance [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par7">Despite its continued importance, traditional medicinal knowledge in Ethiopia is facing increasing pressure from rapid socio-economic and environmental change [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>]. Expanding agriculture, urbanization, deforestation, and shifts in lifestyle and education have disrupted customary systems of knowledge transmission, leading to a gradual decline in ethnomedicinal expertise, particularly among younger generations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>]. From an ecological perspective, this erosion has broader consequences. Indigenous knowledge often guides sustainable harvesting, seasonal use, and species selection, and its loss can accelerate biodiversity decline and weaken community-based conservation practices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>].</p><p id="Par8">In eastern Ethiopia, remnant forest ecosystems such as the Karamara forest patches play a vital role in sustaining both biodiversity and traditional livelihoods.
