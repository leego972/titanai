The availability of these plant species was firstly revealed by their spatial accessibility, explained by their wide spread distribution and cultivation; secondly, it was explicated by their seasonal stock and the quantities of plant material sold throughout the year. Similarly to Venezuela, Peru, and Bolivia, these plant species are used individually or in herbal mixtures infusions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>], especially to treat different kinds of afflictions related to the nervous, digestive, and genitourinary systems.</p></sec><sec id="Sec11"><title>Vernacular names of medicinal plant species traded in Loja province</title><p>A total of 204 vernacular names were recorded for the 160 medicinal plant species; 59.4 % of the species had at least one name, 36.1 % had two names and 4.5 % had three names. Most of the vernacular names used to identify each plant were in Spanish, followed by a few in Kichwa language from the Andean highlands. The name given to the whole plant is the same name given to the plant’s morphological structure. In the case of gathering wild plant species belonging to the <italic toggle="yes">Malva</italic> genus or cultivated hybrids of the <italic toggle="yes">Fuchsia</italic> genus, the vendors recognized all the species in the genus with the same vernacular name.</p><p>The vernacular names of the medicinal plants compiled in this study were compared to others from previous studies conducted at other traditional markets in Bolivia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>], Peru [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>], and Ecuador [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR83">83</xref>] as well as ethnobotanical surveys in the Andean highlands [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR125">125</xref>] and Spain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR124">124</xref>]. The most common five medicinal plant species that share the same cosmopolitan vernacular names were <italic toggle="yes">Dysphania ambrosioides</italic> (“paico”), <italic toggle="yes">Equisetum giganteum</italic> (“cola de caballo”), <italic toggle="yes">Matricaria recutita</italic> (“manzanilla”), <italic toggle="yes">Melissa officinalis</italic> (“toronjil”), and <italic toggle="yes">Rosmarinus officinalis</italic> (“romero”).</p></sec><sec id="Sec12"><title>Medicinal plants: morphological structures and therapeutic administration</title><p>The therapeutic administration recommended by the market vendors revealed the 13 kinds of medicinal plant morphological structures sold, where each one was used to treat a human organ and/or fluid. The most frequently traded morphological structures were branches for 44 medicinal plant species (27.5 %), followed by leaves (25.6 %), flowers (16.9 %), and plants without roots (16.9 %). The less frequently sold morphological structures, available only at 18 open markets, were bark, fruit, inflorescence, latex, seed, stem, style, root, and wood.</p><p>The research identified 20 modes of therapeutic administration (Table <xref rid="Tab5" ref-type="table">5</xref>): 14 were prepared using fresh plant material sold at 18 open markets, and six used dried plant material sold at 15 established markets. Oral was the most frequent mode of therapeutic administration (83.8 %), prepared with fresh and/or dry plants, especially in a drink locally known as “bajeada”. Compared to other Andean markets, oral infusions have similar preparations in Bolivia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>] and Cuzco [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR117">117</xref>].</p><p>Other therapeutic administrations were rubbing (16.9 %), topical applications (8.7 %), hot baths after childbirth (5.6 %), and cleaning wounds (5 %). The majority of the prescribed medicinal plants given to patients by vendors were applied without any standardized doses. Only a few elder vendors made warnings about adverse side effects of some medicinal plants, but they never mentioned antidotes. The six most common orally administered medicinal plant species were <italic toggle="yes">Aerva sanguinolenta</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus hybridus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Equisetum bogotense, Matricaria recutita</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Melissa officinalis,</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Oreocallis grandiflora.</italic></p><p>The therapeutic administration, locally known with the term “zumo”, generally refers to the extract of a plant morphological structure or fruit pure juice, thus differentiating “zumo” from “jugo” (juice); the latter is associated with the fruit diluted in an amount of water [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>].
