Similar patterns have been documented in Tanzania and Nigeria, indicating that ethnomedicinal knowledge is closely tied to lifestyle, occupation, and cultural roles [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR123">123</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">132</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR190">190</xref>].</p><p id="Par196">Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of familial transmission, age, and practical experience in shaping medicinal plant knowledge. However, the declining number of traditional healers and socio-economic changes pose threats to the continuity of indigenous knowledge. Globally, comparable challenges are reported in Europe, America, Asia, and Australia, where oral transmission remains central to ethnobotanical learning but is increasingly vulnerable to modernization and urbanization [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>]. These results emphasize the urgent need for systematic documentation, community-based preservation programs, and integration of traditional practices into education and health initiatives to ensure the sustainability of ethnobotanical knowledge in Sheikh District.</p><p id="Par197">
