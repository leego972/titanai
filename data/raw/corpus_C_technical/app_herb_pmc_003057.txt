# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

colspan="1" rowspan="1">7th</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">5th</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">6th</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">4th</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">7th</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">3rd</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">2nd</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">5th</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1"/><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1"/></tr></tbody></table></table-wrap> </p></sec><sec id="Sec23"><title>Novel ethnobotanical findings from Karamara forest patches</title><p id="Par133">The ethnobotanical survey of the Somali community in Karamara forest patches revealed several novel medicinal uses, plant-part applications, and preparation methods, expanding the knowledge of Ethiopia’s traditional medicinal flora. Several species exhibited therapeutic applications not previously reported in the region. For instance, <italic toggle="yes">Withania somnifera</italic> roots are used to manage common cold, while leaves are administered for epilepsy, demonstrating a dual-purpose therapeutic role. Similarly, <italic toggle="yes">Opuntia ficus-indica</italic> is ingested to treat amoebic infections, <italic toggle="yes">Cadaba glandulosa</italic> leaves are applied topically for skin infections, and <italic toggle="yes">Iphionopsis rotundifolia</italic> roots are chewed for gastric disorders, all representing new ethnomedicinal uses for the region.</p><p id="Par134">Several species showed novel combinations of plant parts and preparation methods. <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic> leaves are consumed to manage hypertension in addition to their nutritional role, reflecting an integrated food-medicine function. <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic> leaves and latex are applied topically for ringworm, while fruits are ingested for gastrointestinal complaints, highlighting dual-use practices previously unrecorded in eastern Ethiopia. <italic toggle="yes">Ocimum lamiifolium</italic> leaves are inhaled to alleviate cough, common cold, and headache, illustrating a novel route of administration. Additionally, <italic toggle="yes">Clerodendrum myricoides</italic> roots are chewed to relieve toothache, showing a localized adaptation of known therapeutic applications.</p><p id="Par135">Several under-studied taxa were documented with new medicinal applications. <italic toggle="yes">Cadaba glandulosa</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Iphionopsis rotundifolia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Opilia campestris</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Asepalum eriantherum</italic> are used for ailments ranging from gastrointestinal disorders to wound healing and snakebite management. Resinous species such as <italic toggle="yes">Boswellia rivae</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora myrrha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora hodai</italic> are applied for snakebites, headaches, and gastrointestinal issues, revealing specific therapeutic roles not previously highlighted in Ethiopian literature.</p><p id="Par136">The survey also highlighted dual-use species, demonstrating an important interface between nutrition and healthcare. Plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Opuntia ficus-indica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus mauritiana</italic> serve both as food and medicine, supporting household nutritional security while contributing to traditional healthcare. Such integrated use has not been previously documented in dryland forest systems of eastern Ethiopia, emphasizing the multifunctional role of medicinal flora in local livelihoods.</p><p id="Par137">Finally, several taxa are used for culturally specific and ritualistic purposes. <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic> is burned or inhaled to ward off evil spirits, and <italic toggle="yes">Punica granatum</italic> is ingested to counteract culturally recognized spiritual afflictions. These practices highlight the cultural and psychosocial importance of medicinal plants, reflecting their role in maintaining social identity and traditional knowledge systems in Karamara.</p></sec><sec id="Sec24"><title>Comparison with previous ethnobotanical studies using the Jaccard similarity index</title><p id="Par138">The MPs documented in the Karamara forest patches were compared with previous ethnobotanical surveys across Ethiopia to assess species overlap and knowledge similarity. The Jaccard Similarity Index (JI) quantified the proportion of shared species between Karamara and other study areas (Table <xref rid="Tab8" ref-type="table">8</xref>).</p><p id="Par139">JI values ranged from 10.7% to 79.4%, reflecting both ecological differences and ethnocultural specificity in medicinal plant use. The highest similarity was observed with Jigjiga town and Jigjiga district, indicating strong overlap in species composition and traditional knowledge among nearby Somali communities.</p><p id="Par140">Moderate similarity (20–35%) occurred with Guraferda, Yeki, Armachiho, Bita, and Asagirt, suggesting partial sharing of medicinal plant knowledge across regions.
