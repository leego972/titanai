Such methodologies are particularly relevant for slow-growing species, those with limited natural distribution, or taxa threatened by overharvesting, offering a sustainable and scalable alternative to conventional cultivation practices [<xref rid="B34-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>,<xref rid="B36-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>].</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Polyscias fruticosa</italic>, a pharmacologically promising member of the Araliaceae family, has gained attention due to its rich content of bioactive secondary metabolites, particularly triterpenoid saponins. However, the traditional cultivation of this species is constrained by low biomass yield, poor seed germination rates, and an extended vegetative period, significantly limiting its applicability in industrial phytopharmaceutical production [<xref rid="B17-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B22-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>].</p><p>In vitro propagation systems represent a viable solution to these constraints, providing rapid, reproducible, and environmentally independent means of generating uniform plant material. These systems also facilitate metabolic studies and the targeted enhancement of pharmacologically relevant compounds through the use of biotic and abiotic elicitors. The present study reviews and compares the efficacy of the main regenerative strategies for <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticose</italic>—namely, somatic embryogenesis, adventitious root culture, and suspension culture—with particular emphasis on their applicability in phytochemical and pharmacognostic research.</p><sec id="sec4dot2dot1-molecules-30-03460"><title>4.2.1. In Vitro Regeneration of <italic toggle="yes">Polyscias fruticosa</italic>: Embryogenesis and Shoot Apices</title><p>Somatic embryogenesis remains a widely adopted method for clonal propagation in plant biotechnology [<xref rid="B37-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="B38-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]. Within the Araliaceae family, this technique has been successfully applied to species such as <italic toggle="yes">Panax ginseng</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Eleutherococcus senticosus</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Aralia elata</italic> [<xref rid="B35-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>,<xref rid="B36-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="B37-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="B38-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]. The process involves the differentiation of somatic cells into embryogenic structures that mimic the developmental stages of zygotic embryos—globular, heart-shaped, torpedo, and cotyledonary. The embryogenic transition can proceed directly from explants or indirectly through a callus phase, offering high regeneration efficiency and facilitating adaptation to bioreactor-based systems [<xref rid="B39-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>,<xref rid="B40-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>].</p><p>Meristem-based micropropagation utilizes shoot apices or axillary buds as explants and exploits the high mitotic activity and low differentiation status of meristematic tissues [<xref rid="B41-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. This approach allows for the rapid proliferation of genetically stable and pathogen-free plantlets, minimizing somaclonal variation and chimerism. It is particularly advantageous when maintaining clonal fidelity is essential for phytopharmaceutical applications.</p><p>Both somatic embryogenesis and meristematic propagation have proven effective for <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>, enabling the generation of uniform, high-quality explants for downstream metabolic and biotechnological applications [<xref rid="B42-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>].</p><p>Tram et al. [<xref rid="B43-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>] established an efficient protocol for somatic embryogenesis of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> using leaf explants from two-year-old ex vitro plants. Callus induction was achieved on MS [<xref rid="B44-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>] medium with 0.3 mg·L<sup>−1</sup> 2,4-D, 2.0 mg·L<sup>−1</sup> BAP, and 7 mL/L coconut water. Subculturing onto MS medium with 0.5 mg·L<sup>−1</sup> BAP and 100 mL/L CW resulted in a high embryogenesis rate (75.5%). Embryos developed into shoots on MS medium with 0.1 mg·L<sup>−1</sup> kinetin and 150 mL/L CW, producing on average 6.3 shoots per explant.</p><p>These results indicate that the combination of BAP and CW is highly effective in promoting embryogenesis and shoot conversion, confirming the morphogenic potential of coconut-derived additives.</p><p>Phuong et al. [<xref rid="B24-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>] established embryogenic suspension cultures from leaf-derived calli in liquid MS medium with 2,4-D or NAA (0.5–2.0 mg·L<sup>−1</sup>), achieving the highest biomass (5.7 g/flask) and somatic embryo yield (489/flask) at 1.5 mg·L<sup>−1</sup> NAA.
