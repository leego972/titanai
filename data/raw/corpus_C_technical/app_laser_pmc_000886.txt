The EHF model requires codependent sample-related parameters, which cannot be determined independently without <italic toggle="yes">a priori</italic> knowledge of <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math168"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi>s</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> or <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math169"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>g</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. For Monte-Carlo-based estimations, the same problem arises, for which the scattering phase function has to be assumed. Although multiple models for the OCT signal have been studied in the literature, the most frequently applied one remains the single exponential model, relying upon the predominance of single scattering, such that the first-order Born approximation is valid. The single exponential model, together with accurate correction for system parameters, provides a valid approach without the mutual dependence of the fit parameters. Alternatively, the depth-resolved method can be applied to estimate <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math170"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>OCT</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> per pixel in depth, avoiding assumptions of tissue homogeneity in the axial direction. Although more work is undoubtedly needed to establish the level of model sophistication required, it is likely that meaningful results require one to be in an image-forming regime, and that image formation likely requires the single scattering assumption to be valid.
