Similar home-garden strategies have been reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, China, and India, where cultivated MPs function both as conservation reservoirs and as accessible sources of nutrition and therapy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>].</p><p id="Par99">Some species, such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe spp.</italic>, were obtained from both wild and cultivated environments depending on seasonal availability and the plant part required. For instance, latex may be collected from wild stands, while cultivation near homesteads provides continuous access for routine household use. This dual sourcing strategy demonstrates the flexibility of indigenous knowledge systems in maintaining key medicinal resources. Comparable adaptive practices have been reported in Uganda, Tanzania, and parts of Europe, where communities combine wild harvesting with small-scale cultivation to sustain traditional remedies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR120">120</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR124">124</xref>].</p><p id="Par100">However, the heavy reliance on wild habitats also raises conservation concerns. MPs harvested exclusively from natural ecosystems particularly those collected for roots, bark, or whole plants are especially vulnerable to overexploitation, land-use change, deforestation, and climate variability. Similar threats have been reported in Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Cameroon, emphasizing the need for sustainable harvesting guidelines and community-based conservation initiatives [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR125">125</xref>].</p><p id="Par101">Overall, wild ecosystems remain the primary source of MPs in Sheikh District, while home gardens provide an important supplementary source for frequently used taxa. These findings highlight the importance of integrated conservation strategies that combine sustainable wild harvesting with the promotion of household and community-level cultivation. Such approaches are essential not only for protecting medicinal plant diversity but also for safeguarding the cultural knowledge systems that continue to underpin primary healthcare in Somaliland and comparable dryland regions worldwide.</p></sec><sec id="Sec16"><title>Ethnomedicinal uses and plant parts employed in traditional healthcare</title><p id="Par102">The present study documented 54 medicinal plant species belonging to 34 botanical families that are traditionally used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District to manage a wide range of human health conditions. These remedies address ailments affecting multiple body systems, including gastrointestinal, respiratory, febrile, dermatological, ocular, musculoskeletal, metabolic, urogenital, and infectious disorders. The breadth of ailments treated reflects the depth, adaptability, and continued relevance of local ethnomedicinal knowledge.</p><p id="Par103">A similar reliance on plant-based healthcare has been widely reported throughout East Africa—particularly in Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania where traditional medicine remains essential due to limited healthcare infrastructure and strong cultural continuity of indigenous healing systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR126">126</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR131">131</xref>].</p><p id="Par104">Gastrointestinal disorders were the most frequently treated category, with 17 species (31.4%) reported for conditions such as diarrhea, stomachache, constipation, and intestinal parasitic infections. This pattern mirrors findings from Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Cameroon, where digestive ailments commonly dominate ethnomedicinal practice [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR102">102</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">132</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR133">133</xref>].</p><p id="Par105">The prominence of gastrointestinal treatments may be culturally and environmentally linked to challenges such as unsafe drinking water, sanitation limitations, and dietary stress in rural semi-arid settings. In Sheikh District, species such <italic toggle="yes">as Citrullus colocynthis</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Maytenus undata</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Psidium guajava</italic> were widely used, consistent with ethnobotanical reports from East Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East that highlight their antimicrobial, antidiarrheal, and anti-inflammatory potential [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR52">52</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR134">134</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR136">136</xref>].</p><p id="Par106">Respiratory and febrile illnesses were treated using ten species (18.5%), emphasizing the importance of MPs in managing colds, influenza-like symptoms, and fever.
