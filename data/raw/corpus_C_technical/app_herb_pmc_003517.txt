</p></sec><sec id="Sec18"><title>Routes, mode of administration, application, and dosage of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par127">MPs documented in Sheikh District were administered primarily through oral, dermal (topical), and ocular (eye) routes. Among these, oral administration was the most frequently employed, followed by dermal application and ocular use. Oral remedies were predominantly used to treat systemic conditions, including gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, diabetes, respiratory infections, and fever, reflecting the community’s reliance on liquid formulations for internal delivery. This pattern is consistent with observations from Mana Angetu and Artuma Fursi districts in Ethiopia, where over 50% of traditional remedies were administered orally for systemic ailments, with topical applications used less frequently [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR172">172</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR173">173</xref>]. Similarly, in Yunnan, China, oral preparations are preferred due to perceived efficacy and ease of absorption [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR141">141</xref>].</p><p id="Par128">Dermal applications were primarily targeted at localized conditions such as wounds, skin infections, eczema, and insect bites. Remedies were applied as crushed or powdered plant parts, pastes, or latex, demonstrating the community’s nuanced understanding of disease localization. Comparable practices have been reported in Kenya and Uganda, where topical applications of plant-based pastes and ointments are widely used to manage skin ailments and external injuries [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR174">174</xref>]. Ocular administration, although less frequent, was applied for eye conditions such as conjunctivitis. Plant juices from species such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe citrina</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Olea europaea</italic> were applied directly to the eyes, a practice also documented in Pakistan, illustrating targeted use of MPs for specific ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR175">175</xref>].</p><p id="Par129">Preparation methods varied according to plant part and route of administration. Oral remedies were generally prepared by crushing, grinding, or powdering plant parts and mixing them with water, tea, milk, or other carriers. Examples include <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> bulbs crushed and mixed with water for the common cold, <italic toggle="yes">Moringa stenopetala</italic> leaves powdered and consumed with tea for diabetes, and <italic toggle="yes">Citrullus colocynthis</italic> seeds dried, crushed, and mixed with water to treat diarrhea. Dermal applications involved direct application of pastes, crushed plant parts, or latex to affected areas, such as <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic> latex for white eczema, <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic> latex for wound healing, and burned <italic toggle="yes">Momordica charantia</italic> leaves to repel mosquitoes. Ocular remedies included squeezed or extracted plant juices applied directly to the eyes, as with <italic toggle="yes">Aloe citrina</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Olea europaea</italic>, or <italic toggle="yes">Terminalia prunioides</italic> latex mixed with water for conjunctivitis. These preparation methods align with ethnobotanical practices in China, India, and Pakistan, where crushing, decoction, and infusion techniques are widely used to maximize medicinal efficacy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR176">176</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR179">179</xref>].</p><p id="Par130">Among the various methods of application, drinking was the most predominant (42.1%), followed by application to the skin (14.3%) and washing with plant extracts (13.3%). Dosage was largely informal and guided by traditional knowledge. Local healers determined quantities based on patient age, type and location of illness, severity, disease stage, physical condition, and extent of infection.
