The Yalo Woreda is malarious, and the people used 37 plant species to treat the illness, and 57 plant species as a treatment for liver and gastrointestinal infections that are developed over long years of trial and error similar to other studies conducted in the country [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR88">88</xref>]. The ethnobotanical study conducted in pastoral area Samburu district, Kenya has similar result that malaria and GIT are treated with 15 to 20 medicinal plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>]. The majority of the plants with fidelity value of 1.00 was reported by few informants (one to two) and was used only as a treatment for an ailment. <italic toggle="yes">Aizoon canariensis</italic> L. is used to treat devil disease and madness, On the other hand, medicinal plants that were used to treat a variety of ailments had lower FL values. The values, in such cases, may not indicate the disagreement among the informants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>] rather it means that these plants are more favored by the local people in the treatment of a variety of diseases such as diphtheria, typhoid, herpes zoster, scorpion bite, devil illness, ETPB, lung infection, dysentery, breast cancer, and angina [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref>]. The medicinal plants reported by 15 and more informants and used as a remedy for multiple diseases were ranked based on their healing potential of a disease. <italic toggle="yes">Balanites rotundifolia</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">A. fruticosa</italic> are used as remedies for diseases such as breast cancer, dyspepsia, epilepsy, ETPB, eye sickness, herpes zoster, infant disease, jaundice, lung infection, and malaria [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR68">68</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR81">81</xref>]. These plants have lower FL values indicating the popularity of the plants as a treatment for a variety of diseases. Therefore, preference ranking does not necessarily indicate healing potential of plants but also their popularity and may be abundance in the study area since their fruits are edible. Other societies also report the medicinal plants as a remedy for multiple diseases such as throat infection, stomachache/diarrhea; sexual incompetence of male; body infection; skin wound, snake bite, madness, typhus, eye problem, anthrax; rabies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR82">82</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec20"><title>Wild edible plants of Yalo Woreda</title><p id="Par44">The wild edible plant parts are all eaten fresh, and duration of gathering and consumption are dependent on the availability of edible parts and seasons [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>]. The seasons are determined by the extent of rain and drought in the region. Sugum is the period of little rainfall (March to April), and Karma (June to September) is the main rain season. Gilal (October to February) and Hagay (May to June) are the dry seasons. Hagay extends to Karma and Gilal to Sugum depending on the length of rain and drought period [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>]. Borena, Kara, and Kwego pastoralist and people in semiarid areas in Ethiopia also consume the edible plants reported such as <italic toggle="yes">B. aegyptiaca</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">B. rotundifolia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Dobera glabra</italic> (Forssk.) Poir., <italic toggle="yes">Carissa spinarum</italic> L., <italic toggle="yes">C.sinensis</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">G. bicolor, G. villosa, T.amarindus indica</italic> L., <italic toggle="yes">X americana</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Z. spina-christi</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR118">118</xref>]. The people in semi-arid and arid areas rely more on edible plants compared to people that inhabit humid regions and highlands.
