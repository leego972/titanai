# PMC: adaptogen stress response HPA axis — Overview

The World Health Organization has called stress “the health epidemic of the 21st century,” recognizing its substantial impact on personal life and also its social and economic consequences (Anghelescu et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0005" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>; Subhani et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0058" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>).</p><p>Stress management strategies include nonpharmacological approaches, such as cognitive behavioral therapy and relaxation, but recourse to pharmacological treatment is standard if stress and its symptoms become harmful. Anxiolytics and antidepressants, associated with known risks of adverse effects and dependency, are generally indicated for more severe situations. Several plants, including chamomile, melissa, and rhodiola, have been shown to be valuable for managing stress and its consequences, with fewer adverse effects and a lower risk of dependency (Sarris, McIntyre, &amp; Camfield, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0055" ref-type="ref">2013</xref>). <italic toggle="yes">Rhodiola rosea</italic> L. (rosenroot or golden root), manifesting adaptogenic properties, is among those most widely used (Anghelescu et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0005" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>; Kasper &amp; Dienel, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0029" ref-type="ref">2017</xref>). Extracts of adaptogenic plants can normalize body functions and reinforce systems compromised by stress (Anghelescu et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0005" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>). They have no specific pharmacological properties and act by increasing resistance to a broad spectrum of adverse expressions of stress. Preclinical in vivo and ex vivo studies in animal models and experiments on cell lines have highlighted several biochemical and pharmacological stress‐reducing properties of <italic toggle="yes">R. rosea</italic> extracts (Abidov, Crendal, Grachev, Seifulla, &amp; Ziegenfuss, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0001" ref-type="ref">2003</xref>; Olsson, von Scheele, &amp; Panossian, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0044" ref-type="ref">2009</xref>; Panossian, Hambardzumyan, Hovhanissyan, &amp; Wikman, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0045" ref-type="ref">2007</xref>; Panossian, Hovhannisyan, Abrahamyan, Gabrielyan, &amp; Wikman, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0046" ref-type="ref">2009</xref>). In clinical studies, various extracts of <italic toggle="yes">R. rosea</italic> were found to be effective and safe, improving mental work capacity, concentration, task performance, fatigue, burnout symptoms, and overall mood, besides reducing stress level and self‐reported mild anxiety (Cropley, Banks, &amp; Boyle, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0010" ref-type="ref">2015</xref>; Darbinyan et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0011" ref-type="ref">2000</xref>; Edwards, Heufelder, &amp; Zimmermann, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0019" ref-type="ref">2012</xref>; Kasper &amp; Dienel, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0029" ref-type="ref">2017</xref>; Panossian, Wikman, Kaur, &amp; Asea, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0048" ref-type="ref">2009</xref>; Punja, Shamseer, Olson, &amp; Vohra, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0050" ref-type="ref">2014</xref>). <italic toggle="yes">R. rosea</italic> was approved by the European Medicines Agency Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products for the indication “temporary relief of symptoms of stress such as fatigue and sensation of weakness” (EMA/HPMC, <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0020" ref-type="ref">2012</xref>).</p><p>Stress response typically begins with activation of the hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, one of the main stress response pathways, and the production of corticosteroids (Anghelescu et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0005" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>; Subhani et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0058" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>). Acute or chronic stress produces characteristic changes in the HPA axis, including an increase in cortisol in humans and corticosterone in rodents, as well as a reduction in the sensitivity of the HPA axis to feedback down‐regulation (Anghelescu et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0005" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>; Panossian, Wikman, et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0048" ref-type="ref">2009</xref>). Chronic stress results in persistent elevation of cortisol or corticosterone levels, which may lead to fatigue, depression, and other symptoms (Anghelescu et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0005" ref-type="ref">2018</xref>). The reduction in stress‐induced damage by <italic toggle="yes">R. rosea</italic> is characterized by a decrease in or the prevention of hormonal changes characteristic of stress, including cortisol or corticosterone release, as shown in humans suffering from chronic stress following administration of the standardized <italic toggle="yes">R. rosea</italic> root extract SHR‐5 during 28 days (Olsson et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0044" ref-type="ref">2009</xref>) and in rabbits subjected to acute stress after 7 days of SHR‐5 administration (Panossian et al., <xref rid="fsn31249-bib-0045" ref-type="ref">2007</xref>).
