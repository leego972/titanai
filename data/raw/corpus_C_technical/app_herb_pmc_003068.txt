Comparable practices are reported in Kenya and Nigeria, where dried roots and bark are preferred for oral remedies targeting gastrointestinal and systemic disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].</p><p id="Par197">Certain species were used both fresh and dried, depending on seasonal availability and intended therapeutic purpose. For example, <italic toggle="yes">A. nilotica</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. mexicana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">S. coagulans</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">A. pirottae</italic> were administered interchangeably in either form. Similar flexibility has been observed in traditional medical systems of India, China, and Pakistan, where both fresh and dried materials are used to ensure continuous access to medicinal resources throughout the year [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par198">Globally, this dual approach reflects consistent ethnomedicinal logic: fresh materials are favored for topical or immediate treatments, while dried materials are preferred for storage, standardization, and oral administration [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>]. In Europe and the Americas, similar practices are reported, where fresh leaves or fruits are used for direct application, and roots, seeds, and bark are dried to extend shelf life and maintain consistent dosage in traditional pharmacopoeias [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR90">90</xref>].</p><p id="Par199">The predominance of fresh plant use in the Karamara forest patches highlights the community’s close connection to its natural environment and reliance on readily accessible resources. Conversely, the use of dried materials demonstrates adaptive strategies for resource preservation and year-round medicinal availability. Together, these practices reflect a well-balanced ethnobotanical system that supports both cultural continuity and ecological sustainability.</p></sec><sec id="Sec35"><title>Dosage of Administration, Additives, and diagnostic features for medicinal plants</title><p id="Par200">In the Karamara forest patches, the dosage of medicinal plant remedies varies according to the type of ailment, patient age, and overall health condition. Traditional healers determine dosages using practical, locally available tools such as cups, spoons, liters, or the palm of the hand. This flexible, experience-based approach reflects the adaptive nature of traditional medicine, where individualized treatment is central to healing practices. Similar dosage variability has been documented in other parts of Ethiopia and across Africa, emphasizing the personalized nature of ethnomedical systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>].</p><p id="Par201">Diagnosis within the Somali community of Karamara relies primarily on a combination of patient interviews and visual observation of symptoms. Healers assess indicators such as changes in urine color, skin and eye tone, tongue appearance, and the presence of swelling, bleeding, or visible parasites. This empirical and symptom-based diagnostic method parallels ethnomedical traditions elsewhere, including India, Pakistan, and Sudan, where healers integrate visual inspection and patient-reported experiences to guide diagnosis and treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>]. In traditional Chinese medicine, similar diagnostic techniques such as visual and pulse examination are also employed to determine the most appropriate dosage and remedy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par202">Additives play an important role in the preparation and administration of herbal remedies. While most treatments in Karamara are administered without additional substances, some incorporate water, milk, coffee, honey, or meat to enhance taste, efficacy, or spiritual potency. Water, particularly cold water, serves as the principal solvent for decoctions and infusions, while milk and oils are used for spiritual cleansing or topical application, as observed with <italic toggle="yes">P. granatum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Z. mauritiana</italic>. Comparable use of solvents and additives has been reported in traditional medicine systems across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, where such ingredients aid in extracting active compounds, improving palatability, and enhancing therapeutic effectiveness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR90">90</xref>].</p><p id="Par203">Certain treatments involve heating, burning, or smoking plant materials, particularly for managing respiratory problems, pain, or spiritual ailments.
