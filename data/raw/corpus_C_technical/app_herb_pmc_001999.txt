This enzymatic inhibition is further complemented by the extract’s anti-inflammatory properties, mediated via suppression of the NF-κB signaling cascade, and its stimulation of the Nrf2-dependent antioxidant response pathway [<xref rid="B160-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">160</xref>].</p><p>In comparison, <italic toggle="yes">Morus alba</italic> (white mulberry) exerts its hypoglycemic effect primarily through 1-deoxynojirimycin (DNJ), a selective α-glucosidase inhibitor with limited or no activity against α-amylase. As a result, its ability to attenuate postprandial hyperglycemia may be less pronounced than that of multitarget agents such as PFS [<xref rid="B160-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">160</xref>]. Moreover, <italic toggle="yes">M. alba</italic> lacks strong evidence of anti-inflammatory or antioxidant effects, which limits its utility in addressing the complex pathophysiology of T2DM.</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Momordica charantia</italic> (bitter melon) contains insulin-mimetic compounds that promote glucose uptake and enhance glycemic control; however, marked variability in phytochemical composition among cultivars and geographic origins has been shown to affect the reproducibility of its hypoglycemic effects [<xref rid="B156-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">156</xref>]. Similarly, <italic toggle="yes">Panax notoginseng</italic> exerts antidiabetic effects mainly through ginsenosides that improve insulin sensitivity and β-cell function, yet the overall magnitude and consistency of these effects remain variable, and the cost and standardization of ginsenoside-rich extracts present additional challenges [<xref rid="B161-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">161</xref>].</p><p>A noteworthy comparison can be drawn with <italic toggle="yes">Aralia elata</italic>, a close relative of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> within the Araliaceae family. This species contains oleanolic acid and triterpenoid saponins such as araloside A, which have demonstrated inhibition of aldose reductase, a key enzyme involved in diabetic complications, and enhancement of glucose homeostasis [<xref rid="B157-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">157</xref>]. This suggests a conserved triterpenoid-based antidiabetic mechanism within the Araliaceae family.</p><p>The multitarget pharmacodynamic profile of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticose</italic>—encompassing digestive enzyme inhibition, modulation of inflammatory signaling, and activation of redox-regulatory pathways—distinguishes it from other botanicals with more limited mechanisms of action. In addition to its low toxicity and traditional dietary use in Southeast Asia, these properties support its potential as a safe, culturally accepted, and biologically effective phytotherapeutic agent.</p><p>Unlike single-mechanism herbs such as <italic toggle="yes">M. alba</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> may yield additive or synergistic effects when co-administered with conventional antidiabetic agents. For instance, co-administration with acarbose has demonstrated additive inhibitory effects on carbohydrate-digesting enzymes, supporting its role in combinatorial phytotherapy [<xref rid="B14-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>].</p><p>Further comparative studies in diabetic animal models and human trials are warranted to confirm the clinical relevance and superiority of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> in managing type 2 diabetes mellitus.</p><p>In our opinion, the unique multitarget action of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> positions it as a more comprehensive phytotherapeutic candidate compared to single-mechanism botanicals. Its synergy with standard drugs further increases its translational relevance in modern diabetes management.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec6dot3-molecules-30-03460"><title>6.3. Potential of Polyscias fruticosa in Neurodegenerative Diseases</title><p>Neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) are characterized by progressive neuronal loss and currently lack effective disease-modifying therapies. Although pharmacological treatments such as levodopa and dopamine agonists offer symptomatic relief, their prolonged use is associated with significant side effects, including neurotoxicity, motor fluctuations, and dyskinesias [<xref rid="B4-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="B20-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="B21-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="B162-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">162</xref>]. These limitations have stimulated growing interest in medicinal plants such as <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> as potential alternative or adjunct therapies.</p><p>Multiple studies have shown that phytochemicals—particularly polyphenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, and saponins—can modulate oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, which are central mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration [<xref rid="B21-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="B162-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">162</xref>].
