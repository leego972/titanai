Hydroxycinnamic acids such as <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>36</bold> (0.042), ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> (0.007), caffeic acid <bold>35</bold> (0.003), <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> (0.002), ellagic acid <bold>44</bold> (12.17), and catechol <bold>45</bold> (6.54) were also identified. The identified compounds were not isolated to confirm their antibacterial activity in pure form. Different work by the same group showed that pure caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold>, and ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> inhibited the growth of <italic toggle="yes">P. carotovorum</italic> subsp at concentrations ranges of 800–3200 μg/mL [<xref rid="B60-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>]. </p><p>Benzoic acid <bold>24</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic acid <bold>31</bold>, and salicylic acid <bold>32</bold> were reported to completely inhibit <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> lpxC/tolC strains at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Furthermore, these benzoic acids were active again in the Gram-positive bacteria <italic toggle="yes">S. aureus</italic> EP167 strain with 100% inhibition at 1 mg/mL [<xref rid="B61-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>]. Benzoic acid <bold>24</bold> and its hydroxyl derivatives were active against Gram-negative bacteria due to their ability to cross the bacteria’s hydrophilic outer membrane and exert their antibacterial properties [<xref rid="B62-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>].</p><p>Hydroxycinnamic acids such as caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>36</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37,</bold> and ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> were reported to have broad-spectrum antibacterial activities against Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogen bacteria, including the <italic toggle="yes">Mycobacterium tuberculosis</italic> (H37Rv) [<xref rid="B63-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>]. For instance, <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>36</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold>, caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>, and ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> were shown to be active against the <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> 0157:H7 strain with MIC values of 2.74 μM, 2.74 μM, 1.94 mM, and 2.23 mM, respectively [<xref rid="B64-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">64</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-Coumaric acid <bold>36</bold> and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> were tested against <italic toggle="yes">M. tuberculosis</italic> H37Rv and showed MIC values of 122 μM and 244 μM, respectively [<xref rid="B65-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">65</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-Coumaric acid <bold>36</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37,</bold> and caffeic acid <bold>35</bold> were shown to be active against <italic toggle="yes">S. aureus</italic> #917 with MIC values of 760 μM, 761 μM, and 694 μM, respectively [<xref rid="B66-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">66</xref>].</p><p>Ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> was also reported to be active against the pathogens of concern, such as <italic toggle="yes">Enterococcus faecalis</italic> ATCC 2921 (MIC = 659 uM), <italic toggle="yes">Klebsiella pneumoniae</italic> RSKK 574 (MIC = 1.3 mM) [<xref rid="B67-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">Pseudomonas aeruginosa</italic> ATCC 10,145 (MIC = 515 μM) [<xref rid="B68-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">68</xref>]. The literature indicates that hydroxycinnamic acids displayed marked antibacterial properties. A possible mechanism for their inhibition of bacterial growth or bactericidal properties is their damage to the bacteria’s cell wall [<xref rid="B69-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">69</xref>]. Ferulic acid <bold>38</bold> and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> were shown to interact with the cell membranes of the bacteria and disrupt the phospholipid or lipid bilayers. The loss of cell membrane integrity resulted in the formation of pores and increasing membrane permeability [<xref rid="B70-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">70</xref>]. In addition, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> was shown to disrupt membrane permeability and create pores in <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">S. dysenteriae</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">S. Typhimurium</italic> [<xref rid="B71-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">71</xref>].
