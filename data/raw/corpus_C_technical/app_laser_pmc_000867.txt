# PMC: optical coherence tomography clinical application — Overview

The scattering coefficient depends both on the wavelength and the scatterer dimensions. In elastic scattering, which is relevant for OCT, no energy conversion takes place, but scattered light is spatially redirected and so the intensity of an incident wave is diminished. The scattering phase function describes this process of angular redistribution of energy. It is often convenient to parameterize the phase function in terms of Legendre moments, the first of which is called the scattering anisotropy <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math22"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>g</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. It physically corresponds to the average cosine of the scattering angle (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math23"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>g</mml:mi><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, thus, implies all light is scattered in the forward direction). The phase function and scattering anisotropy are dependent on wavelength and scatterer dimensions as well. In general, large particles (with respect to wavelength) will scatter more strongly in the forward direction.</p><p>The absorption and scattering coefficients are formally defined in terms of interaction probability per unit path length. Their sum is the attenuation coefficient, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math24"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi>t</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi>a</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo>+</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi>s</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, which describes the decay of the incident light due to the tissue optical properties. In contrast, the OCT attenuation coefficient, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math25"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>OCT</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, parameterizes the loss of OCT signal with depth, caused by absorption and scattering, when this loss is modeled as a single exponential decay in the form of Beer’s law, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math26"><mml:mrow><mml:mo>∝</mml:mo><mml:mi>exp</mml:mi><mml:mo stretchy="false">(</mml:mo><mml:mo>−</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>OCT</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub><mml:mi>z</mml:mi><mml:mo stretchy="false">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. This simple parameterization absorbs most of the complexity, but sacrifices a direct relation to tissue optical properties (e.g., anisotropy, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math27"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>g</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>; <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math28"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>a</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>; <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math29"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>s</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) for the sake of robustness and uniqueness of the measurement. In addition, OCT system properties, such as the confocal point spread function (CPSF) and sensitivity roll-off (for Fourier-domain OCT systems) also cause a depth-dependent response. We will discuss in Sec. <xref ref-type="sec" rid="sec3">3</xref> how various signal models summarize tissue optical properties in a single parameter, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math30"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>OCT</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. Modeling the connection between tissue structure, optical properties, and measured quantities remains a formidable challenge, which we will not resolve in this review.
