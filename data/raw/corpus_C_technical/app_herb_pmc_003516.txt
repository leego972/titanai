In Sheikh District, decoctions prepared from <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Sterculia africana</italic> were commonly used for respiratory illnesses and febrile conditions. Such methods enhance extraction efficiency and are widely associated with the treatment of systemic infections. Comparable uses of decoction have been documented across East Africa, South Asia, and Latin America, highlighting a cross-cultural convergence in addressing fever and respiratory disease through heated herbal preparations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR158">158</xref>].</p><p id="Par120">Direct application of latex or sap was reported for a limited number of species, including <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia tortilis</italic>, mainly for wound healing and skin infections. Latex-based remedies are culturally valued for their strong antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, although they often require careful handling due to potential toxicity. Similar topical applications of latex-bearing plants have been recorded in Africa, India, and Pakistan, reflecting shared ethnomedical perceptions of plant exudates as powerful healing agents [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR98">98</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR140">140</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR166">166</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR167">167</xref>].</p><p id="Par121">Medicinal remedies were prepared using fresh, dried, or both fresh and dried plant materials, reflecting indigenous knowledge of harvesting cycles, storage strategies, and potency preservation. Fresh materials accounted for 52.4% of preparations, emphasizing the cultural preference for immediacy and perceived therapeutic strength. For example, fresh bulbs of <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic> were crushed to treat colds, while fresh leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Aloe citrina</italic> were applied for eye infections.</p><p id="Par122">Dried materials represented 36.2% of remedies and were particularly common for roots, seeds, and bark, allowing continued availability during dry seasons or periods of scarcity. Examples include dried seeds of <italic toggle="yes">Citrullus colocynthis</italic> for diarrhea and dried roots of <italic toggle="yes">Maytenus undata</italic> for stomachache. In 11.4% of cases, communities used both forms interchangeably depending on seasonal accessibility, as observed with <italic toggle="yes">Aloe microdonta</italic> latex for typhoid and jaundice. Similar seasonal flexibility has been documented in Ethiopia, Kenya, and China, suggesting that storage practices are essential for ensuring year-round healthcare resilience in rural environments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR103">103</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR168">168</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR170">170</xref>].</p><p id="Par123">Most remedies (62%) involved a single plant species, while 38% combined two or more species. The presence of polyherbal mixtures suggests a cultural understanding of synergistic effects, especially for complex or chronic ailments. Comparable use of multi-species formulations has been widely reported in Nigeria, India, and Australia, where combined preparations are often perceived as enhancing efficacy and broadening therapeutic scope [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR171">171</xref>].</p><p id="Par124">Hence, the preparation methods documented in Sheikh District demonstrate the depth and sophistication of local ethnobotanical knowledge. The dominance of crushing and grinding reflects practical accessibility, while maceration and decoction indicate culturally refined extraction strategies adapted to different ailments and plant chemistries.</p><p id="Par125">These practices also carry important implications for environmental sustainability and public health. Heavy reliance on fresh material may increase harvesting pressure on wild populations, whereas drying and cultivation can enhance medicinal continuity under climate stress. Understanding preparation modes is therefore essential not only for pharmacological validation but also for designing culturally grounded conservation and healthcare strategies in Somaliland and comparable dryland regions.</p><p id="Par126">
