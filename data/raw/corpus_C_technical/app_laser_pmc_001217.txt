Image courtesy of Eric Swanson, <italic toggle="yes">OCT News</italic>, and Eric Buckland, <italic toggle="yes">Leica</italic> (Bioptigen).<xref rid="r115" ref-type="bibr"><sup>115</sup></xref></p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="JBO-022-121715-g016.jpg"/></fig></sec><sec id="sec4.2"><label>4.2</label><title>Endoscopy</title><p>There are many target tissue sites that can be observed within the human body via an endoscope, especially when enhanced with OCT.<xref rid="r116" ref-type="bibr"><sup>116</sup></xref> Probes with a long distal tip have been used to reach deep into the larynx or the sinuses to allow visualization of these regions. OCT catheters can be directed through the many branches of the cardiovascular system<xref rid="r117" ref-type="bibr"><sup>117</sup></xref> or through the gastrointestinal tract,<xref rid="r118" ref-type="bibr"><sup>118</sup></xref> and needle-based probes can enable an internal view of solid tissues.<xref rid="r119" ref-type="bibr"><sup>119</sup></xref> Ultimately, endoscopic systems allow a user to view tissue within the subject that would otherwise not be possible. These systems rely on their shape or rigidity to navigate the potentially tortuous pathways inside the body, and a rotational or forward scanning mechanism to visualize internal structures of interest. Many of these systems attempt to provide an earlier or improved detection rate of cancer, ameliorate the poor survival rate of some cancers,<xref rid="r120" ref-type="bibr"><sup>120</sup></xref> to examine surgical margins,<xref rid="r121" ref-type="bibr"><sup>121</sup></xref> assess for the presence of plaques and weakened arterial structure,<xref rid="r118" ref-type="bibr"><sup>118</sup></xref> or provide contrast or functionality not available with more traditional techniques, such as Doppler OCT.<xref rid="r122" ref-type="bibr"><sup>122</sup></xref></p><p>One such example relies on a tethered pill-like capsule that is used as an OCT probe to produce high-resolution images of the esophagus in order to screen for and detect Barrett’s esophagus.<xref rid="r123" ref-type="bibr"><sup>123</sup></xref> Initial versions of this system were developed and translated through an iterative process to produce the version currently found in the literature, as shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="f17">Fig. 17</xref>. This system was designed for regular clinical use, which took into account the additional considerations and restrictions needed in a clinical environment and to use the device <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>, such as patient safety and comfort, probe design, and rapid speed of imaging, to name only a few. Many of these aspects are detailed in prior publications.<xref rid="r118" ref-type="bibr"><sup>118</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r124" ref-type="bibr"><sup>124</sup></xref> The tethered capsule design provided a means to access part of the stomach and the entire esophagus with OCT, and this system is now being used in outpatient settings.<xref rid="r124" ref-type="bibr"><sup>124</sup></xref> In the future, it may be possible to diagnose pathological esophageal conditions using high-resolution OCT images without the need for biopsy, and do so without the need for specialized healthcare resources or personnel.</p><fig id="f17" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Fig. 17</label><caption><p>Tethered capsule OCT endomicroscopy.
