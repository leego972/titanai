In this study, most respondents (66%, <italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 60) reported learning from family members, while a smaller proportion (34%, <italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 46) acquired knowledge from other community members, including neighbors and traditional practitioners. This underscores that familial transmission remains the primary avenue for preserving ethnobotanical knowledge, a pattern also documented in Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where intergenerational learning within families ensures continuity of traditional medicinal practices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR65">65</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR161">161</xref>].</p><p id="Par192">Despite these strong cultural mechanisms, the study revealed emerging threats to knowledge retention. These include a declining number of traditional healers, limited written documentation, and the influence of industrialization and modern healthcare systems, which may reduce the transmission of medicinal plant knowledge to younger generations. Similar concerns have been reported in Uganda, Nigeria, and India, where modernization has contributed to the erosion of traditional ethnobotanical expertise [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR95">95</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR163">163</xref>].</p><p id="Par193">Analysis across informant groups revealed significant disparities in medicinal plant knowledge associated with gender, literacy, and experience. Male informants demonstrated higher mean knowledge scores (3.4 ± 1.8) than females (1.9 ± 1.1; t = 5.0, <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> &lt; 0.05). Similarly, illiterate participants reported greater knowledge (4.0 ± 1.9) than literate participants (1.9 ± 1.3; t = 6.4, <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> &lt; 0.05). Key informants, including traditional healers and community elders, exhibited the highest expertise (6.0 ± 1.2) compared to general informants (2.5 ± 1.2; t = 7.8, <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> &lt; 0.05) (Table <xref rid="Tab10" ref-type="table">10</xref>). These results indicate that specialized experience and active engagement in medicinal plant use are critical determinants of knowledge retention, a pattern consistent with observations from China, Pakistan, and East Africa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR140">140</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR170">170</xref>].</p><p id="Par194">Age also emerged as a strong predictor of medicinal plant knowledge. One-way ANOVA revealed significant variation across age categories (F = 67.12, <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> &lt; 0.05), with older informants consistently scoring higher than younger participants (Table <xref rid="Tab11" ref-type="table">11</xref>). Correlation analysis showed a strong positive relationship between age and knowledge (<italic toggle="yes">r</italic> = 0.82, <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> &lt; 0.001), and linear regression indicated that approximately 67% of the variability in knowledge could be explained by age alone (Knowledge = 0.15 × Age − 1.2, R² = 0.67, <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> &lt; 0.001) (Fig. <xref rid="Fig3" ref-type="fig">4</xref>). This reflects the accumulation of experiential knowledge over a lifetime, and mirrors findings from East Africa, India, and Europe, where elders serve as custodians of traditional botanical wisdom, while younger generations increasingly rely on formal healthcare systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR137">137</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR192">192</xref>].</p><p id="Par195">The observed gender and literacy trends suggest that medicinal plant knowledge is deeply embedded in social and cultural contexts. In Sheikh District, men often have greater access to outdoor medicinal resources, whereas women’s knowledge is frequently oriented toward domestic healthcare practices. Illiterate individuals, relying more on experiential learning than formal education, preserve traditional knowledge that may be lost among literate or urbanized populations. Similar patterns have been documented in Tanzania and Nigeria, indicating that ethnomedicinal knowledge is closely tied to lifestyle, occupation, and cultural roles [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR123">123</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">132</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR190">190</xref>].</p><p id="Par196">Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of familial transmission, age, and practical experience in shaping medicinal plant knowledge.
