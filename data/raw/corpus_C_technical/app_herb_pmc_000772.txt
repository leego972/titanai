BY-NC</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec sec-type="other"><title>CURRENT TRENDS IN SYSTEMS PHARMACOLOGY FOR HERBAL MEDICINE RESEARCH</title><p>Medicinal herbs have been used extensively for the treatment of various ailments in ancient medical traditions of China (traditional Chinese medicine, TCM), Korea (traditional Korean medicine), Japan (Kampo medicine), India (Ayurveda), Indonesia (Jamu), North America (phytotherapy), and Europe (herbalism) (<xref rid="ref1" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>). Standardized herbal extracts (hereafter referred to as herbs) or herbal formulae that blend several herbs into a single formula are composed of a variety of bioactive chemical compounds. They provide a fertile ground for modern drug development with therapeutic leads. Antimalarial quinine and artemisinin, antipyretic analgesic aspirin, and arsenic trioxide for leukemia are examples of modern drugs originally used in traditional medicine (<xref rid="ref2" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>). In general, pharmacological effects of herbal medicine are achieved by their active ingredients that simultaneously modulate multiple biomolecules in the human body via an additive or synergistic manner. This multi-component nature of herbal medicine has been considered advantageous over single-target drugs for treating complex multifactorial disorders such as cancer and nervous system disease (<xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>, <xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>). Although therapeutic effects of herbal medicine have been clinically verified in traditional settings for thousands of years, its unknown mode of action on the human body hinders its application and development.</p><p>Along with great progress in systems biology, systems pharmacology or network pharmacology approaches have been introduced to decipher complex mechanisms of action (MOAs) of drugs in networks of biomolecules that interact with the drugs (<xref rid="ref5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref><xref rid="ref6" ref-type="bibr"/>-<xref rid="ref7" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>). These approaches have been extensively applied to explore pharmacological effects of multi-component herbal medicine acting on multiple targets of disease from a holistic perspective (<xref rid="ref8" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>). A typical network pharmacology approach starts from constructing a network in which a node represents either an herb, herbal ingredient, or target protein/gene and an edge indicates link between herb and its constituent compound or interaction between compound and its target (<xref rid="F1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>). By associating targets with biological functions or diseases, an herb-compound-target-function/disease network is constructed and leveraged to study the MOA of an herbal treatment. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network could be further employed to interpret the synergistic effect of herbal medicine by analyzing interactions among target proteins (<xref rid="ref9" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>, <xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>). As this network-based approach is solely based on the network constructed, a significant factor influencing subsequent analysis is the reliability of connections (herb-compound, compound-target, and target-disease) in the network.
