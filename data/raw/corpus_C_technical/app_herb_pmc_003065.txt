Preserving such ethnobotanical wisdom is essential for maintaining cultural heritage and for the discovery of novel pharmacologically active compounds.</p><p id="Par180">Within Karamara, the Somali community identifies <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic> as key medicinal species, each used to treat four distinct ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR85">85</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR86">86</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic> is traditionally employed in Ethiopia for wounds, dandruff, malaria, diabetes, impotence, and colon cleansing [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR87">87</xref>], while <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic> is commonly used for warts and possesses notable antibacterial and antifungal properties [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR88">88</xref>].</p><p id="Par181">The use of these species in Karamara is consistent with ethnobotanical reports across Africa, where <italic toggle="yes">Aloe</italic> species are frequently applied to manage diabetes mellitus, malaria, gastrointestinal disorders, pain, inflammation, and snakebites [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR87">87</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR91">91</xref>]. Their versatility and accessibility make <italic toggle="yes">Aloe</italic> among the most cited plants in African traditional medicine. Beyond Africa, <italic toggle="yes">Aloe</italic> species are widely used in Asia to treat amoebiasis, cough, earache, fever, hernia, malaria, ringworm, stomachache, and swellings [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR90">90</xref>]. European practices similarly employ <italic toggle="yes">Aloe</italic> for wound healing and gastrointestinal problems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>], and in the Americas, indigenous communities have long used <italic toggle="yes">A. vera</italic> for skin treatment and general healing [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR91">91</xref>].</p><p id="Par182">The consistent use of <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic> across diverse ecological and cultural contexts emphasizes their pharmacological potential and ethnobotanical significance. Their widespread application for similar ailments worldwide suggests convergent medicinal knowledge shaped by empirical observation and cultural exchange. Future pharmacological investigations are warranted to validate their traditional uses, identify active compounds, and support their integration into evidence-based healthcare systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR85">85</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR86">86</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec33"><title>Plant parts Used, methods of Preparation, and routes of administration</title><p id="Par183">In the Karamara forest patches, the Somali ethnic community predominantly uses leaves for medicinal preparations, followed by roots and latex. This trend aligns with ethnobotanical studies across Ethiopia and beyond, where leaves are most frequently utilized [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>]. Similar patterns have been reported in Nigeria [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR91">91</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>], India [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR95">95</xref>], Tanzania, and Uganda [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR79">79</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR96">96</xref>], reflecting a global preference for leaves and roots.</p><p id="Par184">Leaves are favored because they are easily accessible, require minimal processing, and are rich in bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and phenolics. Leaf harvesting is also more sustainable than root or bark extraction, allowing plants to regenerate and reducing long-term ecological impact. However, in some Ethiopian and Indian studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR71">71</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR95">95</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR97">97</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR100">100</xref>], roots were more commonly used, highlighting how local flora, disease prevalence, and cultural practices shape plant part selection.</p><p id="Par185">Certain species, such as <italic toggle="yes">C. gigantea</italic>, are valued for multiple parts leaves, roots, and latex which are incorporated into remedies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>].
