# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

While high-FL species are often considered candidates for further scientific investigation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>], this study emphasizes that FL primarily reflects patterns of cultural emphasis and shared practice. Ethiopian ethnobotanical studies have similarly employed FL to explore medicinal plant prioritization while acknowledging its interpretive limitations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>]. When applied cautiously, FL contributes to understanding how traditional communities structure and transmit medicinal plant knowledge across diverse health needs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec38"><title>Preference ranking and direct matrix ranking of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par220">The preference ranking exercise in the Karamara forest patches revealed community perceptions of medicinal plant efficacy for malaria treatment. Among seven commonly cited antimalarial species, <italic toggle="yes">C. kelleriana</italic> was the most preferred, followed by <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">L. inermis</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">E. camaldulensis</italic>. Lower-ranked species included <italic toggle="yes">J. aceroides</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">T. indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">C. adenocaule</italic>. These rankings reflect local knowledge and perceived therapeutic effectiveness, consistent with ethnobotanical principles linking preference to efficacy, accessibility, and familiarity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref>].</p><p id="Par221">The high preference for <italic toggle="yes">C. kelleriana</italic> aligns with studies in Ethiopia and Kenya, where potent antimalarial plants are consistently prioritized due to perceived effectiveness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>]. Aloe species are widely recognized for treating malaria and febrile disorders across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, emphasizing their bioactive compounds and therapeutic reliability [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR90">90</xref>]. Moderate preference for <italic toggle="yes">L. inermis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">E. camaldulensis</italic> suggests that while culturally validated, efficacy or availability may vary, a pattern observed in Nigeria, Sudan, and Pakistan [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>]. Lower preference for <italic toggle="yes">J. aceroides</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">T. indica</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">C. adenocaule</italic> likely reflects limited local knowledge, reduced accessibility, or lower perceived effectiveness, consistent with ethnobotanical findings in India, China, and Europe [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>].</p><p id="Par222">Preference ranking provides critical insights for identifying priority species for pharmacological validation and sustainable use. Globally, highly ranked antimalarial plants often represent the most reliable and culturally significant remedies, guiding conservation and scientific research [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>]. In Karamara, these rankings underline the interplay of cultural knowledge, perceived efficacy, and accessibility in shaping community preferences.</p><p id="Par223">The direct matrix ranking of multipurpose MPs highlights their socio-economic and ethnobotanical significance beyond therapeutic use. <italic toggle="yes">A. nilotica</italic> emerged as the most valuable species, primarily for construction and furniture, followed by <italic toggle="yes">P. guajava</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">C. myrrha</italic>, reflecting broad utility across multiple categories. Similar patterns are reported in Ethiopia and Kenya, where Acacia and <italic toggle="yes">Commiphora</italic> species are valued for timber, fodder, and medicinal purposes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>].</p><p id="Par224">The prominence of fence and construction uses highlights the community’s reliance on woody species for household and communal infrastructure, a trend observed in Sudan, Nigeria, and other African regions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR107">107</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].
