</p></sec><sec id="jdv20452-sec-0010"><title>What does this study add?</title><p>
<list list-type="bullet" id="jdv20452-list-0002"><list-item id="jdv20452-li-0002"><p>Throughout the follow‐up, the percentage of cleared lesions and participants with complete clearance remained higher after PDT with BF‐200 ALA compared to placebo. Skin quality improved throughout the follow‐up.</p></list-item></list>
</p></sec><sec id="jdv20452-sec-0011"><title>What are the implications of this study for disease understanding and/or clinical care?</title><p>
<list list-type="bullet" id="jdv20452-list-0003"><list-item id="jdv20452-li-0003"><p>The promising efficacy data and improvement of all parameters of skin quality during the follow‐up indicate that field‐directed PDT is a long‐lasting treatment for patients suffering from AK.</p></list-item></list>
</p></sec></boxed-text>
</p><sec id="jdv20452-sec-0012"><title>INTRODUCTION</title><p>Actinic keratosis (AK) is a common skin condition resulting from accumulated damage by ultraviolet (UV) light.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0001" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>1</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0002" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>2</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0003" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>3</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>4</sup>
</xref> AK is considered precancerous, as between 0.025% and 16% of individual AK lesions (AKs) transform into cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), although no prediction can be made on which lesions will transform.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>5</sup>
</xref> Although single AKs can show spontaneous regression without treatment, the probability that these lesions recur within 12 months is estimated to be 15%–53% based on a systematic review.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0006" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>6</sup>
</xref> This underlines the chronic nature of the disease and the necessity of long‐lasting treatment.</p><p>Different gradings aim at characterizing AKs to estimate severity. Clinically, Olsen et al.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0007" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>7</sup>
</xref> described the severity of AKs as mild (grade I), moderate (grade II) and severe (grade III) based on palpability and visibility. Histological characterization is based on the extent of atypical keratinocytes, as described by Röwert‐Huber et al. (AK I‐III) or Cockerell (keratinocyte intraepithelial neoplasia (KIN I‐III)).<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>8</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0009" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>9</sup>
</xref> More recent scores like the AK area and severity index (AKASI) aim at considering multiple factors such as lesion number, size of affected area or localization.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0010" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>10</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0011" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>11</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>Importantly, the progression of AK to SCC does not follow consecutive histological staging from mild to full‐thickness atypia. In this regard, Fernández‐Figueras et al.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0010" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>10</sup>
</xref> found that AK I is the most common type associated with invasive SCC. Thus, not only clinically visible AKs, but also the surrounding, clinically inconspicuous skin containing subclinical AK lesions (cancerized field) should be treated.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0011" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>11</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0012" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>12</sup>
</xref> A field‐directed approach might reduce the risk of recurrent AKs resulting from incomplete elimination of neoplastic cells within these cancerized fields.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0013" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>13</sup>
</xref> Consequently, experts advise early and field‐directed treatment, including subclinical manifestations.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0012" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>12</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0013" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>13</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0014" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>14</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0015" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>15</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0016" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>16</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0017" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>17</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0018" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>18</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>Photodynamic therapy (PDT) with aminolaevulinic acid (ALA) is one of the most effective treatment options for AK.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0019" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>19</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0020" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>20</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0021" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>21</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0022" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>22</sup>
</xref> It is a non‐invasive, selective treatment performed over a few hours, entirely controlled by the healthcare professional, if required.<xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0023" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>23</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="jdv20452-bib-0024" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>24</sup>
</xref> Importantly, PDT can be used either lesion‐ or field‐directed and has excellent cosmetic results.</p><p>Previously, we presented data of a pivotal phase III trial on the field‐directed treatment of AKs with PDT using a nanoemulsion formulation of ALA (BF‐200 ALA) and a narrow‐spectrum red light LED lamp (BF‐RhodoLED®).
