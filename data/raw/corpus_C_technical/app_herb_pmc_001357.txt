Emerging research underscores their ability to modulate cellular responses, mitigate oxidative stress, and improve metabolic regulation, supporting their integration into personalized nutrition, preventive healthcare, and drug discovery. However, several critical challenges must be addressed to fully harness their benefits:<list list-type="bullet"><list-item><p>Standardization and bioavailability: The optimization of extraction techniques, formulation strategies, and dosage recommendations is necessary to ensure consistent bioactivity and efficacy [<xref rid="B244-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">244</xref>].</p></list-item><list-item><p>Clinical validation: Rigorous clinical trials are essential to substantiate their therapeutic properties and establish evidence-based applications [<xref rid="B245-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">245</xref>].</p></list-item><list-item><p>Sustainability and conservation: Agricultural and conservation strategies must prioritize biodiversity preservation [<xref rid="B246-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">246</xref>] and the safeguarding of indigenous knowledge while fostering equitable economic opportunities for local communities.</p></list-item></list></p><p>Moving forward, interdisciplinary collaborations among biochemists, nutritionists, pharmacologists, and agronomists will be instrumental in translating ethnobotanical knowledge into scientifically validated applications. The promotion of Andean plants as functional foods and nutraceuticals presents a valuable opportunity to address global nutrition challenges, enhance food security, and advance novel therapeutic strategies.</p><p>The development of new functional foods and nutraceuticals based on ancestral plants represents a significant opportunity for innovation in the food sector. These products not only offer health benefits, but also respond to the growing consumer demand for foods that contribute to overall well-being [<xref rid="B247-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">247</xref>]. By integrating traditional wisdom with modern scientific methodologies, these plants can play a pivotal role in shaping sustainable health solutions for future generations.</p><sec><title>Considerations on Antinutritional Factors</title><p>While quinoa (<italic toggle="yes">Chenopodium quinoa</italic>), amaranth (<italic toggle="yes">Amaranthus</italic> spp.), and lupin (<italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> spp.) are widely recognized for their rich repertoire of bioactive compounds (<xref rid="sec4-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 4</xref>, <xref rid="sec6-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 6</xref>, and <xref rid="sec8-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 8</xref>) and associated health benefits (<xref rid="sec5-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 5</xref>, <xref rid="sec7-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 7</xref>, and <xref rid="sec9-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 9</xref>), these crops also harbor antinutritional factors (ANFs) that can impair nutrient bioavailability and digestive efficiency if not properly mitigated.</p><p>In quinoa, saponins—predominantly located in the seed pericarp—represent the most abundant ANFs, with concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 2.4 g/100 g [<xref rid="B19-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>]. These compounds contribute to the characteristic bitterness of the seeds and can reduce protein digestibility. However, their levels can be substantially lowered through conventional processing techniques such as washing or dehulling, which also preserve their antimicrobial activity (<xref rid="sec5dot1-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 5.1</xref>). In addition, quinoa contains phytic acid (1.05–1.35 g/100 g) and tannins (0.023–0.031 g/100 g), both of which may chelate essential minerals and limit their absorption [<xref rid="B19-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>].</p><p>Amaranth seeds similarly contain phytates (0.5–0.61 g/100 g), tannins (0.043–0.116 g/100 g catechin equivalents), and trypsin inhibitors (0.94–5.46 TIU/mg protein), which are known to interfere with protein digestion and mineral uptake. Nonetheless [<xref rid="B248-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">248</xref>], the levels of tannins in amaranth are moderate relative to other pseudocereals, and targeted processing strategies such as germination or dehulling—discussed in <xref rid="sec6-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 6</xref>—have been shown to markedly reduce these ANFs, thereby enhancing the nutritional profile of the seeds (<xref rid="sec7dot4-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="sec">Section 7.4</xref>).</p><p>In the case of lupin, particularly <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus mutabilis</italic>, quinolizidine alkaloids such as lupanine (0.1–4 g/100 g in bitter varieties) constitute the primary antinutritional concern, given their potential toxicity.
