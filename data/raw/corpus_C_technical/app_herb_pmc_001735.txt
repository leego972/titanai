# PMC: phytochemical bioactive compound anticancer — Overview

[<xref rid="B101-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">101</xref>]. It would be beneficial to find safer and more affordable alternatives. Studies have revealed that the inhibition of <italic toggle="yes">α</italic>-glucosidase enzyme and antioxidant activity could be achieved using natural products such as flavonoids [<xref rid="B102-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">102</xref>].</p><p>Buttner et al. [<xref rid="B19-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>] recently reported the yeast α-glucosidase and porcine pancreatic <italic toggle="yes">α</italic>-amylase inhibitory activities of their ethanolic bark and leaf extracts from <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> harvested in South Africa. The leaf extract showed inhibition against α-glucosidase and α-amylase with IC<sub>50</sub> values of 2.35 µg/mL and 10.45 µg/mL, respectively. The IC<sub>50</sub> of the bark extract against α-glucosidase was 3.64 µg/mL, which was relatively similar to that of the leaf extract. In comparison, the activity of the bark extract against <italic toggle="yes">α</italic>-amylase was weaker than that of the leaves (IC<sub>50</sub> = 17.67 µg/mL). The IC<sub>50</sub> values of all extracts were much lower than the positive control acarbose (IC<sub>50</sub> = 330.71 µg/mL). Only Folin–Ciocâlteu reagent analysis was carried out on these active extracts, and they were shown to have high flavonoid and phenolic contents. As indicated in the earlier sections (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref> and <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref>), various compounds such as quercetin <bold>3</bold>, rutin <bold>6</bold>, miquelianin <bold>7</bold>, isoquercetin <bold>8</bold>, hyperoside <bold>9</bold>, gallic acid <bold>25</bold>, and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold> were identified in the crude leaf extracts of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> harvested in Egypt [<xref rid="B13-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. HPLC-based analysis of the bark extract carried out by Salem et al. [<xref rid="B18-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>] revealed the presence of quercetin <bold>3</bold>, rutin <bold>6</bold>, kaempferol <bold>22</bold>, benzoic acid <bold>24</bold>, gallic acid <bold>25</bold>, vanillin <bold>29</bold>, caffeic acid <bold>35</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">o</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>36</bold>, <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-coumaric acid <bold>37</bold>, ferulic acid <bold>38</bold>, rosmarinic acid <bold>39</bold>, chlorogenic acid <bold>40</bold>, and caffeine <bold>46</bold>. These compounds might also be present in the bark and leaf extracts investigated by Buttner et al. [<xref rid="B19-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>].</p><p>Recent work by our group reported that the methanolic extracts were obtained via a sequential polar-based extract from the flowers, leaves, and bark of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> collected in Australia. The methanolic extracts were active against yeast α-glucosidase compared to their non-polar counterpart. The methanolic bark extract showed superior α-glucosidase inhibitory activity (IC<sub>50</sub> 4.373 µg/mL) compared to acarbose (IC<sub>50</sub> = 254 µg/mL) (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t006" ref-type="table">Table 6</xref>) [<xref rid="B26-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>]. This value was comparable with the inhibitory activity of crude ethanolic bark and leaf extracts of the South African <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> [<xref rid="B19-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>]. </p><p>Although the yeast enzyme was commonly used as an in vitro initial screening of medicinal plants for their α-glucosidase inhibitory activities due to the commercial availability of the enzyme, it is essential to note that an inhibitor, for instance, acarbose, was reported to exert more inhibitory activity against mammalian <italic toggle="yes">α</italic>-glucosidase enzymes than the yeast enzyme. Furthermore, Pacillia et al. [<xref rid="B103-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">103</xref>] reported that naringenin <bold>42</bold> displayed an effective inhibition against the yeast enzyme (IC<sub>50</sub> = 6.51 μM). However, when tested on the rat intestinal glucosidase, it was poorly active (IC<sub>50</sub> = 384 μM). Therefore, the mammalian enzymes from rats or from humans should be considered when discovering candidates for human application.</p><p>The yeast α-glucosidase inhibitory activities of isolated compounds from the three active extracts were determined using the same procedure for the extracts. The IC<sub>50</sub> values of isolated compounds are shown in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t006" ref-type="table">Table 6</xref>. (–)-Epicatechin <bold>51</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 63.58 μM), <italic toggle="yes">D</italic>-(+)-pinitol <bold>48</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 74.69 μM), naringenin <bold>42</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 89.71 μM), isosalipurposide <bold>1</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 116.5 μM), (–)-pinitol <bold>49</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 164.2 ± 8.362 μM), and quercitrin <bold>4</bold> (IC<sub>50</sub> = 177.3 ± 11.34 μM) inhibited the enzymes better than compounds <bold>47</bold>, <bold>50</bold>, <bold>11</bold>, and the positive control acarbose.
