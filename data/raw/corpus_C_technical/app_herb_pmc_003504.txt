In the case of diarrhea, eight key informants were asked to assess six frequently cited medicinal plant species. Each informant assigned a score ranging from 5 (most preferred) to 1 (least preferred) based on their experience and perception of therapeutic efficacy.</p><p id="Par63">The scores from all informants were then summed, and the plant species were ranked according to their total values. This method provided a culturally grounded hierarchy of the plants considered most effective and important for the treatment of diarrhea within the local healthcare system.</p><p id="Par64">Direct matrix ranking was additionally applied to examine the multipurpose roles of selected medicinal plant species and to assess potential exploitation pressure. Seven MPs known to have multiple uses beyond healthcare were selected, and six major use categories were identified for each species. Six key informants assigned use scores from 0 (not used) to 5 (highest importance) for every use category.</p><p id="Par65">Average scores were calculated and summed to generate an overall ranking for each species. This approach allowed comparison of the relative cultural utility and potential overharvesting risks of multipurpose plants, highlighting those species that may require priority attention for sustainable management within the community.</p></sec><sec id="Sec10"><title>Data analysis</title><p id="Par66">A mixed-methods analytical framework combining qualitative and quantitative approaches was employed to provide a comprehensive understanding of medicinal plant knowledge in Sheikh District.</p><p id="Par67">Qualitative data, including local plant names, ailments treated, preparation techniques, and community perceptions, were coded and categorized according to standard ethnomedicinal classifications. Information was cross-validated through repeated interviews and triangulation across multiple informants to enhance reliability. Thematic analysis was used to identify recurring patterns in plant use, cultural interpretations of healing, and mechanisms of indigenous knowledge transmission.</p><p id="Par68">Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics and widely applied ethnobotanical indices, including Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Fidelity Level (FL), Cultural Value Index (CVI), and Informant Consensus Factor (ICF). These indices were used to assess the cultural importance of plant species, agreement among informants, and the perceived therapeutic relevance of specific taxa.</p><p id="Par69">All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.4.3). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in ethnobotanical indices across socio-demographic variables (such as age, gender, and informant category) were examined using ANOVA or appropriate non-parametric alternatives. Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses were further applied to explore relationships between informant age and the number of MPs reported [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>].</p><p id="Par70">Overall, this integrated analytical approach provided a robust assessment of knowledge distribution, cultural consensus, and the significance of MPs within the indigenous healthcare system of Sheikh District.</p></sec><sec id="Sec11"><title>Ethical considerations</title><p id="Par71">The study was conducted in accordance with internationally recognized ethnobotanical ethical standards and received ethical approval from the Department of Biology, Jigjiga University, as well as administrative authorization from the Sheikh District offices. Research procedures followed the ethical principles outlined in the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE).</p><p id="Par72">Prior informed consent was obtained from all participants after clearly explaining the objectives of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the interview procedures, and the measures taken to ensure confidentiality and anonymity. Informants were informed of their right to refuse participation or withdraw at any stage without any consequences.</p><p id="Par73">Special attention was given to culturally sensitive knowledge, particularly regarding plants with sacred, ritual, or restricted uses. No personal identifiers were recorded, and all ethnobotanical information was handled in a manner that ensured participant privacy and community respect.</p><p id="Par74">In addition, formal permission was secured from local elders and municipal authorities prior to fieldwork.
