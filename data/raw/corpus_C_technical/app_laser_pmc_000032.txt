Introduction</title><p>Targeted cancer therapy aims at delivering medicine to a preferential and designated site while reducing systemic dosage [<xref rid="B1-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. Today, the conventional drug distribution system bypasses delivering medicine at effective concentrations selectively to tumors and is associated with many side effects [<xref rid="B2-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. Recently, the use of nanoparticles (NPs) in medicine and particularly drug delivery has received immense interest [<xref rid="B3-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="B4-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="B5-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>,<xref rid="B6-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. Hence, NPs with measured dimension, surface charge, physicochemical properties, and modification of surface polymers allow improved biocompatibility and overall effective targeted delivery [<xref rid="B7-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. Particularly, near-infrared light-responsive NPs are favored because of their biological window (650–900 nm) that is easy to operate and has minimal scattering and absorbance of tissue for deep penetration [<xref rid="B8-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>].</p><p>Photodynamic therapy (PDT) involves the administration of a photosensitizer (PS) with local illumination parallel to the absorption peak of the administered drug [<xref rid="B9-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B10-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. Today, PDT can be targeted very precisely, has no long-term effect, is minimally invasive, and is a clinically accepted method for treating cancer patients [<xref rid="B11-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B13-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. The photochemical reaction is initiated by the absorption of a photon of light by a photosensitizer molecule in its ground state and stimulating it to an excited state [<xref rid="B12-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. Subsequently, the photosensitizer transfers energy to molecular oxygen (<sup>3</sup>O<sub>2</sub>), exciting it to its highly reactive singlet state (<sup>1</sup>O<sub>2</sub>) [<xref rid="B14-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="B15-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>]. These singlet oxygen species produced are highly reactive, extremely toxic, and lead to cell damage [<xref rid="B16-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. PDT is conducted typically in the wavelength range between 600–800 nm [<xref rid="B17-ijms-23-02286" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>].
