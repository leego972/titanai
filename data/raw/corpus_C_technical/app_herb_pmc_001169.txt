It is estimated that around 100–120 million tons of waste are produced annually, containing peel (60–65%), pulp (30–35%), and seeds (0–10%) [<xref rid="B182-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">182</xref>,<xref rid="B183-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">183</xref>]. These residues may also include portions of spoiled fruit [<xref rid="B184-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">184</xref>]. Due to rapid decay and the appearance of flies, mold, and mycotoxins, these residues are perishable, posing serious disposal problems and requiring significant investments for citrus farming [<xref rid="B181-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">181</xref>]. A major issue with this waste is that it cannot be disposed of in the environment without prior treatment due to its pollutant potential, necessitating landfill disposal, which is costly for industry. To mitigate these problems, processing industries often reuse these residues to produce cattle feed in pellet form, although such a product is not profitable [<xref rid="B185-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>]. These residues can still be repurposed in other sectors due to their high levels of soluble sugars, pectin, proteins, hemicelluloses, cellulose fibers, and bioactive compounds, especially flavonoids, and are also a valuable source of essential oil, as shown in <xref rid="molecules-31-00996-t010" ref-type="table">Table 10</xref> [<xref rid="B186-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">186</xref>].</p><p>Due to its diverse chemical composition, this residue still holds potential for use in a range of biological activities, demonstrating that it is a valuable natural resource with added value [<xref rid="B177-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">177</xref>]. One of its major uses is for the production of essential oils, with D-limonene representing about 94% of the composition, which is utilized in various industries, including agri-food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals, among others [<xref rid="B177-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">177</xref>]. Furthermore, citrus residues, as shown in <xref rid="molecules-31-00996-t010" ref-type="table">Table 10</xref>, still contain high amounts of organic and phenolic acids, also including some flavonoids, such as polymethoxylated flavones that are not found in any other fruit species [<xref rid="B177-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">177</xref>,<xref rid="B185-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">185</xref>]. The most studied polymethoxylated flavones are tangeretin and nobiletin, where studies by Lv et al. 2021 [<xref rid="B194-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">194</xref>] found that nobiletin and its derivatives showed anticancer activity. However, such compounds still have a wide range of other biological activities, including anti-obesity, anti-atherosclerotic, antiviral, and antioxidant properties [<xref rid="B195-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">195</xref>].</p><p>A summary of the main applications as a biopesticide, followed by its properties and results, is presented in <xref rid="molecules-31-00996-t011" ref-type="table">Table 11</xref>.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec6-molecules-31-00996"><title>6. Circular Economy and Industry 4.0: Integrating Biopesticide Production into Sustainable Agri-Food Systems</title><p>Previous studies on biopesticides derived from natural sources highlight the significant potential of agro-industrial residues and by-products for bioactive compound extraction and biopesticide production. Contrary to common practice, FAO distinguishes between “waste” (consumer-level) and “loss” (production and processing), aligning with circular economy principles, as well as the SDGs of the UN agenda 2030 [<xref rid="B201-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">201</xref>]. The circular economy model fundamentally shifts from discarding materials and waste to reuse, repair, and recycling, creating a closed-loop production system, avoiding the disposal of consumed goods in landfills [<xref rid="B202-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">202</xref>]. This model aims to minimize the use of new raw materials and the generation of waste and pollution. While some research focuses on using these materials for biofuel production, such as methane or ethanol, according to the “waste hierarchy”, utilizing these materials for energy production is less preferable compared to other strategies [<xref rid="B202-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">202</xref>,<xref rid="B203-molecules-31-00996" ref-type="bibr">203</xref>].
