Complexity is certainly one of the main problems, and it is essential to reduce the number of parameters in huge datasets without worsening the dynamic behavior of the model [<xref rid="B54-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>,<xref rid="B55-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>].</p><p>In classical pharmacology, it has been the primary goal to develop mono-specific or at least highly specific drugs. This concept was difficult to realize since most drugs are more or less promiscuous. Having the complexity of “omics”-based network models in mind, systems biological interventions may take advantage of this fact, and novel drugs could be developed that are capable of targeting multiple selected targets at the same time [<xref rid="B56-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B57-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>,<xref rid="B58-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>] (<xref rid="pharmaceuticals-15-01051-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>). A proof-of-principle of this concept represents several clinically approved multi-kinase inhibitors [<xref rid="B59-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>,<xref rid="B60-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>]. The realization that mono-specificity is more fiction than fact in classical pharmacology also led to the “drug repurposing” concept. Old drugs that are approved for a certain disease indication occasionally reveal activity in other diseases, too [<xref rid="B61-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>,<xref rid="B62-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>,<xref rid="B63-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>,<xref rid="B64-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">64</xref>,<xref rid="B65-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">65</xref>]. Novel network-based methods of studying drug–target interactions and their disease–gene relationships represent attractive tools to systematically screen the human protein–protein interactome to reposition already approved drugs [<xref rid="B66-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">66</xref>,<xref rid="B67-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>].</p><p>Moreover, this concept substantiates the hypothesis that network pharmacology is powerful in explaining the multi-specific activity of many—if not all—pharmacologically active natural products. In this sense, network pharmacology makes, for the first time in pharmacological research, the use of complex herbal mixtures in phytotherapy more rational. We will elaborate on this point in more detail below.</p></sec><sec id="sec2dot8-pharmaceuticals-15-01051"><title>2.8. Network Pharmacology with a Natural Product and Complex Herbal Mixtures</title><p>As outlined above, former drug discovery programs focus on potent drugs that exert (some) specificity to a given therapeutic target. Due to the promiscuity of many drugs to modulate multiple targets and pathways [<xref rid="B56-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B68-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">68</xref>], the term “polypharmacology” has been coined. Bioinformatical tools have the power to design multi-specific drugs [<xref rid="B69-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">69</xref>,<xref rid="B70-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">70</xref>,<xref rid="B71-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">71</xref>]. Thus, drug promiscuity turned from the appearance of unwanted effects to an intended strategy in drug discovery and development. Network pharmacology can now integrate complex “omics”-based datasets from “multi-omics” and single-cell “omics” technologies to form the fundament for novel next-generation drugs. Intriguingly medicinal plants ad phytochemicals play a central role in this emerging new field of drug research.</p><p>Botanicals and phytochemicals are indispensable sources for medications both in traditional medicines and classical drug development [<xref rid="B72-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">72</xref>,<xref rid="B73-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">73</xref>]. Natural products possess tremendous structural diversity and are characterized by their target-related multi-specificity [<xref rid="B74-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">74</xref>]. Thus, they can be understood as examples of natural polypharmacology. The construction of molecular networks for compounds isolated from medicinal plants and complex herbal mixtures has emerged as a thriving new field in network pharmacology [<xref rid="B73-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">73</xref>]. Among numerous approaches accumulated in the literature, we choose two of them to exemplarily illustrate the multiple-site attacks of natural products in cancer therapy and the usefulness of network construction to unravel these bioactivities: (1) Natural products can simultaneously target several pathways in a tumor and (2) they can modulate the immune response in the tumor microenvironment [<xref rid="B75-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">75</xref>,<xref rid="B76-pharmaceuticals-15-01051" ref-type="bibr">76</xref>].
