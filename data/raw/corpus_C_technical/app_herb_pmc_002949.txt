# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

Information regarding useful plants in the Guianas and South America in the seventeenth century is scarce, with Maria Sybilla Merian’s [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>], Willem Piso’s and George Marcgraf’s [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>] works being the oldest records. The German naturalist Marcgraf and the Dutch physician Piso explored northeastern Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>]. Paul Hermann, a German botanist at Leiden University, owned the oldest documented herbarium collection of Surinam and the Guianas region [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>]. The Irishman Hans Sloane cataloged Jamaican plants between 1687 and 1689 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>].</p><p id="Par7">The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were a period of major expeditions worldwide [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>]. Important Spanish expeditions were led by Hipólito Ruiz and Antonio Pavón (1777–1786), José Celestino Mutis (1782–1808), and Martín Sessé (1788–1796), who stimulated the description of regional floras, e.g., Flora Mexicana (1885) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">Plantae Novae Hispaniae</italic> (1889–1893) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">Flora Peruviana et Chilensis</italic> (1798–1802) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>], and Flora de la Real Expedición Botánica del Nuevo Reino de Granada (1828) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>]. In addition, French naturalists reported Latin American natural resources described in expeditions conducted by Charles Plumier (1689–1697) to the West Indies (Martinique and Haiti) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>], by Joseph de Jussieu (1735–1771) to Peru [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>], by Charles-Marie de la Condamine (1735–1744) from the Peruvian Andes to the Atlantic Ocean through the Amazon river [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>], and by Jean-Baptiste C. F. Aublet (1762) in the French Guiana [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>], among others [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>]. Some of the most important naturalists of that time were Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland who explored South and Central America between 1799 and 1804, collecting more than 6000 plant specimens [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>].</p><p id="Par8">Although the findings and observations of all these naturalists had been published at their times, it is very common to note that the ethnobotanical data in their works still did not receive necessary attention. The discussion of this information recorded by naturalists is essential, even more so in the scenario of accelerated deforestation of South American native ecosystems, which contributes to the loss of knowledge about medicinal plants used in traditional medicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>]. In addition to the records obtained from a primary source (either the people who used these plants or those who promoted their use), the information dates back to a time when native vegetation was practically intact and traditional medicine was based on native plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>], in contrast to the current reality marked by intense deforestation and the introduction of several exotic plants in traditional medicines.</p><sec id="Sec2"><title>Neglecting female naturalists in science</title><p id="Par9">A survey among key natural history anthologies for presence of chapters on/by women scientists found a generally absence of women, reporting that natural history compilations shows far more entries from and about men [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>]. Women’s trajectories are usually neglected by the History of Science. In numerous fields, exploring this history brought many women who have been neglected [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>]. It is notable that all tables and citations of travelers and naturalists mostly or exclusively refer to male names [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>]. Looking with more attention, women are cited in footnotes or superficially in texts about scientific expeditions as wives and daughters of male leading naturalists. There are a lot of possible reasons that could prevent women from becoming a naturalist, like being unable to join an academic society at the time or not being encouraged to pursue a research path or not receiving credit for their work [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>]. Schiebinger [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>] also reports that an important obstacle was the fact that women were not hired by commercial companies, scientific academies or governments, which were the main funders of these trips.
