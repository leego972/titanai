# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">5</td><td char="." align="char" colspan="1" rowspan="1">3.79</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1"> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>]</td></tr></tbody></table><table-wrap-foot><p>PS: Number of MPs Reported in Previous Studies; Na: MPs Only in Previous Studies; Nb: MPs Only in current Study; Nc: MPs Common in Both Areas, Nd: Common MPs with Similar Uses, RSI: Rahman’s Similarity Index in %</p></table-wrap-foot></table-wrap></p></sec><sec id="Sec31"><title>Limitations of the study</title><p id="Par230">This study relied primarily on self-reported ethnomedicinal information obtained from local informants. Such data may be influenced by recall bias, personal experience, and culturally specific interpretations of illness and healing practices. In addition, seasonal constraints during fieldwork limited access to certain plant species, which may have resulted in the underrepresentation of taxa available only during particular seasons. To reduce these limitations, repeated field visits were conducted across different periods, multiple data collection approaches (e.g., semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, and group discussions) were triangulated, and information was cross-validated among a diverse group of informants.</p><p id="Par231">Despite these precautions, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The results represent the ethnomedicinal knowledge of communities in Sheikh District and may not capture the full diversity of medicinal plant use across broader ecological or cultural landscapes. As noted in the comparative analysis, ecological variability strongly influences plant availability and use patterns; therefore, direct comparisons with humid or ecologically dissimilar regions should be approached carefully. Furthermore, the pharmacological efficacy and safety of the reported species were not experimentally verified in this study. Similarly, assessments of plant threats and conservation status were based largely on community perceptions rather than long-term ecological monitoring or quantitative population studies.</p><p id="Par232">These limitations are common in ethnobotanical research, where similarity indices and self-reported use reports provide culturally meaningful insights but do not constitute definitive evidence of therapeutic effectiveness or conservation risk.</p><p id="Par233">Nevertheless, the documented information remains valuable. It identifies culturally significant medicinal species, illustrates patterns of knowledge transmission, and establishes a baseline for future pharmacological validation, ecological assessment, and conservation planning. By systematically recording local plant use practices within their ecological context, this study contributes to the preservation of traditional knowledge and provides a foundation for sustainable resource management and community-centered healthcare strategies.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec32"><title>Conclusion and recommendations</title><p id="Par234">This study highlights that Sheikh District harbors rich ethnomedicinal knowledge, with 54 medicinal plant species documented for treating a diverse array of human ailments. These plants serve multidimensional roles, contributing not only to healthcare but also to food security, cultural identity, and local livelihoods. Species with high fidelity levels, such as <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia bussei</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Melia azedarach</italic>, emerged as particularly significant, emphasizing their potential for pharmacological investigation and conservation prioritization. The findings also underscore that indigenous knowledge is primarily transmitted orally, making it vulnerable to modernization, environmental pressures, and the decline of traditional healers.</p><p id="Par235">Preserving this knowledge requires community-based strategies, including cultivating high-demand species to reduce pressure on wild populations, promoting sustainable harvesting practices, and protecting natural habitats to maintain ecological balance. Participatory documentation initiatives that actively engage elders, traditional healers, and youth are also critical for ensuring intergenerational knowledge transfer and cultural continuity. Policy measures are needed to formally recognize the importance of medicinal plants for healthcare and biodiversity conservation. Local and national strategies should encourage sustainable trade, protect indigenous intellectual property rights, and integrate traditional knowledge into public health and environmental programs.</p><p id="Par236">Future research should prioritize pharmacological and phytochemical investigations of high-fidelity species to validate their therapeutic properties and explore novel bioactive compounds. Additionally, social-ecological studies on knowledge erosion, market dynamics, and climate change impacts will strengthen the resilience and sustainability of ethnomedicinal systems in Sheikh District and similar regions.
