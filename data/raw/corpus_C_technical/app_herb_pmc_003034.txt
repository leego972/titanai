# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

Ethnomedicinal knowledge is transmitted orally, primarily through elders, traditional healers, and knowledgeable family members, with apprenticeship and observational learning central to its maintenance. Knowledge transfer typically follows social norms and familial lines, though it is increasingly challenged by formal education, urbanization, and reduced engagement of younger generations with forest resources.</p><p id="Par24">By linking ecological characteristics with sociocultural reliance, the Karamara forest patches represent a critical biocultural landscape. They provide a valuable setting for documenting medicinal plant diversity, understanding traditional healthcare practices, and informing conservation strategies that integrate biodiversity preservation with sustainable community use.</p><p id="Par25">

</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Reconnaissance Survey, study Site, and informant selection</title><p id="Par26">A reconnaissance survey was conducted from 12 to 29 February 2023 to gather baseline information and guide site selection within the Karamara forest patches. Data on the district and forest ecosystem were obtained from the Jigjiga District Office of Agriculture, supplemented by consultations with local elders, kebele leaders, forest guards, and other knowledgeable community members. Jijiga district was purposively selected due to its relatively rich vegetation, high reliance on MPs, and the absence of formal conservation initiatives for these resources.</p><p id="Par27">A total of 64 informants (40 men and 24 women), aged 20–80 years, participated in the study. Of these, 40 general informants were recruited through snowball sampling, targeting long-term residents recognized by their communities for extensive indigenous knowledge of MPs. An additional 24 key informants were purposively selected based on recommendations from community leaders and local experts. Key informants included traditional healers, vendors, elders, and other individuals with specialized knowledge. This approach ensured coverage of both general community knowledge and expert insights.</p><p id="Par28">Informants were categorized into three age groups: young adults (18–30 years, 18.7%), middle-aged adults (31–49 years, 29.7%), and elders (50–80 years, 51.6%), following common ethnobotanical research classifications (Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>). The sample included 62.5% males and 37.5% females, reflecting gender roles in plant collection, preparation, and use. Marital status was 62.5% married, 28.1% single, and 9.4% divorced. Educational backgrounds varied, with 70.3% illiterate and 29.7% literate. Livelihoods were diverse: 38.6% farming, 31.4% livestock keeping, 18.3% merchants, 11.7% charcoal or firewood production, and the remainder engaged in other activities.</p><p id="Par29">While this sampling strategy captured a broad range of knowledge, several limitations must be acknowledged. The total sample size (<italic toggle="yes">N</italic> = 64) is relatively small, limiting the statistical power of demographic comparisons. Gender representation is uneven, reflecting local roles but potentially biasing findings toward male knowledge. Reliance on purposive and snowball sampling may also introduce selection bias, emphasizing individuals known for their expertise while potentially overlooking less prominent knowledge holders. These factors are considered when interpreting the results and framing conclusions.</p><p id="Par30">

</p></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>Ethnobotanical data collection and plant identification</title><p id="Par31">Ethnobotanical data in the Karamara forest patches were collected through semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, direct observations, and focus group discussions (FGDs).
