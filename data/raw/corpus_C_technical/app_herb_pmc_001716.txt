Introduction</title><p>There are about 1380 species of <italic toggle="yes">Acacia</italic> worldwide, and about two-thirds of them are native to Australia [<xref rid="B1-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. Indigenous Australians have used the plants’ leaves, bark, and flowers as medicinal agents for centuries [<xref rid="B2-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. Decoction and infusion have been the most common preparations for ethnomedicinal plants [<xref rid="B3-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="B4-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="B5-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. The Australian continent consists of some arid, semi-arid, and dry subtropical regions, which allow Acacia plants to grow and produce unique secondary metabolites [<xref rid="B6-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. In addition to their survival purpose, the phytochemicals have various benefits for human health, such as anti-digestive disorders (tannins, saponins, flavonoids); anti-plasmodial (tryptamine, tannins, organic acids, saponins); antioxidant (polyphenols); anticancer (triterpenoids, saponins); nutraceutical, diuretic and natriuretic therapies (polysaccharide, glucosides, and gum) [<xref rid="B7-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. The Acacia plants of medicinal importance are <italic toggle="yes">A. kempeana, A. ligulata, A. tetragonophylla, A. mearnsii,</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">A. pycantha</italic>. They have been reported to contain rich contents of polyphenolic compounds in their extracts. Another Acacia of note is <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic>. <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> is commonly found on poor sandy soils and coastal dune systems. The tree is a highly tolerant species against drought, saline and alkaline soils, and frosty environments [<xref rid="B8-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. Due to its ability to stabilise a coastal dune system, this species was cultivated in the Eastern States of Australia and some countries in the Middle East, Africa, and South America. Although it is not known to be used as traditional medicine, <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> has been studied for its in vitro bioactivities and phytochemicals from different plant parts growing in the Middle East, Africa, and South America.
