A study that evaluated the hepatotoxicity of the aqueous and acetone extracts of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum Praeparata</italic> on mice (5, 10, and 20 g/kg/day, p.o., for 28 days) indicated that the hepatotoxicity of the aqueous decoction was much higher than that of the acetone extract. Moreover, the hepatotoxicity of the acetone extract of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic> was considerably higher than that of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum Praeparata</italic> (<xref rid="bib164" ref-type="bibr">Wu et al., 2012</xref>). Another study established the “dose-time-toxicity” relationship of the hepatotoxicity caused by administration of a single dose of the water-extracted and ethanol-extracted components of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic> to mice. The water-extracted components (from 5.5 to 30.75 g/kg) and the ethanol-extracted components (from 8.5 to 24.5 g/kg) caused obvious damage to the liver organization, resulting in significantly increased ALT and AST serum levels, and this effect was dose-dependent (<xref rid="bib54" ref-type="bibr">Huang, et al., 2011</xref>). Other studies have demonstrated that <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum Praeparata</italic> also exerts side effects on the rat liver; however, the toxicity was less than that obtained with <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic> (<xref rid="bib74" ref-type="bibr">Li et al., 2012d</xref>, <xref rid="bib49" ref-type="bibr">Hu et al., 2006a</xref>, <xref rid="bib50" ref-type="bibr">Hu et al., 2006b</xref>, <xref rid="bib47" ref-type="bibr">Hu et al., 2007</xref>).</p><p id="p0265">Emodin and rhein, the major components of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic>, have been demonstrated to exert concentration- and time-dependent toxic effects on human liver (L-02) cells. Emodin at a concentration of 30 μM led to significant apoptosis in a time-dependent manner, as determined by morphological changes in the drug-treated cells (<xref rid="bib63" ref-type="bibr">Li et al., 2012a</xref>). An <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> study suggested the occurrence of time- and dosage-dependent (in the range of 6.25–50 μmol/L) toxic effects of rhein on L-02 cells (<xref rid="bib141" ref-type="bibr">Sun et al., 2010</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="s0145"><label>6.2</label><title>Nephrotoxicity</title><p id="p0270">Emodin at a concentration of 40 μM displayed apoptotic/necrotic effects in a dose- and time-dependent manner on human proximal tubular epithelial (HK-2) cells. Cell cycle analysis revealed that the HK-2 cells were locked in G1 phase, and the observed apoptosis was mediated through the induction of a caspase 3-dependent pathway (<xref rid="bib147" ref-type="bibr">Wang et al., 2007a</xref>). Rhein also induced the apoptosis of HK-2 cells at a dose of 40 μM. However, the toxicity of rhein was weaker than that of emodin (<xref rid="bib30" ref-type="bibr">Da et al., 2009</xref>). Another study demonstrated that emodin, rhein and physcion can significantly inhibit the proliferation of HK-2 cells with IC<sub>50</sub> values of 130.65, 82.97 and 76.02 μmol/L, respectively (<xref rid="bib154" ref-type="bibr">Wang et al., 2007c</xref>). The suppression of the phosphorylation of ERK through the MAPK/ERK signal pathway and alterations in the proportions of lipid moieties may lead to the impairment of the outer mitochondrial membrane in renal tubular epithelial cells. This effect can in turn cause the release of cytochrome c and lead to apoptosis, inducing a decline in the reabsorption of low-molecular-weight compounds in the renal tubule, aminoaciduria and glucosuria (<xref rid="bib153" ref-type="bibr">Wang, 2007b</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="s0150"><label>6.3</label><title>Other toxicities</title><p id="p0275">Emodin induces embryonic toxicity in mouse blastocysts at doses of 25, 50 and 75 μM. The injury in mouse blastocysts occurs via the intrinsic apoptotic signaling processes, impairing subsequent embryonic development (<xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr">Chang et al., 2012</xref>). Moreover, the adverse effects of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic> can be observed not only in the liver and kidney but also in the lung. An ethanol extract of <italic toggle="yes">Polygonum multiflorum</italic> was found to exert toxic effects in the rat lung after repeated intragastric administration for 28 days at a dosage of 40 g/kg (<xref rid="bib71" ref-type="bibr">Li et al., 2013b</xref>).
