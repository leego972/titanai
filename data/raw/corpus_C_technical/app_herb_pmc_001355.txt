Based on these properties of amaranth oil, we hypothesize that it could be of significant benefit for patients with cardiovascular disease [<xref rid="B149-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">149</xref>]. However, the use of amaranth oil instead of rapeseed oil may increase cardiovascular risk in obese and overweight subjects [<xref rid="B223-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">223</xref>]. </p><p>The prevalence of anemia was significantly lower with processed amaranth bread (32%) compared to the maize group (56%). The risk of iron deficiency anemia is significantly lower in the amaranth group, but there was no difference in iron deficiency [<xref rid="B224-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">224</xref>].</p><p>Elevating plasma nitrites through dietary nitrates (NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup>) supplementation is associated with enhanced muscle efficiency, fatigue resistance, and performance. Recent evidence identified an alternative source rich with dietary NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> as a possible nitric oxide precursor, amaranth (<italic toggle="yes">A. hypochondriacus</italic>), with a standardized concentration of 9–11% of NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup>. Long-term (6 days) use of dietary NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> from amaranth may improve the aerobic capacity during ICE in young physically active male persons. It can be recommended as a nutritional supplement during the last week of preparation for competition in endurance events [<xref rid="B225-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">225</xref>]. Also, a single oral dose of amaranth extract increased the NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> and NO<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> levels in the body by at least 8 h. The increase in NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> and NO<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> levels can help to improve the overall performance of people involved in vigorous physical activities or sports [<xref rid="B147-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">147</xref>].</p><p>Supplementation with amaranth oil caused mild pro-oxidant activity, resulting in the improved uptake of oxidative destruction products and modulation of catalase and SOD activity, with the subsequent development of an antioxidant effect [<xref rid="B226-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">226</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec10dot3-nutrients-17-01749"><title>10.3. Lupinus</title><p>This has been the most studied product and with several clinical trials, most of them focusing on metabolic and cardiovascular diseases. Below is a summary of this available evidence.</p><p>Initial evidence, almost 40 years ago, showed that <italic toggle="yes">L. termis</italic> extractive was effective in the treatment of chronic eczema, comparable to those obtained with the corticoid therapy [<xref rid="B227-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">227</xref>]. Then, in the decade of the 1990s, the protein quality of <italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic> cv Multolupa was evaluated in young adult males using the nitrogen balance technique at graded levels of N intake, and revealed that the net protein utilization of lupin was 77% that of egg protein [<xref rid="B228-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">228</xref>]. Similarly, the absorption of Zn from lupin-protein foods was found to be higher than from comparable soya-bean products; therefore, lupin milk could be an attractive alternative to soya-bean milk for infant formulas [<xref rid="B229-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">229</xref>].</p><p>The addition of Australian sweet lupin (<italic toggle="yes">L. angustifolius</italic>) kernel fiber (LKFibre) to the diet provided favorable changes to some serum lipid measures in men, which, combined with its high palatability, suggest that this novel ingredient may be useful in the dietary reduction in coronary heart disease risk [<xref rid="B230-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">230</xref>]. However, the Australian sweet lupin (<italic toggle="yes">L. angustifolius</italic>) flour (ASLF) addition to the breakfast reduced its glycemic index and raised its insulinemic index, but did not affect palatability, satiety, or food intake [<xref rid="B231-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">231</xref>]. Finally, the addition of LKFibre to the diet incorporated into food products stimulated colonic bifidobacteria growth, which suggests that this dietary fiber may be considered a prebiotic and may improve some markers of healthy bowel function and colon cancer risk in men [<xref rid="B232-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">232</xref>].</p><p>Although it is not a therapeutic use, it is important to stress that, in peanut-sensitized patients, clinically relevant sensitization to either lupine, pea, or soy occurs frequently. The ED for lupine is low (0.5 mg), which is only fivefold higher than for peanuts. Also, lupine-enriched pasta can be tolerated by most subjects suffering from peanut allergy, but a sizeable minority (2/12 of them in this case) can develop potentially dangerous clinical reactions.
