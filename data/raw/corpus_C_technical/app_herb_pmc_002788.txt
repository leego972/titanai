In northern Uganda, where more than a third of snakebite victims are younger than 18 years, antivenom supplies are insufficient even at hospitals for optimum treatment of envenomation according to WHO guidelines [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR121">121</xref>]. These interrelated factors underline the importance of locally available envenomation treatments.</p><p id="Par86">Of the four taxa cited for use in treatment of snakebite, <italic toggle="yes">Annona senegalensis</italic> Pers. root for treatment of snakebite was corroborated nine times in the ethnobotanical reports, and has been assessed clinically against venom of puff adder <italic toggle="yes">Bitis arietans</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR122">122</xref>], black-necked spitting cobra <italic toggle="yes">Naja nigricollis</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR123">123</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR124">124</xref>], and West African Carpet viper <italic toggle="yes">Echis ocellatus</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR125">125</xref>].</p><p id="Par87">No clinical antivenom studies were found on <italic toggle="yes">Gardenia ternifolia</italic>, although a 2015 study observed anti-inflammatory cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) inhibitory activity in an unspecified ethanolic extract, noted as a pathway of relevance to mouse hind paw oedema induced by <italic toggle="yes">Bothrops insularis</italic> (Golden Lancehead pit viper) snake venom [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR126">126</xref>]. Two studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR127">127</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR128">128</xref>] have evaluated the in vitro antioxidant activity of <italic toggle="yes">G. ternifolia</italic> leaf extract containing flavonoid aglycones.</p><p id="Par88">Although <italic toggle="yes">Steganotaenia araliacea</italic> root is cited as a snakebite remedy in seven ethnobotanical sources (with another report for its leaves), no clinical studies nor phytochemical data could be found for the species. No clinical evidence of antivenom activity was found on <italic toggle="yes">Trichilia emetica</italic>, but compositional studies indicate the presence of compounds with activity along potential antivenom pathways, including limonoid triterpenoids in root bark with notable anti-inflammatory properties [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR129">129</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR130">130</xref>], noting further that a 2007 study reported a neurotoxin blocking activity of the limonoid triterpenoid toosendanin [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR131">131</xref>]. The flavonoid glycosides found to be present in a <italic toggle="yes">T. emetica</italic> seed extract [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">132</xref>] have been found to inhibit the phospholipase A2 (PLA2)-II toxins associated with some snake venoms [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR133">133</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR134">134</xref>].</p><p id="Par89">Although malaria is evidently a malady of great significance in northern Uganda, and the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Uganda [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR135">135</xref>], it was ranked very low in the data (with just two use reports in Teso, and one in Lango), possibly reflecting the availability of pharmaceutical treatments, which are widely sold across the region by local traders and at weekly rural markets. The vernacular name for <italic toggle="yes">Schkuhria pinnata</italic>, reported as being used to treat malaria, was recorded as ‘<bold><italic toggle="yes">kilorokwin</italic></bold>’ in Ateso—certainly not a word characteristic of that language, but phonetically identical in the local pronunciation to the antimalarial compound chloroquine (chloroquine phosphate)—long the first line of pharmaceutical defense against non-resistant <italic toggle="yes">Plasmodium</italic> infection.</p><p id="Par90">The sole taxon cited by respondents (in both Lango and Acholi) as a treatment for epilepsy, <italic toggle="yes">Hoslundia opposita</italic>, is abundantly evidenced in both the ethnobotanical record and in clinical studies.
