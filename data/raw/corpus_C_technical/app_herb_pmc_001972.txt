Le et al. [<xref rid="B80-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">80</xref>] demonstrated that storage temperature significantly influences the integrity of ethanol extracts from <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> leaves. After 30 d, samples stored at 5 °C retained over 90% of their initial saponin content, whereas those stored at 30 °C and 60 °C exhibited reductions of 29.65 and 57.82%, respectively. These losses were accompanied by proportional declines in antioxidant activity, confirming that elevated storage temperatures accelerate saponin degradation and diminish biological efficacy. Therefore, cold storage is essential to maintain extract quality and efficacy.</p></sec><sec id="sec5dot1dot4-molecules-30-03460"><title>5.1.4. Proposed Biosynthetic Pathway of Triterpenoid Saponins (TSBP) in <italic toggle="yes">Polyscias fruticosa</italic></title><p>Based on the available literature and comparative analyses, a complete triterpenoid saponin biosynthetic pathway (TSBP) is proposed here for the first time in <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> (<xref rid="molecules-30-03460-f003" ref-type="fig">Figure 3</xref>). This reconstruction integrates structural and biochemical data from closely related Araliaceae species, such as <italic toggle="yes">Aralia elata</italic> [<xref rid="B81-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">81</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">Aralia spinosa</italic> [<xref rid="B82-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">82</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">Panax ginseng</italic> [<xref rid="B83-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>].</p><p>In <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>, the TSBP proceeds via the canonical cytosolic mevalonate (MVA) pathway, yielding isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) as key isoprenoid precursors. Their condensation forms farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP), which dimerizes to squalene. Oxidation by squalene epoxidase generates 2,3-oxidosqualene, which is cyclized by β-amyrin synthase into β-amyrin, the oleanane-type triterpenoid core predominant in this species [<xref rid="B84-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">84</xref>]. This reaction is catalyzed by oxidosqualene cyclases (OSCs), which determine the aglycone skeleton; in <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>, β-amyrin predominates, whereas dammarane- and ursane-type skeletons dominate in <italic toggle="yes">Panax</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Centella</italic>, respectively [<xref rid="B84-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">84</xref>].</p><p>Further oxidation at the C-28 position, likely catalyzed by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases from the CYP716 subfamily, converts β-amyrin to oleanolic acid (OA), the principal sapogenin in <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> [<xref rid="B83-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>]. This step mirrors CYP716A12 and CYP716A94 catalysis in <italic toggle="yes">Medicago</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Kalopanax</italic>, and may involve CYP716A52v2-like or CYP716A75-like enzymes [<xref rid="B83-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>].</p><p>Subsequent glycosylation steps are catalyzed by UDP-dependent glycosyltransferases (UGTs), incorporating saccharides such as pentoses (L-arabinopyranosyl, Ara), deoxyhexoses (L-rhamnopyranosyl, Rha), hexoses (D-glucopyranosyl, Glc; D-galactopyranosyl, Gal), and uronic acids (D-glucuronopyranosyl, GluA). Sugar chains are typically attached at the C-3 hydroxyl and C-28 carboxyl groups. UGTs implicated in these reactions may include members of the UGT73, UGT74, and UGT94 subfamilies, as demonstrated in other Araliaceae. Initial glucosylation at C-3 is often followed by sugar branching at O-2, O-3, or O-4 of the glycosidic residues [<xref rid="B83-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>,<xref rid="B84-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">84</xref>].</p><p>The proposed TSBP comprises five sequential stages of glycosylation and methylation, culminating in the structurally diverse array of saponins found in <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>. The first committed intermediate is presumed to be calenduloside E, formed by glycosylation of OA at C-3 with GluA. Although not yet isolated from <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic>, this compound is likely a transient precursor rapidly converted to more complex saponins [<xref rid="B85-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">85</xref>].</p><p>Glycosylation with Glc or Gal yields zingibroside R1 and ladyginoside A through substitutions at O-2 and O-4 of GluA, respectively.
