Home gardens have been studied in several Latin-American countries such as Mexico, Guatemala, Ecuador, Argentina and Brazil [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>]. Some of these studies have explored cultivation of medicinal plants in different locations ranging from urban over suburban to rural home gardens [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>], while others have focused more on identifying types of medicinal plant knowledge shared between rural and urban locations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>]. In general, studies that focus on the floristic composition of home-gardens and other private green spaces often tend to report a relatively low percentage of species used for medicinal purposes compared to species used as food or ornamentals. A study in Nicaragua, for example [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>], resulted in 293-total plant species, primarily used as ornamentals, although some medicinal plants were mentioned as well<italic toggle="yes">.</italic> A plant survey conducted in Bogota, Colombia, showed that a meagre 9% of the total plants registered were used medicinally, but only sparse information was given by the garden owners [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>]. In addition, in a study in the Brazilian city of São Luís (Maranhão State) medicinal plants only represented 7.5% of the total [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. Palheta and colleagues [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>], on the other hand, found 127 medicinal species in the city of Abaetetuba in Brazil used as medicine. These varied findings not only point to a potential socioeconomic gradient in medicinal plant diversity and knowledge as we will describe further, but also indicate that documenting intra- and intercultural knowledge of medical plant diversity is much needed and also necessary to achieve conservation goals [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>]. As such urban home gardens can contribute to both ecological, economic and social sustainability [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>], which is ever so urgent in light of global climate change [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>].</p><p id="Par7">As a middle-income country with an advanced welfare and social security system, Costa Rica stands out from its neighboring countries in Central-America, for its excellent health outcomes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>], its economic and political stability and general social progressiveness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>]. In addition, Costa Rica has a long history of conservation of its exceptional biodiversity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>]. Despite the rich medicinal flora of the country, consisting of over 500 medicinal plant species (belonging to 104 genera)—of which 37% are harvested from their natural habitats and 126 are commercialized species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>]—very little ethnobotanical research has been carried out, and even less so on the use of medicinal plants in urban areas. Ethnobotanical studies in Costa Rica have mainly focused on (i) specific species, growth forms or habitats [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>] or on (ii) ethnobotanical practices within different indigenous communities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>]. However, to date, hardly any research has been conducted on the cultivation of medicinal plants in private urban domestic gardens and the knowledge surrounding their uses, other than the study by Madaleno and colleagues [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>]. In the latter study, the authors compiled an inventory of medicinal plants used in San José based on interviews with urban gardeners, medicinal plant vendors from several local markets and a professional herbalist. Other research has only indirectly touched upon the use of medicinal plants in urban settings. García et al. [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>] conducted interviews with patients at the Hospital Clinic in the capital San José, which revealed no less than 51 different plant species used for medicinal purposes.</p><p id="Par8">The present study aims to close this gap in the existing urban ethnobotanical literature on Costa Rica by documenting the range of medicinal plants used by owners of urban private gardens (home gardens) in three socioeconomically different neighborhoods in Heredia (Central Heredia, Maria Auxiliadora and Bernardo Benavides).
