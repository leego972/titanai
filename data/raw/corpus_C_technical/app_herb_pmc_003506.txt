Such cross-regional similarities suggest both shared therapeutic traditions and convergent adaptation to common health challenges.</p><p id="Par83">Notably, many plant families in Sheikh District were represented by only a single medicinal species. This pattern emphasizes the ecological heterogeneity of the district and demonstrates the detailed local knowledge required to identify and maintain less common taxa for specialized therapeutic purposes. Similar trends have been documented in ethnobotanical studies from Cameroon, South America, and parts of Asia, where culturally important MPs may persist in traditional practice despite limited abundance or restricted distribution [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR73">73</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>].</p><p id="Par84">Thus, the taxonomic composition of MPs recorded in Sheikh District aligns with broader ethnobotanical patterns observed across Africa and beyond. The dominance of key families such as Fabaceae, alongside the inclusion of locally specific taxa, illustrates the adaptive and culturally embedded nature of traditional healthcare systems. These findings also underline the importance of conserving both plant biodiversity and the indigenous knowledge systems that sustain community health, cultural continuity, and future ethnopharmacological potential.</p><p id="Par85">
