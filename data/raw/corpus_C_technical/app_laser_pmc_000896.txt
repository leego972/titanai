AFR, axial fitting range; CPSF, confocal point spread function; N/A, not applicable; NS, not specified; SRF, sensitivity roll-off function.</p><fn id="t002fn1"><label>a</label><p>Measured in the data presented by the authors.</p></fn></table-wrap-foot></table-wrap><p>The attenuation coefficients vary significantly between the different studies reported in <xref rid="t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>. Qualitative identification of vascular tissue has generally followed the classification of Yabushita et al.:<xref rid="r79" ref-type="bibr"><sup>79</sup></xref> fibrous tissue is homogeneous and signal-rich; calcified tissue is signal-poor with well-defined borders; and lipid-rich/necrotic tissue is signal-poor with diffuse borders. This set of criteria implicates low attenuation for both fibrous and calcified tissues, with high backscattering for fibrous tissue and low for calcifications. Based on a tissue optics interpretation of the qualitative classification, lipid-rich/necrotic tissue can be inferred to have strong attenuation. The attenuation (and backscattering, when provided) values reported by Xu et al.,<xref rid="r8" ref-type="bibr"><sup>8</sup></xref> van Soest et al.,<xref rid="r16" ref-type="bibr"><sup>16</sup></xref> and Liu et al.<xref rid="r40" ref-type="bibr"><sup>40</sup></xref> are consistent with this pattern; whereas, the contrast measured by van der Meer et al.<xref rid="r7" ref-type="bibr"><sup>7</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r78" ref-type="bibr"><sup>78</sup></xref> appears to be inverted. A possible explanation of this difference may lie in the selection of fitting regions, which in the case of van der Meer et al. appears to exclude the signal-rich proximal areas in attenuating tissues, causing them to derive data from the slowly varying multiple-scattering background. The <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math265"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi>OCT</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> values for different arterial tissue types do not significantly depend on temperature or tissue fixation, which eases the requirements on <italic toggle="yes">ex vivo</italic> studies.<xref rid="r80" ref-type="bibr"><sup>80</sup></xref></p><p>More recently, Liu et al. implemented a depth-resolved method on intravascular OCT scans acquired <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> on 135 images from coronary arteries on two cadaver hearts.<xref rid="r40" ref-type="bibr"><sup>40</sup></xref> Using a variety of signal descriptors (intensity, attenuation, and backscatter), they were able to distinguish up to six different tissue types (mixed, calcified, fibrous, lipid-rich, macrophages, and necrotic core). <xref rid="t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref> shows that the attenuation coefficient values reported by Liu et al.<xref rid="r40" ref-type="bibr"><sup>40</sup></xref> are much smaller than those reported by other studies.<xref rid="r8" ref-type="bibr"><sup>8</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="r16" ref-type="bibr"><sup>16</sup></xref> Two possible causes of this discrepancy are the blind reconstruction of amplitude (not intensity) data from 8-bit images stored on the acquisition system, which may introduce an unknown scale factor; and the omission of correction factors for CPSF or sensitivity roll-off.
