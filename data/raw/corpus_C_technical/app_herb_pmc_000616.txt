Toxicity</title><p>In 1990, it was reported that <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> has medium to strong allergenic effects and is poisonous to mammals, and atractyloside and carboxyatractyloside are considered to be the major toxic compounds [<xref rid="B137-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">137</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> is prudently ranked into the medium grade with less toxicity in the Shennong Bencao Jing, a monograph of materia medica. Some other Chinese materia medicas aslo record that <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> possessed mild toxicity, such as Bencao Pinhui Jingyao, Bencao Huiyan. Thus, it is obvious that the ancient Chinese people have had a clear understanding of the toxicity of <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> for a long time [<xref rid="B138-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">138</xref>].</p><p>In recent years, many investigations have indicated the toxic effects and related mechanisms of the extracts and monomers of <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> (<xref rid="molecules-24-00359-t004" ref-type="table">Table 4</xref>). In 2005, Li et al. found that the median lethal concentration (LD<sub>50</sub>) value of the WEX in mice was 201.14 g/kg (i.g., crude herbs mass equal) [<xref rid="B139-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">139</xref>]. In addition, a report in 2012 suggested that the LD<sub>50</sub> value of the WEX in mice was 167.60 g/kg (crude herbs mass equal, i.g.), however the LD<sub>50</sub> value was 194.15 g/kg (i.g., crude herb mass equivalent) in Fu’s research report [<xref rid="B140-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">140</xref>,<xref rid="B141-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">141</xref>]. These changes can be attributed to the toxicity of <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> which varied with the processing method, genetic characteristics and growing conditions [<xref rid="B138-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">138</xref>]. Furthermore, the LD<sub>50</sub> value of the EEX in mice was 275.41 g/kg (crude herbs mass equal, i.g.), which was higher than WEX [<xref rid="B140-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">140</xref>]. Another study showed that the carboxyatractyloside (10–100 mg, i.v.) can induce death in swine [<xref rid="B142-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">142</xref>].</p><p>Recently, animal experiments and clinical studies on <italic toggle="yes">X. strumarium</italic> showed that hepatotoxicity is the main toxicity. In 2011, Wang et al. demonstrated that kaurene glycosides including atractylosid (50–200 mg/kg, i.p.) and carbxyatractyloside (50–150 mg/kg, i.p.) induced hepatotoxicity in mice by way of its induction of oxidative stress as lipid peroxidation in liver [<xref rid="B143-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">143</xref>]. Besides, the chief mechanism of atractyloside poisoning is deemed to be inhibition of the mitochondrial ADP transporter [<xref rid="B144-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">144</xref>]. Furthermore, the WFEEX and NFEEX (0.06, 0.3, 0.7 g/kg, i.g., for 28 days), which have marked hepatotoxicity to rats, can cause pathological changes, such as enlarged hepatic cell space, karyolysis, and inflammatory cell infiltration [<xref rid="B145-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">145</xref>]. Moreover, it has been reported that WEX (21.0 g/kg i.g., for 28 days) significantly increased the content of ALT, AST in mice serum and decreased weight loss [<xref rid="B146-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">146</xref>]. In addition, a study in 2014 found that WEX (7.5, 15.0 and 30.0 g/kg, i.g., for 5 days) can increased the serum ALT, AST, ALP, TBIL levels and the contents of LDL/vLDL, β-HB, glutamate, choline, acetate, glucose in male rats [<xref rid="B147-molecules-24-00359" ref-type="bibr">147</xref>].
