The most mentioned medicinal plant families were Asteraceae, Fabaceae, Lamiaceae, Euphorbiaceae, Solanaceae, Apiaceae, Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Poaceae, Amaranthaceae, Boraginaceae, Apocynaceae, Convolvoulaceae, Asclepiadaceae, Capparaceae, Polygonaceae, and Zygophyllaceae. All these families as well as other families mentioned in this review are already represented in Saudi Arabia flora [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>].</p><p id="Par22">Resorting to traditional medicinal plants for treating some illnesses has resulted in many the production of many promising drugs and medications [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>]. The present paper reviews these practices from an ethnopharmacological perspective by targeting 471 medicinal plants used on a regular basis by almost all Saudis (Additional file <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">1</xref>: Table S1).</p><p id="Par23">According to Ali et al. (2017), these therapeutic practices and use of medicinal plants appear on the Use Index (UI), [UI = (na/NA × 100), where na is the number of questioners who refer to the species as valuable and NA is the total number of people met]. Three vital plants were at the core of Saudis’ practices: <italic toggle="yes">Juniperus procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Rumex nervosus</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus spina-chris</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>]. Therefore, these three important and commonly used plants have been discussed here in detail.</p><p id="Par24"><italic toggle="yes">Juniperus procera</italic> Hochst. ex Endl is known as “Arar” in KSA. It is a long tree that reaches up to 8 m tall with needle-shaped leaves, extending from the south reigon of the Arab Peninsula across the Red Sea into Africa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">Juniperus</italic> is one of the major genera of Cupressaceae family consisting of approximately 70 species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>]. Traditionally in Saudi Arabia, <italic toggle="yes">J. procera</italic> used for treating hepatic diseases, jaundice, gastrointestinal disturbances, and pharyngitis, as antirheumatism, for gout and several inflammatory conditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>]. In ethnomedicine, the resinous material of <italic toggle="yes">J. procera</italic> was added to bee-honey and used as a remedy for curing hepatic and skin diseases [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>].</p><p id="Par25">A previous phytochemical study of different parts of <italic toggle="yes">J. procera</italic> resulted in the isolation of different classes of sesquiterpenes and diterpenes, where the stem bark of <italic toggle="yes">J. procera</italic> afforded two lignans identified as β-peltatin and deoxypodophyllotoxin [95]. In addition, the stem barks and leaves of the same plant gave two diterpenes designated as totarol and sugiol with strong antimicrobial activity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>].
