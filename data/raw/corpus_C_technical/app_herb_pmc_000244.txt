It involves the development, handling, and use of materials in the size range between 1 and 100 nm [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>]. Advancements in nanotechnology over the past years have allowed the synthesis of a wide class of materials with many potential applications in the environmental and biomedical fields [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>]. There are two main techniques for the production of nanoparticles, which are the bottom-up (chemical) and top-down approaches (physical) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>]. Additionally, and more recently, the biological method was introduced, which is a bottom-up approach that synthesizes nanoparticles using viruses, algae, bacteria, yeasts, plants, and fungi [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>]. Nanoparticle synthesis via the biological and physicochemical approaches is illustrated in Fig. <xref rid="Fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref>.<fig id="Fig4" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 4</label><caption><p>Different methods for synthesis of nanoscale particles</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d32e1526" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="10856_2023_6764_Fig4_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par38">Plants and their extracts are considered cheap, safe, abundant, and easy-to-use sources for the synthesis of various types of nanoparticles. The green synthesis process utilizes the aqueous or alcoholic extracts of the plant that are rich in various phytochemical compounds (e.g., phenols, amino acids, flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, terpenoids, and tannins). These active compounds act as a reducer, stabilizers, and capping agents in the synthesis and stabilization of nanoparticles from metallic salt solutions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>], Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5</xref>. Moreover, the green process relies mainly on several aspects of the type of plant extract, the method of extraction, the volume ratio of the metallic salt solutions to the extract, and reaction conditions (pH, temperature, and incubation time) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>].<fig id="Fig5" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 5</label><caption><p>Green synthesis of metal ion nanoparticles using a natural plant extract</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d32e1544" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="10856_2023_6764_Fig5_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par39">Ginger (<italic toggle="yes">Zingiber officinale</italic> Roscoe), is a widely used spice that has been used as a remedy for centuries in Ayurvedic, Unani, and Chinese medicines. Ginger is rich in phytochemical compounds particularly gingerols and shogaols phenolic compounds that are well known for their diverse bioactivity against different resistant bacteria [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR115">115</xref>]. Zingiber officinale (ginger) was used for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles. The antimicrobial efficacy and compressive strength of GIC combined with ginger-AgNPs, lyophilized miswak, and chlorhexidine diacetate (CHX) were tested. The combination of GIC with green synthesized AgNPs and chlorhexidine together has shown enhanced antimicrobial efficacy and compressive strength compared to the other combinations alone [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>].</p><p id="Par40">Cypress trees have been widely used in folk medicine due to their reported antibacterial, wound-healing, and anti-inflammatory activities. The green synthesis of AgNPs was performed using <italic toggle="yes">Cupressus macrocarpa</italic> extract and the biosynthesized particles were incorporated with amoxicillin into GIC to synergize its effect against <italic toggle="yes">Streptococcus mutans</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Staphylococcus aureus</italic>. The combination was found effective against both tested strains without impairing the mechanical properties of the cement [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR117">117</xref>].</p><p id="Par41">Cellulose is one of the most abundant biopolymers in the biosphere, which can be obtained from a broad range of plants and animals. Cellulose and its derivatives have been considered as a template for the synthesis of bio-inspired bioactive glass nanoparticles in environmentally friendly conditions. Pure cellulose and amine-grafted cellulose resulted in nanoparticulate composite formation. Methylcellulose has shown to be an excellent candidate for the synthesis of bioactive glass nanoparticles (55 nm).
