Despite its continued importance, much of this knowledge remains undocumented and is rapidly eroding as elder healers pass away without fully transmitting their expertise to younger generations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>].</p><p id="Par8">The Horn of Africa is considered a global biodiversity hotspot, yet Somaliland’s flora remains relatively understudied despite its richness and ecological importance [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>]. In rural and pastoralist contexts, MPs are vital for treating gastrointestinal, respiratory, dermatological, febrile, and other common ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>]. Sheikh District, located in the Sahil Region, is characterized by montane and semi-arid landscapes supporting diverse woody and herbaceous vegetation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>]. Although the district holds significant ecological and cultural value, no comprehensive ethnobotanical investigation has yet been conducted there. As a result, there is limited understanding of the diversity of medicinal taxa, local preparation methods, dosage systems, cultural perceptions of healing, and the conservation challenges facing these plant resources.</p><p id="Par9">Documenting medicinal plant knowledge in Sheikh District is also relevant beyond the local context. Comparative ethnobotanical studies across regions contribute to identifying shared traditions, unique cultural adaptations, and potentially novel medicinal reports. Such research aligns closely with global sustainability priorities, including the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, by supporting culturally appropriate healthcare, strengthening biodiversity conservation, and promoting sustainable resource management.</p><p id="Par10">Against this backdrop, the present study investigates the ethnobotanical knowledge of MPs used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland. The study is designed not only as a descriptive inventory but also as a contribution to broader cross-regional ethnomedicinal research in the Horn of Africa and comparable dryland socio-ecological systems.</p><p id="Par11">Specifically, the objectives are to: (i) identify and document the medicinal plant species used for human healthcare in Sheikh District, including local names, plant parts utilized, preparation techniques, and modes of administration; (ii) assess cultural importance and agreement in plant use through quantitative ethnobotanical indices, such as Jaccard Similarity Index, Rahman’s Similarity Index, Fidelity Level and Informant Consensus Factor, in comparison with patterns reported in other Somali and East African ethnobotanical studies; (iii) examine the distribution of ethnobotanical knowledge across socio-demographic groups (age, gender, literacy, and healer experience), evaluating whether knowledge patterns in Sheikh District reflect broader trends observed in other pastoral and indigenous societies; (iv) identify novel or locally unique medicinal reports, as well as commonalities with previously documented ethnomedicinal practices in Somaliland and surrounding macro-regions, thereby contributing to cross-cultural understanding of traditional healthcare systems and (v) evaluate perceived threats and conservation concerns, particularly for multipurpose species under high anthropogenic pressure, and discuss implications for sustainable management and community resilience.</p><p id="Par12">The study is guided by the following hypotheses (i) ethnomedicinal knowledge is unevenly distributed within the community, with older, male, and less formally educated informants possessing greater expertise, consistent with patterns reported in other pastoral societies; (ii) certain medicinal plant species will exhibit high Fidelity Level and Informant Consensus Factor values, indicating strong cultural importance and perceived therapeutic effectiveness; (iii) multipurpose plant species used for both medicinal and non-medicinal purposes will face greater harvesting pressure and higher risk of local depletion compared to species used exclusively for healthcare; (iv) indigenous medicinal knowledge in Sheikh District is primarily transmitted orally through family and community networks, but this transmission is declining among younger generations due to modernization and limited formal documentation, and (v) MPs contribute not only to disease treatment but also to food security, environmental sustainability, and cultural continuity, reinforcing their multidimensional role in strengthening local resilience in semi-arid ecosystems.</p></sec><sec id="Sec2"><title>Methods and materials</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Description of the study area</title><p id="Par13">The present ethnobotanical study was carried out in Sheikh District, located in the Sahil Region of Somaliland, between 9°55′45″ N latitude and 45°11′30″ E longitude.
