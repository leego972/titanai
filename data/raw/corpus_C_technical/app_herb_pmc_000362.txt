ERS and CRP are inflammatory markers that are elevated in ARS, but are not routinely used in daily routine because of limited specificity.</p><p id="Par29">ARS is on one hand underdiagnosed and on the other overtreated, thus missing out on opportunities to treat patients without the use of antibiotics [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>]. Thus, the correct diagnosis of patients with ARS is vital for their proper treatment. In the majority of cases diagnosis is clinical, practically based on history and physical examination with a variety of associated symptoms and signs such as sneezing, malaise, fever, cough, nasal discharge, nasal obstruction, sore throat and headache [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref>]. However, many of them are non-specific and are also present in other infections or inflammations as well as in allergy exacerbations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>].</p><p id="Par30">The three main symptoms and signs that otolaryngology, rhinology and infection disease specialists agree are required to diagnose ARS include: 1) Up to 4 weeks of purulent nasal discharge, accompanied by 2) nasal obstruction, and facial pain/pressure/fullness, and 3) hyposmia / anosmia or both [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>]. Although there are no studies with a high level of documentation for these symptoms, they have nevertheless been proposed over many years by groups of experts. Therefore, eveh though nasal endoscopy is not necessary for the diagnosis, but anterior rhinoscopy is indicated to assess paranasal sinus drainage and other findings, such as mucosal inflammation and edema.</p><p id="Par31">It is important to underline at this point that nasal obstruction alone without purulent nasal discharge is not sufficient enough for the diagnosis, whereas only facial pain or pressure without purulent nasal discharge does not establish a diagnosis of ARS [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. The typical symptoms of allergic rhinitis, such as itching and watery eyes, should also be sought to differentiate ARS from an allergy or other headache disorders that may cause facial pressure and pain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Diagnosis of ARS by the ENT Specialist</title><p id="Par32">Uncomplicated cases of ARS are more likely to be seen in primary care settings as in GP offices. In some health systems however, as for instance in the Greek National Health System, with rather easy access to secondary and tertiary health structures and a largely developed private sector, patients with ARS can be examined by specialists. In general, the diagnosis can be made following the aforementioned criteria, but sometimes more detailed diagnostic tests can be applied, such as endoscopy and imaging, whereas immediate referral and/or hospitalization are mainly indicated for specific symptoms and deterioration of disease.</p><p id="Par33">In research protocols, more data may be required for setting the diagnosis. Usually, a combination of symptoms is necessary, added to endoscopy, imaging, and culture from the middle meatus [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref>]. Ideally, the diagnostic criteria used throughout studies should be the same so that the results can be compared, but this is practically impossible.</p><p id="Par34">In some settings such as ICUs, ABRS is common (risk factors include nasogastric tube, mechanical ventilation, supine position, and immunosuppression), and is often associated with -poor outcomes and in some cases, even sepsis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>]. Consequently, in such cases, more aggressive diagnostic methods are required, since imaging can be useful for the diagnosis and sinus puncture by an experienced physician can provide important information regarding pathogens and options of treatment [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>].</p><p id="Par35">Another special category of patients are immunocompromised patients since they are much more vulnerable to both ABRS and its complications and likewise, a more aggressive diagnostic approach is needed [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>]. Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis is a very serious disease with high morbidity and mortality requiring prompt diagnosis and treatment with open or endoscopic sinus surgery [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>]. Diagnosis is usually histopathological, so early endoscopic evaluation is indicated followed by biopsy in cases where disease is suspected [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>Pathophysiology</title><p id="Par36">ARS includes acute viral, post-viral and ABRS [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>].
