2015</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1" sec-type="introduction"><title>Background</title><p>Mali is a landlocked country in West Africa with a population of approximately 16 million [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Mali has both the second highest birth rate (46 births per 1000 inhabitants) and infant mortality rate (1 death per 9 live births) in the world. Maternal mortality rate is also high with 54 deaths per 10,000 live births. Total fertility rate is 6.25 children born per woman. Physician density is only one per 12,500 inhabitants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>], while there is approximately one traditional medicine practitioner (TP) for every 500 inhabitants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>]. Approximately 75 % of the population is dependent on traditional medicine for primary health care [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>]. Medicinal plants are an important part of traditional medicine. The plants are most commonly harvested in the wild and to a smaller extent cultivated by TPs and herbalists. The medicinal plants in the areas investigated, grow on Savanna plains or grasslands between equatorial forests and tropical deserts [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>].</p><p>Since 1968 medicinal plants in Mali have been studied with one of the primary objectives to establish a mechanism to assure that ethnomedicine is complementary to conventional medicine, assuming that medicines can be produced from local resources, especially from medicinal plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>]. The Department of Traditional Medicine (DMT) under The National Institute of Research in Public Health, is responsible for this research in Mali, and is a reference center for the West African Health Organization (WAHO) for research on traditional medicine. The WHO has developed a strategy to promote the rational use of traditional medicine with regard to the safety, efficacy, quality and accessibility [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>]. The strategy includes building the knowledge base of traditional medicine, to which this ethnobotanical study is intended to contribute. The TPs are regarded to play a key role in the health care system in Mali, and until now the ethnobotanical research on the traditional use of plants for improving healthcare has been based on knowledge held by the TPs. A recently published study shows that TPs in Mali have a broad experience and knowledge about herbal treatment of pregnant women [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. However, it is not known to what extent women are seeking advice from the TPs on the use of medicinal plants during pregnancy. This is the first study to investigate the knowledge base held by Malian women.</p><p>Data on medicinal plants for women’s reproductive health is in general limited and a traditionally ignored subject [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>].
