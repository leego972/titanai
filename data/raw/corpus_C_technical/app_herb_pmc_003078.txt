Incorporating ethnomedicinal knowledge into primary healthcare programs can increase access to culturally appropriate treatments, reduce reliance on limited pharmaceutical resources, and strengthen community resilience. Simultaneously, recognizing the conservation value of MPs can guide sustainable harvesting practices, habitat protection, and restoration efforts, particularly for species under high extraction pressure. Policymakers should also promote initiatives that encourage intergenerational knowledge transfer and community-based management, ensuring that cultural heritage and ecosystem services are both maintained. By integrating healthcare, nutrition, and biodiversity strategies, these approaches can support sustainable development in semi-arid forest landscapes while safeguarding the livelihoods, health, and cultural identity of the Somali communities.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec43"><title>Limitations of the study</title><p id="Par248">While this study provides important insights into the ethnobotanical knowledge of the Somali community in the Karamara forest patches, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. The sample size was relatively small, and gender representation was uneven, reflecting local sociocultural roles but potentially limiting the generalizability of the results to the wider community. Participation was also influenced by ethical requirements and informed consent procedures, which may have constrained informants’ willingness to disclose certain types of knowledge, particularly information considered sensitive, specialized, or culturally restricted.</p><p id="Par249">The study relied primarily on self-reported data obtained through interviews and discussions, which are inherently subject to recall bias, selective memory, and personal interpretation. Seasonal variation in plant availability and use was not fully captured due to time constraints, potentially leading to the omission or underrepresentation of seasonally important or ephemeral species. The researcher’s outsider status and the need for translation may have introduced cultural and linguistic biases, affecting the accurate recording and interpretation of local plant names, preparation methods, and illness concepts. Additionally, the exclusively oral mode of knowledge transmission within the community posed challenges for precise documentation and cross-verification.</p><p id="Par250">Methodologically, the quantitative ethnobotanical indices applied in this study, including Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) and Fidelity Level (FL), have well-recognized conceptual and analytical limitations. These indices do not measure pharmacological efficacy and may be influenced by sample size, informant selection, and patterns of cultural knowledge transmission rather than therapeutic effectiveness. Consequently, the values reported in this study should be interpreted cautiously as indicators of shared cultural agreement and knowledge emphasis, rather than as evidence of clinical validity.</p><p id="Par251">Furthermore, this study focused on documenting traditional medicinal plant uses and did not include laboratory-based validation of efficacy, safety, dosage, or potential toxicity. As a result, the applicability of the findings beyond the local cultural context remains limited. Despite these constraints, efforts were made to mitigate methodological challenges by encouraging broad participation, triangulating information across multiple informants, using local translators, and conducting guided field walks to enhance accuracy.</p><p id="Par252">Recognizing these limitations is essential for contextualizing the results and avoiding overinterpretation. The findings highlight the need for future research that integrates ethnobotanical documentation with phytochemical, pharmacological, and toxicological analyses, as well as longitudinal and seasonally comprehensive studies, to strengthen understanding of medicinal plant use and support evidence-based conservation and healthcare initiatives.</p></sec><sec id="Sec44"><title>Conclusion and recommendations</title><p id="Par253">This study highlights the rich ethnobotanical knowledge of the Somali community in the Karamara forest patches, revealing a deeply embedded system of medicinal plant use that supports holistic healthcare and contributes to biodiversity conservation. Traditional healers and elders serve as the main custodians of this knowledge, which is predominantly transmitted orally within families, reflecting a strong cultural framework for health management and ecological stewardship.
