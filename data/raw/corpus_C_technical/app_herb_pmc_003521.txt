# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">Yes</td></tr><tr><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1"> <italic toggle="yes">Ziziphus hamur</italic> </td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">Root</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">Kidney infection</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">Yes</td></tr></tbody></table><table-wrap-foot><p>Yes = commonly sold in markets, Partial = occasionally sold / limited demand</p></table-wrap-foot></table-wrap> </p></sec><sec id="Sec20"><title>Novel ethnobotanical findings</title><p id="Par143">The present study in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland, documents a range of ethnomedicinal practices that represent either novel reports for the country or rarely recorded uses within East Africa. While many of the plant species identified are already recognized in African traditional medicine, the specific combinations of plant parts, preparation methods, routes of administration, and targeted ailments observed here provide meaningful additions to the regional ethnobotanical record. These findings reflect a locally refined therapeutic knowledge that illustrates the interplay between ecological adaptation and long-standing indigenous expertise [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR185">185</xref>].</p><p id="Par144">Several medicinal applications documented in this study constitute new reports for Somaliland. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Albizia anthelmintica</italic> was used to treat gonorrhea through dermal washing with preparations derived from roots or root bark (Table <xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>). Although the species is widely acknowledged for its anthelmintic properties in other African contexts, its topical application for sexually transmitted infections has not previously been reported in Somali ethnomedicine. Similarly, the oral administration of latex from <italic toggle="yes">Aloe microdonta</italic> for typhoid and jaundice represents a novel medicinal use in Somaliland, since Aloe species are more commonly associated with dermatological or gastrointestinal treatments rather than systemic infectious or hepatic conditions. Additionally, macerated roots of <italic toggle="yes">Rotheca myricoides</italic> were used for placenta removal, highlighting a rare national-level application and emphasizing the critical role of traditional MPs in obstetric and maternal healthcare.</p><p id="Par145">Specialized ocular treatments, rarely documented in Somali ethnobotanical literature, were also observed. Crushed or squeezed leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Aloe citrina</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Olea europaea</italic> subsp. <italic toggle="yes">africana</italic>, along with diluted latex of <italic toggle="yes">Terminalia prunioides</italic>, were applied directly to the eyes to manage conditions such as trachoma, conjunctivitis, and other ocular infections. These practices demonstrate a nuanced indigenous understanding of safe dosage and plant selection for sensitive organs, echoing similar ocular remedies reported in Pakistan and India, where traditional healers carefully administer plant extracts for eye ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR159">159</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR175">175</xref>].</p><p id="Par146">Several practices documented in Sheikh District also constitute rare or poorly recorded uses within East Africa. Oral administration of <italic toggle="yes">Hydnora hanningtonii</italic> roots for systemic infections demonstrates the ethnomedicinal importance of this parasitic species, which remains scarcely documented regionally. Smoke-based therapies were another distinctive feature: stems of <italic toggle="yes">Cordia sinensis</italic> were burned to alleviate bone and body pain, while smoke from roots of <italic toggle="yes">Tragus racemosus</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Zygophyllum hildebrandtii</italic> was applied dermally to manage culturally defined conditions, such as Xulbad disease in children. These non-ingestive approaches reflect a sophisticated understanding of disease management, underrepresented in East African ethnobotanical literature [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>].</p><p id="Par147">Latex-based remedies were widely utilized and demonstrated remarkable therapeutic diversity. For example, latex from <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia seyal</italic> was applied for diarrhea, <italic toggle="yes">Senegalia senegal</italic> for abdominal pain, <italic toggle="yes">Calotropis procera</italic> for eczema, and <italic toggle="yes">Terminalia prunioides</italic> for conjunctivitis. While latex use is well established across African traditional medicine, the breadth of conditions treated in Sheikh District illustrates a refined local knowledge of plant chemistry and pharmacodynamics, consistent with ethnobotanical practices observed in Africa and Asia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR157">157</xref>].</p><p id="Par148">Several species exhibited notable therapeutic versatility.
