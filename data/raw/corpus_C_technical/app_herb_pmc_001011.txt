GC-MS provides detailed resolution of volatile and semi-volatile compounds<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref></sup>, while FT-IR enables rapid identification of functional groups characteristic of bioactive phytochemical classes such as phenolics, alkaloids, and glycosides<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref></sup>. Used in combination, these techniques offer a comprehensive approach to plant compound characterization.</p><p id="Par10">Although previous studies have used GC-MS to investigate <italic toggle="yes">P. americana</italic> leaf extracts<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref></sup>, few have examined the comparative influence of solvent polarity on extractable constituents. Moreover, no published work has systematically compared ethanolic and ethyl acetate extracts of <italic toggle="yes">P. americana</italic> leaves using an integrated GC-MS and FT-IR approach. These two solvents differ markedly in polarity, which directly impacts the type and quantity of phytochemicals recovered. Furthermore, the phytochemical profile of <italic toggle="yes">P.</italic>
<italic toggle="yes">americana</italic> leaves grown in Uganda, a country with diverse agroecological zones, remains underexplored and may yield distinct chemotypic signatures.</p><p id="Par11">The aim of this study is to characterize and compare the phytochemical content of ethanolic and ethyl acetate extracts of avocado (<italic toggle="yes">P. americana</italic>) leaves collected from Uganda using both FT-IR and GC-MS. By linking functional groups and compound identities to known pharmacological effects, this work seeks to assess how solvent polarity influences phytochemical extraction, identify compounds of potential therapeutic relevance, and provide foundational data to guide future bioactivity studies, including antidiabetic and anti-inflammatory screening.</p><p id="Par12">This research addresses a current gap in phytochemical profiling literature and emphasizes the value of underutilized plant parts as a sustainable resource for natural product drug discovery.</p></sec><sec id="Sec2"><title>Materials and methodology</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Collection, identification, and preparation of plant specimens</title><p id="Par13">Fresh leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Persea americana</italic> (avocado) were collected from the botanical premises of Kampala International University (KIU), Ishaka, Uganda. The plant was taxonomically identified and authenticated by Dr. Eunice of the Biology Department, Mbarara University of Science and Technology, and a voucher specimen (ID: AOM-2024-001) was deposited for reference.</p><p id="Par14">The leaves were rinsed with distilled water to remove surface dust and contaminants, and then shade-dried in a well-ventilated space at ambient temperature for 14 days to preserve heat-sensitive phytochemicals and minimize microbial contamination. The dried material was pulverized using a high-speed electric blender and sieved to a fine powder for uniform extraction efficiency.</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Extraction methods (cold maceration)</title><p id="Par15">Two solvents of varying polarity, ethanol (polar) and ethyl acetate (semi-polar), were used to extract phytoconstituents to evaluate solvent-specific extraction efficiency.</p><sec id="Sec5"><title>Sample preparation</title><p id="Par16">The finely blended powdered avocado leaves were weighed using an analytical balance, and the reading was recorded at equilibrium.</p><p id="Par17">Exactly 600 g of powdered avocado leaves was separately soaked in 3000 mL of ethanol or ethyl acetate (1:5 w/v) in sterile glass containers. The mixtures were kept at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 72 h with occasional shaking/stirring every 6–12 h to facilitate solute diffusion.</p></sec><sec id="Sec6"><title>Filtration and concentration</title><p id="Par18">After maceration (72 h), the extracts were filtered through double-layered muslin cloth followed by Whatman No. 1 filter paper to remove residual plant debris.</p><p id="Par19">The combined filtrates (ethanol and ethyl acetate) were concentrated at lower pressure using a rotary evaporator at temperatures below the solvent boiling points (ethanol: 40 °C; ethyl acetate: 35 °C) to prevent thermal degradation. Final drying was performed using a vacuum oven at 40 °C. Extract yields were calculated as; 49.72 g (8.29% yield) for Ethanolic extract and 32.38 g (5.40% yield) for Ethyl acetate extract.</p><p id="Par20">The concentrated crude extracts were stored in sealed amber bottles at 4 °C for further analysis.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec7"><title>Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis</title><sec id="Sec8"><title>GC-MS principle</title><p id="Par21">The model of the instrument used for the analysis is Agilent technologies 7890 GC system and the model of the detector is Agilent technologies 5975 MSD (Mass Spect.
