For instance, the first studies on the stimulating and tonic effects of <italic toggle="yes">Schisandra</italic> <italic toggle="yes">chinensis</italic> were published in Soviet Union WWII military journals [<xref rid="B5-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>,<xref rid="B6-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. The Russian interest in <italic toggle="yes">S.</italic>
<italic toggle="yes">chinensis</italic> arises from ethnopharmacological investigations by Komarov (1895) and Arsenyev (1903–1907) in the Far East regions. It was discovered that the berries and seeds were used by Nanai (Goldes or Samagir) hunters as a tonic, to reduce thirst, hunger and exhaustion, and to improve night vision [<xref rid="B7-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B10-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. During the period 1950–60, the idea of using herbal medicinal plants to increase stamina and survival in harmful environment was developed, and a new concept of “adaptogens” was introduced by the toxicologist Lazarev to describe compounds which could increase “the state of non-specific resistance” in stress [<xref rid="B11-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. This concept was based on Hans Selye’s theory of stress and general adaptation syndrome, which have three phases: alarm phase, phase of resistance and phase of exhaustion [<xref rid="B13-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>], <xref ref-type="fig" rid="pharmaceuticals-03-00188-f001">Figure 1</xref>.</p><fig id="pharmaceuticals-03-00188-f001" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 1</label><caption><p>Adaptogens increase the state of non-specific resistance in stress and decrease sensitivity to stressors, which results in <underline>stress protection</underline>, and prolong the phase of resistance (<underline>stimulatory effect</underline>). Instead of exhaustion, a higher level of equilibrium (the homeostasis) is attained the heterostasis. The higher it is, the better the adaptation to stress. Thus, the stimulating and anti-fatigue effect of adaptogens has been documented in both in animals and in humans. In the early 1960’s, the study of adaptogens developed into a field of biomedicinal research in its own right in the USSR. This was due to a major targeted project or direction of research, such as mapping or screening of biologically active substances from the plant kingdom. The aim of the stress research was to develop drugs and methods able to stimulate the intrinsic adaptive mechanisms of the organism to help it survive in situations of intense or prolonged stress, whilst preferably maintaining the capability for physical and mental work [<xref rid="B15-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>]. The extent of the research carried out was enormous with 1009 studies (primarily pharmacological and clinical) published in Russia up until 1982 and most of them concerned extracts or isolates prepared from <italic toggle="yes">Eleutherococcus</italic>
<italic toggle="yes">senticosus</italic> [<xref rid="B16-pharmaceuticals-03-00188" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>].</p><p>Today, research into adaptogens comprises the following four areas: (a) phytochemistry: isolation and structure elucidation of active constituents of adaptogenic plants; (b) biochemistry and molecular biology: mechanisms of stress protective activity of adaptogens on the molecular and cellular levels; (c) experimental and clinical pharmacology: efficacy and safety of adaptogens in stress related disorders on animals and humans; (d) pharmaceutical development of herbal preparations/products that have well established medicinal use in evidence based medicine.
