Only <italic toggle="yes">A. vasica</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic> showed inhibitory activity against AChE with IC<sub>50</sub> value 294 and 68 μg/ml, respectively. Both plants contain β-carboline alkaloids, which demonstrated potent activity against AChE [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">24</xref>]. Extracts from natural resources usually containing un-determined number of secondary metabolites and expected to play different role upon their interaction with human biological system. In this study the major biological activity demonstrated by both extracts could be attributed to the dominant major constituents in each extracts which should be able to go through blood brain barrier and interact with the active sites. The major constituent of <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic> is harmaline and <italic toggle="yes">A. vescia</italic> contains vasicine (from 0.0541 to 1.105%). Generally β-carbolines are a large group of natural indole alkaloids that are widely distributed in nature. They possess diverse pharmacological activities such as sedative, hypnotic, anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, antitumor, antithrombotic, antiparasitic, antimicrobial, as well as antiviral activities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">24</xref>]. Harmaline, the major active constituent of <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic> is a common dihydro β-carboline type, it possess interesting pharmacological activities and can interact with several enzymes and neurotransmittors including topoisomerase I, and monoamine oxidase-A [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B25">25</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B26">26</xref>]. The different activities demonstrated by both major compounds could explain the difference in relative potential IC<sub>50</sub> values of both plants. Although, <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic> has been used in traditional medicine, there are reports of severe intoxication in cattle, donkeys, sheep and horses [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B27">27</xref>]. Digestive and nervous syndromes have been reported in animals that consume a sub-lethal amount of the plant. Harmaline and harmine are toxic alkaloids characterized in the seeds of <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic>. Harmaline is almost twice as toxic as harmine and in moderate doses cause tremors and clonic convulsions, but with no increase in spinal reflex excitability [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B28">28</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">A vasica</italic> showed safety when intragastrically administrated at 2.5 g/kg, clinical trials performed on combination preparations containing <italic toggle="yes">A</italic>. v<italic toggle="yes">asica</italic> showed no serious adverse effects [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B29">29</xref>].</p><p>Although IC<sub>50</sub> value of <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic> is about four times higher than that of <italic toggle="yes">A. vasica</italic>, the inhibition type study showed that <italic toggle="yes">A. vasica</italic> reversibly inhibits AChE and can be used for AD’s medication rather than <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic> which inhibits irreversibly AChE. This recommendation was supported by the toxicity reports in literature which indicated the higher safety margin of <italic toggle="yes">A. vasica</italic> as compared to <italic toggle="yes">P. harmala</italic>.</p><p>According to the best of our knowledge this is the first report about the reversible anti-chloinsterase interaction of <italic toggle="yes">A vasica</italic> extracts growing in Egypt, which add new value and activity to this important plant.</p><p>Anti-inflammatory COX-inhibiting NSAIDs have received increased attention in experimental and therapeutic trials for Alzheimer’s disease. Interestingly, COX-1-expressing microglia surrounds amyloid plaques. There is no evidence that COX-1 expression in microglia is changed in AD brain. However, accumulation of COX-1-expressing microglia in AD could result in local increase in prostaglandin synthesis and oxidative stress. <italic toggle="yes">F. assafeotida</italic> demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against COX-1 enzyme. Asafoetida used in traditional medicine to improve memory and as an antihelminthic, antispasmodic and antibacterial agent [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B11">11</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B14">14</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">Z. officinalis</italic> showed potent inhibitory activity against COX-1 enzyme, and also, demonstrated high radical scavenging properties, which may attributed to their contents of gingerols and shoagols. Further understanding of the role of COX inhibitory activity of herbal medicine in mechanisms leading to AD generation is critical to the future development of NSAID therapy for AD from traditional medicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B30">30</xref>].</p><p>Increased oxidative stress causes cell damage in the form of protein, lipid, and DNA oxidations.
