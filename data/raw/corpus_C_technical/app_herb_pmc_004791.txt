# PMC: adaptogen stress response HPA axis — Overview

align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">x</td></tr><tr><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">Metabolic health</td><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1"/><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1"/><td align="left" colspan="1" rowspan="1">x</td></tr></tbody></table></table-wrap></p></sec></sec><sec id="sec0065"><label>4</label><title>Discussion</title><p id="par0240">To examine the clinician perspective on what constitutes adaptogenic activity, a FG study was conducted to explore Australian naturopaths’ (the major providers of WHM in Australia) understanding and clinical use of adaptogens from a WHM perspective, as well as to identify sources of knowledge on adaptogens influential to practice. To our knowledge, this is the first study examining clinical use and expert clinician understanding of adaptogens. This data adds a new, previously unexamined layer to the existing body of evidence on adaptogens, which has had predominant focus on pharmacological perspectives.</p><p id="par0245">An analysis of the data highlights the adaptogenic concept is understood by Australian naturopaths to have been imported into that practice from cultures or traditions outside the Western herbal medicine tradition. However, the traditional origins of the adaptogenic concept appear to be either lost or unknown to clinicians. Though clinicians identified adaptogens coming from ‘other’ herbal cultures of practice, there was no detail (known or mentioned) in relation to the use of adaptogens by other cultures. Rather, clinicians stated it was simply known that many of those particular herbs (that Western herbalists commonly refer to as adaptogens) come from other cultures such as China, India and Russia. Nevertheless, participants suggested the adaptogenic concept aligns with naturopathic principles and philosophy, and as such greater emphasis was placed on the philosophical alignment with traditional knowledge and practice than on the cultural origins of that traditional knowledge.</p><p id="par0250">The general reliance on empirical observation, clinical experience and intuition amongst participants suggests the clinical use of adaptogens may have ‘evolved’ into WHM practice through a combination of experimentation, clinical experience, the emergence of research and the intuitive synthesis of these components with limited traditional knowledge; rather than an adaptation of other traditional medicine practices into WHM practice. Intuition was identified as an important component of participants decision-making process. Intuition can be a contentious topic in complementary medicine commentary, where it has been associated with pseudoscience.<xref rid="bib0150" ref-type="bibr"><sup>30</sup></xref> However, the clinical intuition as described by participants in this study is complex and nuanced, reflecting clinical intuition based on unconscious application of experience commonly held to play a key role in other health professions such as nursing and conventional medicine.<xref rid="bib0155" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>, <xref rid="bib0160" ref-type="bibr">32</xref> In these contexts, the ability of a medical professional to have an intuitive grasp of a situation is held as a sign of experience, deep understanding and a skill to be harnassed.<xref rid="bib0165" ref-type="bibr"><sup>33</sup></xref> Although clinical intuition has been identified amongst naturopaths previously,<xref rid="bib0170" ref-type="bibr"><sup>34</sup></xref> there has been little examination of the role it plays in complementary therapies and where attention has been placed it has a tendency to be misconstrued in a negative light despite evidence placing clinical intuition as an esteemed quality of experienced clinicians. However, our study suggests that examination of the clinical knowledge drawn from intuition may help to provide more clarity, standardisation and perhaps even codification of core clinical concepts commonly employed by CM clinicians, but not readily described in biomedical or scientific literature.</p><p id="par0255">Similarly to ambiguous responses regarding cultural origins of adaptogens, there was no consensus knowledge of sources identified and a wide variety of sources were reported, often with non-specific answers such as ‘traditional sources’ and ‘research’ with no elaboration. Many of the participants reported that they rely heavily on research to inform their use of adaptogenic herbal medicines and appear to trust modern evidence more than traditional sources, noting that these two sources often don’t align. Therefore, the naturopathic use of adaptogens may be less based on absorption of traditional knowledge but rather may represent adaptogens having ‘evolved’ into WHM practice as more scientific and experiential evidence supports their use.</p><p id="par0260">One example of this could be the evolution of a more nuanced understanding of historical broader clinical concepts. The majority of participants associated adaptogen with the traditional concept of ‘tonic’, however whether participants noted the overlap of the two concepts or identified clear differences between the two traditional actions was unclear.
