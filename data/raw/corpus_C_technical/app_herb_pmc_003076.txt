Most species are harvested from the wild, with few structured protection measures. However, some traditional practitioners have initiated cultivation of high-use species, such as <italic toggle="yes">O. lamiifolium</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">M. stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, in home gardens, reflecting a proactive approach to ensure year-round availability and reduce pressure on wild populations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR105">105</xref>]. Cultural beliefs and traditional practices also contribute to in situ conservation, including the protection of sacred groves and selective harvesting methods that preserve roots and mature trees [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>].</p><p id="Par240">The preference ranking conducted in this study confirms agricultural expansion as the most critical threat, followed by firewood collection, charcoal production, and construction activities. Moderate threats include fodder collection and the impacts of modernization, while drought was perceived as the least severe threat due to the adaptation of many species to semi-arid conditions. Globally, similar patterns are observed, with anthropogenic pressures and habitat degradation posing major risks to medicinal plant diversity across Africa, Asia, and the Americas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>].</p><p id="Par241">Community-based strategies in Karamara emphasize sustainable management of medicinal resources. Practices include cultivation of high-use species, controlled harvesting, protection of grazing areas, and replanting of harvested plants. Species are prioritized for conservation based on frequency of use, multipurpose applications, or ecological vulnerability, including <italic toggle="yes">C. procera</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">A. megalacantha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">C. myrrha</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">V. bussei</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">O. lamiifolium</italic>. Intergenerational transfer of knowledge, through mentorship by elders, ensures that sustainable collection techniques, plant identification, and preparation methods are preserved for future generations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>]. Thus, the Karamara forest patches exemplify community-driven conservation, where traditional ecological knowledge, cultural norms, and practical management strategies collectively contribute to the sustainable use and preservation of medicinal plants. These findings highlight the importance of integrating indigenous knowledge with formal conservation initiatives to safeguard ethnomedicinal biodiversity both regionally and globally.</p></sec><sec id="Sec42"><title>Implications of medicinal plant use on Health, food Security, environmental Protection, and cultural value</title><p id="Par242">The use of MPs by the Somali ethnic community in the Karamara forest patches carries profound significance for healthcare, food security, environmental conservation, and cultural heritage.</p><p id="Par243">In terms of healthcare, the community heavily relies on ethnomedicinal knowledge to address a wide range of conditions, including gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, respiratory infections, hypertension, wounds, and skin diseases.
