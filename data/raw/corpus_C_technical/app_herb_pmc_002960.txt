Her description is not as detailed as that by Leeuweenhoek and Swammerdam (who are credited), but she probably did not have access to a microscope like they did—again an informal ban aiding the obliteration of a female scientist [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>]. She described the development of frog eggs and tadpole metamorphosis in her diary in 1686 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR113">113</xref>], more than a decade before Leeuwenhoek observed the same phenomenon and reported it to the Royal Society on September 25, 1699 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR115">115</xref>], and she provided the first representation of the amphibian <italic toggle="yes">Pipa pipa</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Trachycephalus venulosus</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].<fig id="Fig5" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 5</label><caption><p>Metamorphosis of a frog and blue flower by Maria Sibylla Merian, circa 1701–1705. She was the first to observe and document the metamorphosis of tropical insects, the first to illustrate many of the tropical plants in detail, and one of the first to offer a color edition of tropical species. For example, she first documented the plant <italic toggle="yes">Inga edulis</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>].</p><p id="Par44">Linnaeus, considered to be the father of modern taxonomy, used Merian's work to name more than one hundred species, with Maria Sibylla's work being one of the main references for tropical species of insects and plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR111">111</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR114">114</xref>]. Over the centuries, species names have been substituted. The specific epithet <italic toggle="yes">merianella</italic> has been abandoned. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Eulamprotes wilkella</italic> (Linnaeus, 1758), which replaced <italic toggle="yes">Tinea merianella</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Micropterix aureatella</italic> (Scopoli, 1763), which replaced <italic toggle="yes">Phalaena merianella</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>]. One of the names of the frog <italic toggle="yes">Trachycephalus venulosus</italic>, which was illustrated for the first time by her, was <italic toggle="yes">Rana meriana</italic>, which credited the discovery to Maria Sibylla Merian [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>]. The specific epithet “<italic toggle="yes">merianella</italic>” is still used, however, in minor frequency than initially.</p><p id="Par45">Her work was copied in the eighteenth century and influenced many later naturalists, such as Eleazar Albin, August Johann, Rosel von Rosenhof and Mark Catesby. Rosenhof, in his work on German frogs [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR117">117</xref>], included images of frogs in their habitat that resemble her work. Eleazar Albin referred to Maria Sibylla's early books in his compositions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR118">118</xref>]. Mark Catesby’s work “Natural History of Carolina, Florida and the Bahama Islands” [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>] is similar to Sibylla's work in design, length and composition, but he only cites her work to criticize errors; he pointed out that she misunderstood the depiction of the cashew nut. It is well established that all the naturalists cited were familiar with her work [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>].</p><p id="Par46">Natural history was becoming an academic discipline and, at the same time, the name of Maria Sibylla Merian was being suppressed from the history of science [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>]. Unlike most naturalists of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Merian acknowledged the help of enslave people, mainly African and indigenous women, in recognizing species and in gaining knowledge about the uses of plant species [[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>], see the preface and illustration number 45].
