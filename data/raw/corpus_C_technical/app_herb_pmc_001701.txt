They reported that day-60 leaves had antioxidant activity close to that of ascorbic acid (0.1 mg/mL). <xref rid="ref-44" ref-type="bibr">Sreelatha &amp; Padma (2009)</xref> also found that mature <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> leaves had higher antioxidant activity than young leaves (IC<sub>50</sub>: 18.15 ± 0.92 <italic toggle="yes">vs</italic> 19.12 ± 0.75), attributing this to increased phenolic and flavonoid content at later growth stages (<xref rid="ref-44" ref-type="bibr">Sreelatha &amp; Padma, 2009</xref>). The rise in antioxidant potential with increasing leaf maturity in both studies may be due to the accumulation of bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and phenols, which are synthesized progressively during leaf development (<xref rid="ref-23" ref-type="bibr">Kong et al., 2017</xref>).</p><p>For ferric reducing power, we found no significant differences (<italic toggle="yes">p</italic> &gt; 0.05) between the three maturity levels of <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> leaf extract and all values were lower than that of the ascorbic acid standard. This contrasts with <xref rid="ref-33" ref-type="bibr">Pakade, Cukrowska &amp; Chimuka (2013)</xref>, who found <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> leaves from South Africa exhibited stronger ferric reducing power than ascorbic acid when using aqueous extraction. The difference could stem from geographical variation in phytochemical content or the use of ethanol extraction in the present study <italic toggle="yes">vs</italic> aqueous in theirs. <xref rid="ref-31" ref-type="bibr">Nobossé, Fombang &amp; Mbofung (2018)</xref> reported that FRAP activity was highest in day-45 leaves and lowest in day-60 leaves for all extraction methods (methanol, ethanol, and aqueous). Their results differ from the current study, where the lowest FRAP activity was seen on day-30. These inconsistencies may be attributed to regional differences in phytochemical profiles. They also observed a moderate correlation (r = 0.59) between chlorophyll content and FRAP activity. Hence, Malaysian <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> leaves, particularly on day-30, may contain lower levels of chlorophyll and related phytochemicals, resulting in weaker ferric reducing power.</p><p>The finding in our anticholinesterase inhibition activity aligns with a previous study that assessed AChE inhibitory activity using various parts of the <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> plant (seeds, leaves, roots, flowers, stems) extracted with methanol, ethanol, and aqueous solvents (<xref rid="ref-32" ref-type="bibr">Nwidu et al., 2018</xref>). In that study, the ethanol extract of the leaves exhibited 50% AChE inhibition (IC50: 0.2105), which is approximately 14% higher than the inhibition observed in this study. However, the earlier study did not control for specific leaf maturity stages and used a test concentration of 200 µg/mL for the highest inhibition. Anticholinesterase activity is commonly associated with bioactive compounds such as alkaloids and terpenoids, which are abundant in traditional medicinal plants including <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> (<xref rid="ref-21" ref-type="bibr">Mukherjee et al., 2007</xref>). In addition, other non-alkaloid metabolites such as phenolics, flavonoids and coumarin-type compounds are also known as the reversible AChE inhibitors (<xref rid="ref-1" ref-type="bibr">Abu-Aisheh et al., 2019</xref>; <xref rid="ref-22" ref-type="bibr">Khan et al., 2019</xref>; <xref rid="ref-45" ref-type="bibr">Szwajgier, 2015</xref>).</p><p>Previous research indicates that alkaloids are more efficiently extracted with ethanol, whereas terpenoids are better extracted with aqueous solvents (<xref rid="ref-4" ref-type="bibr">Adekanmi, Adekanmi &amp; Adekanmi, 2020</xref>). However, in the present work, the untargeted UHPLC-MS/MS performed in negative ESI mode is less sensitive to alkaloids, making it difficult to confirm their presence in the leaf extracts. Hence, it is more plausible that the observed AChE inhibition activities are attributed to the non-alkaloid metabolites. Indeed, the PLS biplot model presented alignment of coumarin and folic acid with the AChE vector, thus supporting the likelihood of their contribution to enzyme inhibition in day-30 extracts. Nonetheless, the confounding basis remains exploratory, and future studies are necessary to confirm the neuroprotective potential of young <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> leaf (day-30).</p><p>The chemical diversity of <italic toggle="yes">M. oleifera</italic> leaves highlights their potential contribution to antioxidant and neuroprotective bioactivities (<xref rid="ref-24" ref-type="bibr">Leone et al., 2016</xref>; <xref rid="ref-30" ref-type="bibr">Mbikay, 2012</xref>; <xref rid="ref-48" ref-type="bibr">Vergara-Jimenez, Almatrafi &amp; Fernandez, 2017</xref>).
