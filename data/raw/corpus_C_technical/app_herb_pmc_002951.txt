Wouldn't the fact that the same Academy, which rejected the celebrated Marie Sklodowska Curie for membership, elected a woman as president in 1995 be a significant enough milestone for Marianne Grunberg–Manago's name to enter the scientists' hall of fame? Or the fact that she discovered the first enzyme that synthesizes nucleic acid? And how about the female scientists who were awarded the Nobel Prize? Would not being recognized by one of the most prestigious awards prove the relevance of their contributions? Donna Strickland herself, the 2018 Physics laureate, admitted not knowing the only other female Physics previously laureate (besides Marie Curie), Maria Goeppert-Mayer; Andrea Ghez is currently the fourth laureate in that category. Does not a Nobel Prize guarantee recognition in your own area of expertise?</p><p id="Par16">The Matilda’s Effect was first described by the nineteenth century abolitionist suffragette Matilda Joslyn Gage (1826–1898) in her essay Woman as Inventor and was coined by science historian Margaret Rossiter in 1993 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>]. This effect results in an erasure of women scientists; their contributions to science are not given due credit or, when they are, the names of these women are nonetheless neglected from history.</p><p id="Par17">Then, the question arises whether it is recognized that women carried out scientific expeditions from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century, mainly in South America, documenting the use of plants, especially medicinal ones, and how much of the valuable information collected by them is being lost or little explored.</p></sec><sec id="Sec5"><title>The case of Maria Sibylla Merian</title><p id="Par18">It is possible to contribute to the reversal of scarcity of female names and deconstruct the image that sciences were an exclusively male practice in the past by highlighting experiences of women and recognizing their participation in the production of knowledge [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>].</p><p id="Par19">One name that stands out is Maria Sibylla Merian (1647–1717) (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>), a German researcher who published a book on insects and plants in Surinam after a self-funded scientific expedition [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>]. Merian traveled on her own in pursuit of her own scientific questions and that is why her trajectory differs from the majority of other women naturalists who were normally in the position of colonial wives, accompanying the project of their husbands [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>].<fig id="Fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>Portrait of Maria Sibylla Merian (1647–1717) painted by Jacob Marrel in 1679</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="MO1" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="13002_2023_589_Fig1_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par20">From childhood, she studied insects, mainly butterflies and moths, and also collected caterpillars in gardens. For years, she systematically observed and noted the behavior of caterpillars, drawing each stage of their development [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>]. In 1679, she published the first edition of “<italic toggle="yes">Der Raupen Wunderbare Verwandelung, und sonderbare Blumennahrung</italic>” [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>] (<italic toggle="yes">The Wonderful Transformation of Caterpillars and Their Eating Habits</italic>), containing 50 plates of engravings and commentaries on European insects; later she would publish two more editions of this book [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref>].</p><p id="Par21">At age 52, in 1699, Maria Sibylla Merian embarked on a self-funded scientific expedition to Surinam to study insects and plants, without any official sponsorship or a team [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>]. Taking her daughter, Dorothea, as a helper, she left with only the money she could scrape together by selling illustrations. Mother and daughter spent two years in Surinam and then returned to the Netherlands, where Merian devoted four years to writing <italic toggle="yes">Metamorphosis Insectorum Surinamensium</italic>, which was published in its first edition in Dutch and Latin in 1705 (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>]. She collected several specimens, mainly seeds and leaves; however, her voucher specimens did not survive over the years [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref>].<fig id="Fig2" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 2</label><caption><p>Cover page from the Dutch version of Maria Sibylla Merian’s <italic toggle="yes">Metamorphosis Insectorum Surinamensium</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>], published in 1705</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="MO2" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="13002_2023_589_Fig2_HTML.jpg"/></fig></p><p id="Par22">The aim of the present report is to reunite the name of many women naturalists who registered knowledge about native flora in scientific expeditions around the globe, mainly South America, focusing on the work of Maria Sibylla Merian and discussing the patterns of the suppression of women scientists.
