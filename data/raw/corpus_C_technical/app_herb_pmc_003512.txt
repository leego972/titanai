Herbaceous species are often preferred for household-level remedies because they are easy to collect, regenerate quickly, and can be prepared fresh with minimal processing. Similar findings have been documented in Uganda, Kenya, Europe, and Asia, where herbs are frequently associated with short-term treatments and everyday healthcare practices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR97">97</xref>]. The relatively lower number of herbs in Sheikh District may be linked to the district’s semi-arid climate, which favors woody vegetation over seasonal herbaceous growth.</p><p id="Par93">Climbers were the least represented growth form in the study area. This limited presence aligns with many ethnobotanical studies across Africa and Asia, where climbers tend to be less abundant or less accessible in dry environments and therefore contribute less prominently to traditional pharmacopoeias [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR98">98</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR99">99</xref>]. Their marginal role may also reflect cultural preferences for woody species, which are often perceived as more potent or reliable in treating common ailments.</p><p id="Par94">Hence, the distribution of medicinal plant growth forms in Sheikh District demonstrates a clear reliance on trees and shrubs, mirroring both the ecological composition of the landscape and culturally embedded healing practices. However, this dependence also raises important conservation concerns, as woody perennials often regenerate slowly and may be particularly vulnerable to overharvesting when bark or roots are collected. Targeted conservation measures and sustainable harvesting strategies are therefore essential to ensure the long-term availability of these medicinal resources and to safeguard the indigenous ethnobotanical knowledge systems that depend upon them.</p></sec><sec id="Sec15"><title>Sources of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par95">The ethnobotanical survey in Sheikh District showed that MPs are obtained from both wild habitats and cultivated settings, reflecting the ecological knowledge and adaptive practices of local communities. Among the 54 recorded species, the majority were harvested from natural ecosystems, including forests, mountainous zones, and communal rangelands. This strong dependence on wild resources highlights the continued importance of surrounding biodiversity for primary healthcare and underscores the role of indigenous ecological expertise in locating, identifying, and harvesting medicinal species in culturally appropriate ways [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR100">100</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR103">103</xref>].</p><p id="Par96">A similar reliance on wild vegetation has been widely reported across East Africa, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, where traditional medicine remains closely tied to natural landscapes due to limited access to biomedical services and enduring cultural trust in herbal remedies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR109">109</xref>]. In this sense, Sheikh District reflects broader ethnomedicinal patterns found in pastoral and agro-pastoral societies across semi-arid Africa.</p><p id="Par97">Key wild-harvested species in Sheikh District included <italic toggle="yes">Vachellia spp.</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Balanites aegyptiaca</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Commiphora myrrha</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Eucalyptus camaldulensis</italic>. These plants are valued not only for their therapeutic roles but also for their availability within the local environment, enabling communities to address a wide range of ailments using familiar and accessible resources. Comparable patterns of wild harvesting have been documented in Nigeria, Cameroon, India, and Pakistan, illustrating a global trend in indigenous healthcare systems that prioritize locally abundant medicinal taxa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR115">115</xref>].</p><p id="Par98">In contrast, a smaller yet important proportion of MPs were sourced from home gardens and cultivated areas, particularly species that are frequently used and easily propagated. Examples include <italic toggle="yes">Allium sativum</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Azadirachta indica</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Moringa stenopetala</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Carica papaya</italic>. Cultivation near households ensures year-round availability, reduces collection pressure on wild populations, and integrates MPs into everyday domestic healthcare practices. Similar home-garden strategies have been reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, China, and India, where cultivated MPs function both as conservation reservoirs and as accessible sources of nutrition and therapy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR116">116</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR119">119</xref>].</p><p id="Par99">Some species, such as <italic toggle="yes">Aloe spp.</italic>, were obtained from both wild and cultivated environments depending on seasonal availability and the plant part required.
