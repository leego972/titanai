The main sources of foreign imported species are East Asia (11%), Southwest Asia (11 %), West and South Asia (8%) and Arabia (4%).</p><p id="Par29">The high proportion of imported plants (as medicinal materials) shows indirect evidence of prolific import in Biblical times [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR104">104</xref>]. Our list does not provide any evidence that any species were cultivated/imported solely as medicinal plants; all had some additional use. Most (87%) of the species had at least one additional use: for example, 16 are edible, 8 are used in rituals, 6 serve for perfume and cosmetics, and 5 are used as incense.</p><p id="Par30">Duke [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>] enumerated 176 plant species as “Biblical Medicinal Plants,” while Jacob [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>] suggested only 55. In our survey, we suggest reducing that number to 45 (Tables <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>-<xref rid="Tab4" ref-type="table">4</xref>). The overlap between Jacob’s list and ours was 29 species in total. Our contribution is 20 “new” suggested BMPs. It is noteworthy that some Biblical names are related to the genus level (e.g., <italic toggle="yes">Artemisia</italic>), or also to two genera as in the case of <italic toggle="yes">Cupressus/Juniperus</italic>.</p><p id="Par31">The discrepancy between Jacob’s list and ours is due to the following: (1) At least 22 species in Jacob’s list are not recognized today as valid “Biblical plants” at all, or they are not related to any specific plant species or genus [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>]. (2) Several identifications from Campbell–Thompson [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>], the only Mesopotamian source used by Jacob, are no longer recognized by modern Assyriologists. (3) Several Mesopotamian plants were only recently identified in a medical context. (4) New recent palynological as well archaeological data allow us to corroborate the possibility of Biblical medicinal uses of some plants.</p><p id="Par32">We excluded about 24 plant species from Jacob’s list [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>] for the following reasons: they are no longer recognized by Amar [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>] as plants, or the reliability of identification level is below “low probability of identification” or is an “aggregate name” and not a species-specific plant name (hence, indicated as NR); the identification by Campbell–Thompson is no longer recognized today or cannot be confirmed by any further evidence (NC-P); the plant is unknown to the flora of the Holy Land (NF); no evidence exists of international trading in the plant (NT); identification is obscure and/or non-specific (OB); they are recorded only from Egypt, with no further evidence related to the Holy Land (OE); they are found only in Mesopotamia (OM), with no data from Egypt and/or Mesopotamia on any medicinal use (EM).</p><p id="Par33">These plants are (1) <italic toggle="yes">Zizyphus vulgaris</italic> Lam.
