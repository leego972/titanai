# PMC: optical coherence tomography clinical application — Overview

xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="anchor" id="jats-graphic-19" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="CEO-53-682-g002.jpg"/></fig></sec></sec><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0015"><label>5</label><title>Limitations</title><p>OCTA is a primarily light‐based interferometry imaging modality; thus, image acquisition and quality in OCTA are highly affected by media clarity. Optic disc oedema in acute optic neuropathies can artifactually obscure vessel details [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0121" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>], acute retinal oedema may obscure retinal microvasculature [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0044" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>], and altered retinal morphology may cause difficulty with automation of vascular segmentation [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. The image quality is sensitive to movement artefact, and this may be affected by poor fixation in those with low vision and in neurological movement disorders [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0018" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0130" ref-type="bibr">130</xref>]. Similar to OCT, the OCTA does not have normative data for different ethnic race and refractive error [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0131" ref-type="bibr">131</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0132" ref-type="bibr">132</xref>]. Therefore, abnormal ocular morphology, such as high myopia and astigmatism, may interfere with automation of analysis. Furthermore, small scan diameter limits analysis. One possible way to overcome this is with ultrawide OCTA or OCTA montage [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0041" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0133" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>]. Lastly, the OCTA detects the red blood cell in a column of vessel rather than following it in real time. Therefore, it does not depict leakage from blood vessels or differentiate regions of low flow [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0133" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0134" ref-type="bibr">134</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0016"><label>6</label><title>Future Applicability of <styled-content style="fixed-case" toggle="no">OCTA</styled-content> in Neuro‐Ophthalmology</title><p>A multimodal imaging approach in neuro‐ophthalmology is becoming important to help with the structural–functional correlate. The OCTA may assist neuro‐ophthalmologists in differentiating between optic nerve pathology in early disease and guide them in predicting the natural history of pathology affecting optic nerve function. The OCTA has the advantage of quantitative tracking of capillary density loss in inflammatory optic neuropathies like NMOSD, MS‐ON as well as hereditary optic nerve diseases like LHON. We are seeing more longitudinal studies in OCTA use in inflammatory and hereditary optic nerve disease. More research is needed to map optic disc vascular changes and improve the specificity and sensitivity of OCTA. Further study to map optic disc vascular changes and improve the specificity and sensitivity of OCTA would be advantageous to help with disease prognostication and monitor treatment progress.</p><p>There are prospective longitudinal studies looking at vascular disease such as NAION to understand the role of the peripapillary vascular changes to help understand the interplay of the vascular versus mechanical risk factors in NAION. Standardisation of OCTA protocol to allow cross‐reference and integration of OCTA into the evaluation of individuals with optic nerve pathology could very well combat these limitations.</p></sec><sec sec-type="conclusions" id="ceo14544-sec-0017"><label>7</label><title>Conclusions</title><p>OCT Angiography has emerged as a crucial tool in assessing microvascular changes associated with neuro‐ophthalmology diseases. It helps by providing detailed structural and functional correlates in optic nerve disease, and often the microvascular structural changes are seen before optic nerve functional change. This review also highlights the emerging pattern to look at the OCTA changes in the non‐affected eye in inflammatory and hereditary optic neuropathy; OCTA may help guide understanding in both disease pathogenesis as well as prognostication in neuro‐ophthalmic conditions.</p></sec><sec sec-type="COI-statement" id="ceo14544-sec-0018"><title>Conflicts of Interest</title><p>The authors declare no conflicts of interest.</p></sec></body><back><ack id="ceo14544-sec-21"><title>Acknowledgements</title><p>Open access publishing facilitated by Flinders University, as part of the Wiley ‐ Flinders University agreement via the Council of Australian University Librarians.</p></ack><sec sec-type="data-availability" id="ceo14544-sec-0020"><title>Data Availability Statement</title><p>The data that support the findings of this study are available on request from the corresponding author.
