This ensured that the research was conducted in accordance with local governance structures, customary norms, and community expectations, supporting ethical collaboration and respectful documentation of indigenous knowledge systems.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec12"><title>Results and discussion</title><sec id="Sec13"><title>Taxonomic diversity of medicinal plants</title><p id="Par75">The present ethnobotanical study documented a total of 54 medicinal plant species, representing 47 genera and 34 botanical families, used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland (Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref>). This diversity reflects not only the botanical richness of the district but also the depth of local ethnomedicinal knowledge that has developed through long-term interaction between communities and their surrounding environments.</p><p id="Par76">Comparable levels of medicinal plant richness have been reported in other ethnobotanical investigations across East Africa, including Ethiopia, Kenya, and Uganda, where district-level studies commonly record between 40 and 80 medicinal species. Such variation is often shaped by ecological heterogeneity, cultural traditions, and the degree of dependence on plant-based healthcare systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>]. In this broader regional context, the findings from Sheikh District contribute important baseline data for understanding both shared and locally distinctive ethnomedicinal practices within the Horn of Africa.</p><p id="Par77">Among the recorded families, Fabaceae was the most species-rich, contributing 10 species (18.5%) of the total medicinal flora. The prominence of Fabaceae is widely recognized across African ethnomedicine and has been consistently reported in studies from Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, Nigeria, and other regions where Fabaceae members are frequently used to treat diverse human ailments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>].</p><p id="Par78">From a cultural and ecological perspective, the dominance of Fabaceae may reflect the wide distribution of this family in semi-arid landscapes, its accessibility to pastoral and agro-pastoral communities, and the long-standing recognition of its therapeutic value. Many Fabaceae species are also known to contain bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, tannins, and alkaloids, which may reinforce local perceptions of their effectiveness. Similar patterns have been observed beyond Africa, particularly in South and East Asia, where Fabaceae plants remain central to traditional systems such as Ayurveda, Unani, and Chinese medicine [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref>]. This cross-cultural recurrence suggests that certain plant families may hold broadly recognized medicinal potential across different ecological and cultural settings.</p><p id="Par79">Asphodelaceae and Solanaceae, each represented by three species (5.5%), also formed important components of the local pharmacopoeia. The significance of Asphodelaceae, especially Aloe species, has been widely documented in arid and semi-arid areas of Ethiopia, Somalia, and Kenya, where these plants are commonly used for wound care, gastrointestinal disorders, and dermatological conditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>].</p><p id="Par80">Similarly, the use of Solanaceae species corresponds with ethnomedicinal reports from Uganda, Cameroon, and Tanzania, where Solanum taxa are often employed in managing respiratory illnesses, fever, and inflammatory problems. However, such use is typically accompanied by careful cultural knowledge regarding dosage and preparation, reflecting community awareness of potential toxicity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR63">63</xref>].</p><p id="Par81">Several additional families, including Apocynaceae, Combretaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Meliaceae, Myrtaceae, and Zygophyllaceae, were represented by two species each. This selective representation highlights that medicinal plant use is not solely determined by plant abundance, but also by accumulated cultural experience, perceived efficacy, and symbolic importance. Comparable patterns have been reported from Ethiopia, Sudan, and Nigeria, where communities often prioritize specific taxa that are culturally trusted and therapeutically valued [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR64">64</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR69">69</xref>].</p><p id="Par82">For instance, Meliaceae species are widely valued across Africa and Asia for antimicrobial and antiparasitic applications, while Myrtaceae members are frequently used for respiratory and digestive ailments in diverse regions including Europe, Australia, and South Asia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>].
