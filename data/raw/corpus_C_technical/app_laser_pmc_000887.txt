The OCT attenuation in the dermis has been assessed <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>, but there is generally limited data in the epidermis,<xref rid="r1" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1</sup></xref> because on many parts of the body it is too thin (sub <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math174"><mml:mrow><mml:mo form="prefix">∼</mml:mo><mml:mn>100</mml:mn><mml:mtext> </mml:mtext><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> for hairy skin) to be readily amenable to measurement. Schmitt et al.<xref rid="r1" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1</sup></xref> were the first to apply an OCT-based method (based on the single-scattering model) to measure the attenuation coefficient in normal cutaneous dermal tissue <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>. They applied their method to human forearm <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>, reporting much higher attenuation values of dermis (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math182"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi>OCT</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>: 10 to <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math183"><mml:mrow><mml:mn>13</mml:mn><mml:mtext>  </mml:mtext><mml:msup><mml:mrow><mml:mi>mm</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mo>−</mml:mo><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>; AFR: <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math184"><mml:mrow><mml:mn>100</mml:mn><mml:mtext>  </mml:mtext><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">m</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) than Schmitt et al. at the same mean wavelength (1300 nm). They attributed this difference to the different experimental protocols, such as the pressure due to the contacting probe and the use of clearing agent (i.e., glycerol) in Schmitt et al.’s work. These factors will be further discussed in Sec. <xref ref-type="sec" rid="sec5">5</xref>.</p><p>The single-scattering model used in these studies assumes tissue homogeneity over the depth range (i.e., AFR) used to estimate the attenuation coefficient. However, the dermis is perfused with a network of blood vessels with highly distinct optical properties. Experimental determination of the OCT attenuation coefficient of whole blood is challenging due to the very high forward scattering of red blood cells.<xref rid="r47" ref-type="bibr"><sup>47</sup></xref> Bosschaart et al.<xref rid="r68" ref-type="bibr"><sup>68</sup></xref> modeled scattering properties of whole blood using Mie theory (to describe a single blood cell) combined with the Percus–Yevick structure factor to account for nonlinear scaling of optical coefficients with volume fraction, which are especially prevalent at high hematocrits. At 1300 nm, they found a high scattering coefficient of <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math185"><mml:mrow><mml:mn>35</mml:mn><mml:mtext>  </mml:mtext><mml:msup><mml:mrow><mml:mi>mm</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mo>−</mml:mo><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> (close to values reported from the literature of <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math186"><mml:mrow><mml:mn>40</mml:mn><mml:mtext>  </mml:mtext><mml:msup><mml:mrow><mml:mi>mm</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mo>−</mml:mo><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> at 1300 nm<xref rid="r68" ref-type="bibr"><sup>68</sup></xref>) and a scattering anisotropy (<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="math187"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>g</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>) of 0.96, indicating a very high degree of forward scattering by whole blood.
