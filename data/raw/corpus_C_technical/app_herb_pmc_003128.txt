We know, however, that Gossypol, produced especially in the seeds in <italic toggle="yes">Gossypium </italic>ssp. is highly toxic to fungi that are pathogenic to animals (Mace, R.D. Stipanovic et al. 1993); and gossypol is particularly effective against the trypanosomosis diseases including African Sleeping Sickness and South American Chagas Disease (Woerdenbag 1993). Gossypol is also cytotoxic to tumor cells (Woerdenbag 1993). Gossypol functions as a contraceptive for men--it is antispermatogenic, reducing total sperm count and sperm motility and velocity (Coutinho EM, Segal SJ et al. 1985). <italic toggle="yes">Gossypium </italic>leaves contain red and green pigments (gossypurin and gossyverdurin) which have similar chemical structures to gossypol in the seeds (Waller, Zaveveld et al. 1985). Infertility may be an unwanted side affect of gossypium medications, however (Lewis, Elvin-Lewis et al. 2003).</p></sec></sec><sec><title><italic toggle="yes">Ti dité</italic> - <italic toggle="yes">Lippia micromera</italic> Schauer</title><sec><title>Vouchers UMO-186354, UMO-186385, University of Missouri herbarium</title><p>The second most salient Dominican herb for fright is <italic toggle="yes">Lippia micromera </italic>Schauer (Verbenaceae), known locally as <italic toggle="yes">ti dité</italic>. Informants mentioned <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>in 45% of the fright freelists. Bwa Mawego residents call <italic toggle="yes">Lippia micromera </italic>"ti dité<italic toggle="yes">" </italic>when speaking both English and French Creoles, and, rarely, "small tine" (pronounced with an N) in English. Elsewhere, common names for this species include "false thyme," "Spanish thyme," and <italic toggle="yes">oregano del país </italic>[<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B76">76</xref>].</p><p>Ti dité, <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera</italic>, is a thyme-scented shrub that is often about a meter high locally, but reaches nearly two meters (see figure <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F3">3</xref>). It has thin, woody stems bearing opposite pairs of small (&lt;12 mm) oval leaves and inflorescences that are tiny, white, tubular flowers with yellow throats growing in heads (figure <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F4">4</xref>).</p><fig id="F3" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 3</label><caption><p><bold>Close-up view of Dominican <italic toggle="yes">ti dité, Lippia micromera </italic>Schauer</bold>.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="1746-4269-6-9-3.jpg"/></fig><fig id="F4" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 4</label><caption><p><bold>Full-plant view of a larger Dominican <italic toggle="yes">ti dité, Lippia micromera </italic>Schauer, plant with Washington State University students Christie Stordeur (left) and Sarah Council, 5'3" (on right)</bold>.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="1746-4269-6-9-4.jpg"/></fig><p>Like the first humans to inhabit Dominica [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B77">77</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>is native to the northern, Caribbean coast of South America--i.e., Columbia, Venezuela and the Guianas, and possibly to Trinidad and Margarita Island [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B76">76</xref>]. <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>occurs elsewhere in Dominica [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B68">68</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B78">78</xref>] and is cultivated in home gardens throughout the Caribbean [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B76">76</xref>]. It is a seasoning in Northern South America, Central America and the Caribbean that people especially tend to eat with meat in soup and gravy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B79">79</xref>].</p><p>In Bwa Mawego, <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>is common for seasoning and medicine and one of the village's most familiar herbs. Practically every household tends a <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>shrub in its home-garden. Locals report that <italic toggle="yes">ti dité </italic>bush tea is calming and warms the blood.</p><p>The literature contains various mentions of tropical peoples' medicinal use of <italic toggle="yes">Lippia </italic>ssp. (see [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B80">80</xref>] for genus review); however, there are few ethnographic reports for <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>specifically. People of San Felipe, Yaracuy State, Venezuela use <italic toggle="yes">L. micromera </italic>for upset stomach [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B81">81</xref>].
