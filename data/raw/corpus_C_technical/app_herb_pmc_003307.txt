This indicates that while the Portuguese-speaking peoples that inhabited the area mainly used Portuguese words to describe the natural world, they also relied upon indigenous Tupi names to some degree. This indigenous influence is not only reflected in the vernacular plant names at the VOP and the number of native species, but also in medicinal and religious plant uses. For example, the thin papery inner bark of <italic toggle="yes">Couratari guianensis</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">tauari</italic>), whose presence in Belém and surroundings was not previously documented, is used during Amazonian indigenous and Afro-Brazilian ceremonies as tobacco paper to roll ritual cigars [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>]. We also found combinations of Tupi and Portuguese names, such as <italic toggle="yes">pião-paje</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Jatropha podagrica</italic> Hook.) and <italic toggle="yes">jurema preta</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Mimosa tenuiflora</italic> (Willd.) Poir.). These are prime examples of names shaped by cultural exchange among people from different cultural and linguistic backgrounds in the area.</p><p id="Par31">We found a few vernacular names that had sub-Saharan African origins. Two of these directly refer to Africa: <italic toggle="yes">alecrim-de-Angola</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Vitex agnus-castus</italic>), native to the Mediterranean region, and <italic toggle="yes">manjerona-de-Angola</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Lippia thymoides</italic>), native to Brazil. The term Angola comes originally from Kimbundu, a Bantu language spoken in Angola, and it etymologically refers to a country of people from the Bantu linguistic group [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR76">76</xref>]. This word was used in the past by European traders to indicate the western coastline of Central Africa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>]. Although it is unclear when <italic toggle="yes">alecrim-de-Angola</italic> entered into Portuguese vocabulary, <italic toggle="yes">V. agnus-castus</italic> is used in Afro-Brazilian ceremonies in baths, as in the <italic toggle="yes">Banho de São João</italic> (bath of Saint John), an Afro-Brazilian ritual during the commemoration of St. John in Belém [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>].</p><p id="Par32">Further, the vernacular name <italic toggle="yes">manjerona-de-Angola</italic> has been documented before in Belém, but associated with <italic toggle="yes">Origanum majorana</italic> L. instead of <italic toggle="yes">Lippia thymoides</italic> [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>]. A closely related plant, <italic toggle="yes">Lippia multiflora</italic> Moldenke, is used as medicine and in rituals along the western coast of Africa [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR78">78</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR79">79</xref>]. We suggest that <italic toggle="yes">L. multiflora</italic> was replaced in use by <italic toggle="yes">L. thymoides</italic> in Belém, and it is possibly used in Afro-Brazilian ceremonies.</p><p id="Par33">The last vernacular name with an African etymology, <italic toggle="yes">tapete de Oxalá</italic> (rug of Oxalá in English, <italic toggle="yes">Episcia cupreata</italic>), is also used in other parts of Brazil for other plant species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>]. Oxalá is a Candomblé deity and has a West African etymology, but it is unknown when and how this name was introduced into the Brazilian Portuguese vocabulary [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>], and therefore how and by whom <italic toggle="yes">Episcia cupreata</italic> was first associated with this deity.</p><p id="Par34">Thus, contrary to our expectation, we found very few vernacular names of medicinal and ritual plants sold at the Ver-o-Peso with African-derived origins. Yet, many of the plants we documented at the VOP with Portuguese and Tupi vernacular names, including African and non-African species, are used in rituals and ceremonies performed by followers of Afro-Brazilian religions, like Candomblé [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR81">81</xref>]. Other areas in Brazil, for instance in the northeast, where the Afro-Brazilian population is larger and may have experienced less difficulty in preserving Yoruba and Kikongo language elements, may exhibit other vernacular plant name patterns, including more African-derived plant names [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>]. So, while we documented only a few plant names of African origin, several plants sold at the VOP were associated with ritual uses that were influenced by Afro-Brazilians.
