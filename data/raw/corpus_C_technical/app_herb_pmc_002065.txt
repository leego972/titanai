Phenolic Composition of Almond Skin</title><p>In the industry, almond skin is obtained by blanching or roasting. Upon these processes, the entire almond is subjected to high temperatures (around 100 °C for blanching and up to 200 °C for roasting) to obtain skinless almond kernels. Even though the almond skin represents just around 4.0% of the total almond weight, 60.0–80.0% of the almond phenolic compounds are distributed within the almond skin [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>,<xref rid="B51-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>]. However, thermal processes in the food industry may affect the stability of polyphenols and promote the degradation and loss of bioactive compounds [<xref rid="B52-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. For instance, albeit subjected to lower temperatures compared to industrial roasting, almond skin polyphenols are easily lost by hot water blanching and demonstrate a lower phenolic content [<xref rid="B53-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>]. Furthermore, results from one study suggested that the majority of the phenolic compounds in the kernel itself does not consist of flavonoids [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Eleven out of 19 phenolic compounds identified in almond kernels, skin, and blanch water (<xref rid="molecules-22-01774-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>) were found only in the skin, whilst 95.0% of the individual flavonoids identified were also present in the skin [<xref rid="B23-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. This situation also points out the role of flavonoids as phytoalexins, secondary metabolites that plants synthesize for self-defense, protecting the skin from different stresses.</p><p>Sang et al. (2002) isolated nine phenolic compounds that were identified as flavonol glycosides, flavanone glycoside, and flavanol and benzoic acid derivatives (<xref rid="molecules-22-01774-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>) [<xref rid="B28-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Upon the determination of the radical scavenging activity using the 1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl (DPPH) test, the strong radical scavenging activity of catechin, protocatechuic acid, vanillic acid, and <italic toggle="yes">p</italic>-hydroxybenzoic acid, as well as of all identified flavonol glycosides, except kaempferol-3-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-α-<sc>l</sc>-rhamnopyranosyl-(1→6)-β-<sc>d</sc>-glucopyranoside, which exhibited very weak DPPH radical scavenging activity, was demonstrated. The basis of the DPPH assay is the scavenging of DPPH radicals by antioxidant molecules [<xref rid="B59-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>,<xref rid="B60-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>] since the radical compound is stable and does not need to be generated [<xref rid="B60-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>]. Thus, this is one of the methods more widely used for determining the antioxidant activity of different food matrices, although frequently its results are complemented by those provided by other methods evaluating molecules with different polarity or the reduction power. This approach is justified by the existence of numerous methods to determine the radical scavenging activity, while their use depends on some factors such as the nature of the sample, the mechanism of generation of target molecules, and the end-product determination, among others [<xref rid="B61-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>]. Many studies have been focused on determining the antioxidant activity of bioactive compounds in almond skin by the radical scavenging methods DPPH [<xref rid="B28-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>,<xref rid="B31-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>,<xref rid="B32-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="B62-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>] and ABTS [<xref rid="B56-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>], the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay [<xref rid="B56-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B62-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>,<xref rid="B63-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>], and the oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assay [<xref rid="B30-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>,<xref rid="B31-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>,<xref rid="B53-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>,<xref rid="B56-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>,<xref rid="B62-molecules-22-01774" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>].
