It can be seen that the main criteria in the OCT detection are layer thickness and tiny structure of mucosa. The high resolution details have been proved to be consistent to those in the anatomic and histological images [<xref rid="ref029" ref-type="bibr">29, 35, 57, 58</xref>], as an example shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="xst-27-xst190559-g004">Figure 4</xref>, which makes OCT to be called “optical biopsy”. Actually, compared with widely used clinic tomography modalities, the key advantage of OCT is its micrometer resolution which enables the detection of tiny pathological changes and validated the comparable performance of OCT in clinic with some gold diagnosing standards [<xref rid="ref014" ref-type="bibr">14, 59, 60</xref>], making OCT a potential clinic alternative in condition of contraindication, tissue removal impossible, avoiding tissue destruction and so on. Take ultrasonic tomography which has similar theory and ability to be used in endoscopy with OCT as example, Mueller-Lisse [<xref rid="ref061" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>] compared the endoluminal ultrasonography and OCT images of porcine upper urinary tract, and demonstrated the better performance of OCT in distinguishing between urothelium, lamina propria, and muscle layer. Horeman [<xref rid="ref060" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>] displayed OCT and IVUS images of cartilage with defects, and found that the OCT has better performance in indicating defects smaller than 1 mm.</p><fig id="xst-27-xst190559-g002" orientation="portrait" position="float"><label>Fig.2</label><caption><p>SD-OCT (a) and OCTA (b-d) images of a patient with diabetic macular edema before (top) and after (bottom) intravitreal dexamethasone implant [<xref rid="ref018" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>].
