# PMC: ethnobotany medicinal plants Africa Asia — Overview

Cross-cultural and ethnopharmacology literature on the medicinal plants suggests probable efficacy in accord with Dominican ethnomedical notions of fright. Further, the cultural salience and beliefs about these medicines may give these medications extra psychoneuroimmune (i.e. mind-body) benefits, or placebo-like effects, for this stress-related folk BY</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec><title>Background</title><p>In Caribbean Creole English, "fright" not only indicates sudden fear or shock, but also panic, anguish, and worry [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B1">1</xref>]. Fright also is an idiom for a prolonged, distressed state blamed on an emotional overload of fear, shock, panic, grieving or anguish. Accounts of Anglophone and Francophone Caribbeans allude to fright as an illness [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B2">2</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>]. Antillean French Creole speakers call it <italic toggle="yes">sésisma</italic>, also written <italic toggle="yes">sézisman </italic>[cf [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B6">6</xref>]] (from the French <italic toggle="yes">saisissement</italic>, translating to shock or sudden chill). The French Creole term, like the Creole English one, indicates both an emotion and the illness that the emotion may generate. The sufferer is said to "have fright" or to "be frightened."</p><p>Here I describe the ethnomedical syndrome of fright, or <italic toggle="yes">sésisma</italic>, in the Commonwealth of Dominica, an island-nation in the Lesser Antilles. Dominicans are bilingual in English and French Caribbean Creoles, and Dominican culture shares traits with both English and French-speaking Caribbean islands, where fright, or <italic toggle="yes">sésisma </italic>is familiar. I describe Dominican views of fright's etiology, ethnophysiology, ethnopharmacology, and the epidemiology of fright in terms of its salience, treatment and occurrence in the Dominican village of Bwa Mawego.</p><p>Caribbean fright illness has not been previously detailed in the literature. Caribbean fright's perceived causes and symptoms differ from other fright illnesses from around the world (including <italic toggle="yes">susto </italic>among neighboring Hispanic populations). The general pattern of this ethnopsychiatric syndrome, or idiom of distress, is nevertheless reminiscent of others. In their seminal review <italic toggle="yes">The Culture-Bound Syndromes</italic>, Simons and Hughes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B7">7</xref>] created a whole section on "the fright illness taxon," Simons and Hughes present seven taxa of syndromes that have culturally specific [or "bounded"] illness expressions, but with comparable etiology or symptoms.) Indeed, various world cultures associate an illness with emotional fright. Symptoms and treatments of fright illnesses vary from culture to culture, but all fright illnesses are blamed on a fright or trauma--many societies even use a term translating to "fright" for an illness. Other fright illnesses include, perhaps most famously, <italic toggle="yes">susto </italic>in Latin America [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>], but also <italic toggle="yes">ceeb </italic>among Hmong [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B9">9</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">fijac </italic>in Yemen [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B10">10</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">kesambet </italic>in Bali [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B11">11</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">lanti </italic>in the Philippines [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B12">12</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">mogo laya </italic>in New Guinea [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B13">13</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">narahati </italic>in Iran [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B14">14</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">saladera </italic>in the Peruvian Amazon [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B7">7</xref>], and "reduced soul" in Cambodia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B15">15</xref>].
