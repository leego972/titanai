Morphology, Systematics, Distribution, and Ethnobotanical Relevance of <italic toggle="yes">Polyscias fruticosa</italic> (L.) Harms</title><p><italic toggle="yes">Polyscias fruticosa</italic> is a perennial, glabrous shrub or undershrub in the Araliaceae family, characterized by a woody, sparsely to moderately branched stem and a distinctive crown of finely dissected, compound leaves. The species is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, the Pacific Islands, Africa, and the Caribbean, primarily for its ornamental and medicinal properties. It typically grows to a height of 1.5 to 4 m, depending on environmental conditions and cultivation practices [<xref rid="B1-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="B2-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>].</p><p>Leaves are alternate, bipinnate to tripinnate and imparipinnate, with long petioles (7–16 cm) and total dimensions reaching up to 29 × 25 cm. Each leaf comprises 7 to 15 pinnae, with pinnules bearing two–three pairs per pinna. The leaflets are narrowly lanceolate to elliptical (0.3–3 cm wide, 4.5–5 cm long), cuneate at the base, long-acuminate at the apex, and irregularly dentate to laciniate along the margins. Leaf coloration ranges from deep green in shade-grown specimens to yellowish hues or variegated forms in sun-exposed or horticultural varieties (<xref rid="molecules-30-03460-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>) [<xref rid="B28-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. The intricate leaf morphology and variable coloring enhance its visual distinctiveness and ornamental appeal [<xref rid="B1-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="B2-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>].</p><p>The inflorescences are terminal or axillary panicles (10–30 cm) composed of numerous umbellate cymes (12–20 per inflorescence). Flowers are actinomorphic, hermaphroditic, or functionally male, with articulated pedicels, a ring-like gamosepalous calyx, five deciduous ovate petals (2–4 mm), and five stamens with beige filaments and ovate yellow to white anthers. The inferior ovary comprises two–three carpels with initially fused styles that may separate or twist during fruit maturation (<xref rid="molecules-30-03460-f002" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>) [<xref rid="B29-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. The fruit is a laterally compressed drupe (3–4 mm in diameter), dark purple to black at maturity, containing two flattened pyrenes and a seed with smooth endosperm [<xref rid="B1-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="B2-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>].</p><p>The species belongs to the genus <italic toggle="yes">Polyscias</italic>, originally described by J.R. and G. Forster in 1775. Historically heterogeneous, the genus was redefined by Lowry and Plunkett [<xref rid="B3-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>], who integrated six previously separate genera (<italic toggle="yes">Arthrophyllum</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Gastonia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Reynoldsia</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Cuphocarpus</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Tetraplasandra</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">Munroidendron</italic>) based on molecular phylogenetic data. As a result, the recircumscribed genus now includes approximately 159 accepted species, systematically organized into ten subgenera. <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> is classified within <italic toggle="yes">Polyscias</italic> sensu stricto, a lineage characterized by articulated pedicels, sheathing petiole bases, and a distinct foliar fragrance upon drying [<xref rid="B3-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>].</p><p>The native range of <italic toggle="yes">P. fruticosa</italic> includes Southeast Asia and western Oceania, from where it has spread through human-mediated cultivation. It is now distributed in Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, the Philippines, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, India, China, and many Pacific and Caribbean islands, including Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands [<xref rid="B2-molecules-30-03460" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. In Cuba, the plant has been cultivated since at least the late 19th century and is commonly used in urban landscaping as a hedge or potted ornamental.
