Several other plants were also reported used for this indication; <italic toggle="yes">Stylosanthes erecta</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 12), <italic toggle="yes">Mitragyna inermis</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 12), <italic toggle="yes">Opilia amentacea</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 7), <italic toggle="yes">Vepris heterophylla</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 6), <italic toggle="yes">Trichilia emetica</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 5), <italic toggle="yes">Combretum glutinosum</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3) and <italic toggle="yes">Parkia biglobosa</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3). The use of these specific plant preparations is highly consistent with findings in a previous study where TPs in Mali were interviewed about which medicinal plants they used to treat symptoms of malaria during pregnancy [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. Nordeng et al. reported a high consensus for <italic toggle="yes">Combretum micranthum</italic> (leaves), <italic toggle="yes">Trichilia emetica</italic> (leaves or root), <italic toggle="yes">Vepris heterophylla</italic> (leaves), <italic toggle="yes">Lippia chevalieri</italic> (leaves or root) and <italic toggle="yes">Parkia biglobosa</italic> (stem bark, leaf or fruit) against malaria [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>]. These plants have also previously been reported used traditionally in Africa against malaria or fever, but research on their potential biological activities is limited. Decoction of roots or leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Combretum micranthum</italic> is widely used in Africa as an antipyretic [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>], which may explain its use against symptoms of malaria. However, results from in vitro studies on antimalaria activities are conflicting [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>], and there are no studies that elucidate any potential effects on a pregnancy. In vitro studies of antimalarial activities of <italic toggle="yes">Mitragyna inermis</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Opilia amentacea</italic> encourage further investigations [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref>]. Leaves and roots from <italic toggle="yes">T. emetica</italic> is extensively used in Africa as an antipyretic and as a remedy to alleviate pain [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>]. In vitro studies suggest that <italic toggle="yes">T. emetica</italic> has anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref>], but the results from in vitro studies on potential antimalarial activities are conflicting [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>]. Toxicity data are limited, but water extracts of the roots have shown toxicity against the brine shrimp larvae [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>]. A kurubasch aldehyde from <italic toggle="yes">T. emetica</italic> inhibited growth of cancer cell lines [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref>], and one extract exhibited hormonal influences on prostate cancer cells [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>]. Leaves of the plant were found to cause death in laboratory guineapigs due to edema of the lungs, while an aqueous extract did not exhibit cytotoxic effects against brine shrimp [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>]. The potential cytotoxic, antiproliferative and hormonal effects urge for caution, especially concerning the root of <italic toggle="yes">T. emetica</italic>, and the use by pregnant women should be discouraged.</p><p>It is of concern that medicinal plants are used by some pregnant women as the only treatment against malaria. Of the 62 women treating symptoms of malaria with medicinal plants, only eight (12.9 %) had been recommended to do so by a TP and over 80 % based this use on advice from family and friends or used them on their own initiative (82.3 %). No doctor had recommended such use. Africa remains the region with the highest burden of malaria cases and deaths in the world. Malaria during pregnancy often contributes to maternal anemia, premature delivery, and low birth weight, thereby leading to increased child mortality [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>]. Ideally, conventional malaria medicines should be used according to the WHO-guidelines [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>], but for many women in Mali medicinal plants may be the only alternative. In a focus group discussion in Mali pregnant women without money resorted to herbal medicine for the treatment of malaria [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR44">44</xref>].
