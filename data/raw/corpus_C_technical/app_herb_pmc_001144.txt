A statistically significant difference was presumed to exist when <italic toggle="yes">P</italic> was less than 0.05.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec20"><title>Results and discussion</title><sec id="Sec21"><title>Preparation and characterization of ChlE</title><p id="Par25"><italic toggle="yes">D. simplex</italic> is a type of red seaweed that is a member of the Rhodophyta phylum. It is one of the most widely distributed types of red seaweed along the coastlines of the Egyptian Red Sea, and it has demonstrated a variety of active metabolites that have the potential to act as possible therapeutic agents [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref>]. It was reported that the kind of extraction solvent that was used had an impact not only on the chemical composition of these algal metabolites but also on the potential biological uses of these compounds [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref>]. Of these solvents, chloroform extracts of red marine macroalgae, in particular, are one of the natural resources for the production of essential bioactive secondary metabolites.</p><p id="Par26">In this work, chloroform extract (ChlE) with a yield of 1.24% was made by extracting the dried powder of <italic toggle="yes">D. simplex</italic> after defatting it with petroleum ether. This extract was phytochemically screened, and the findings, shown in Table <xref rid="Tab1" ref-type="table">1</xref>, indicated the presence of a variety of bioactive secondary metabolites, including terpenoids, phenolics, alkaloids, sterols, and polysaccharides. It is well known that phenolics and terpenoids possess powerful antioxidant and cytotoxic properties [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR43">43</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>]. Anthraquinones, flavonoids, saponins, and cardiac glycosides, on the other hand, were not present.</p><p id="Par27">Because secondary metabolite isolation and identification is a time-consuming and resource-intensive procedure that requires both human and material resources, we adapted a rapid GC–MS study to perform chemical profiling as part of our research. A GC/MS analysis indicated the identification of 39 compounds, all of which belonged to various kinds of valuable and variable chemical classes. Table <xref rid="Tab2" ref-type="table">2</xref> summarizes the following information about these active metabolites: their names, retention times, molecular weights, molecular formulas, and area percentage. These bioactive compounds account for 87.48% of the total peak area. The oxygenated hydrocarbons were by far the most prevalent, accounting for 48.17% of the total compounds found, and these included esters (15.82%), ketones (4.96%), fatty acids (21.15%), and sterols (6.24%). The compounds that make up 37.02% of the total identified ones were hydrocarbons [straight (22.61%) and branched (13.32%) chains] and terpenoids (1.09%). There were also 2.29% of miscellaneous compounds. The primary fatty acid metabolite found in ChlE of <italic toggle="yes">D. simplex</italic> was 9-octadecenoic acid (oleic, omega-9 fatty acid) (16.47%). 2,3-Dihydroxypropyl (<italic toggle="yes">Z</italic>)-octadec-9-enoate (8.07%), 4-hydroxydecan-5-one (4.55%), cholesterol benzoate (3.57%), and fucosterol (2.67%) were found as the prevalent components in the other chemical classes. Previous research, including GC/MS analysis of seaweed extracts, supported similar findings [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>].</p><p id="Par28">An FTIR investigation of a <italic toggle="yes">D. simplex</italic> ChlE was carried out to get some insight into the possible existence of significant functional groups of bioactive chemicals in this extract.
