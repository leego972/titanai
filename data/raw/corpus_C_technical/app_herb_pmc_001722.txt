Bioactivities of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> Extracts and Identified Phytochemicals</title><p>In the following sections, we will discuss the biological activities of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> extracts that are important for human health. The bioactivities, including the possible MOA and SAR of the identified compounds, will be discussed to justify the bioactivities exerted by the crude extracts. The SAR described for a specific target can guide the design and synthesis of derivatives with the structural features required for activity.</p><sec id="sec3dot1-molecules-28-04396"><title>3.1. Antioxidant </title><p>The literature indicates that the antioxidant properties of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> crude extracts and isolated compounds have been the most reported compared to other bioactivities due to the accessibility [<xref rid="B38-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>], practicality, and low cost of the assays. Two commonly used assays are in vitro scavenging free radicals 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and 2,2′-azino-bis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) (ABTS). DPPH is a stable free radical that readily accepts an electron or a hydrogen radical from a reducing agent such as vitamin C (ascorbic acid). The reaction results in the formation of stable dehydroascorbate and DPPH-H [<xref rid="B39-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>]. DPPH exhibits a strong absorption band in a methanolic solution with a deep purple colour. The reduction of DPPH radicals has been routinely monitored spectrophotometrically at 517 nm. In those experiments, the colour of the solution gradually decreased as the concentration of the antioxidant increased, wherein DPPH radicals converted to the colourless DPPH-H. </p><p>The antioxidant activities in this discussion were obtained using the DPPH method, wherein vitamin C or butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) was used as a positive control. The plant parts of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> were mainly harvested in Egypt, Tunisia, Saudi Arabia, and Australia. The active extracts were found to be those of the alcoholic extracts. The alcoholic extracts from the bark, leaves, and flowers were shown to have substantial antioxidant activities, which can be ranked from barks&gt; leaves &gt; flowers based on the IC<sub>50</sub> values of the extracts. HPLC-based analysis and bioactive-guided fractionation of the plant extracts revealed polyphenolic and flavonoid derivatives, as shown in <xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>. </p><p>Ghribia et al. [<xref rid="B11-molecules-28-04396" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>] performed sequential polarity-based extraction of a methanolic crude extract of <italic toggle="yes">A. saligna</italic> flowers collected in Tunisia (<xref rid="molecules-28-04396-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>). Dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, <italic toggle="yes">n</italic>-butanol, and aqueous extracts were obtained. Among these four extracts, the ethyl acetate extract was most active in the DPPH assay, with an IC<sub>50</sub> of 67.26 μg/mL. The ethyl acetate extract was subjected to pure compound isolation using column chromatography, which yielded (reported in % <italic toggle="yes">w</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">w</italic> of the dried flowers) isosalipurposide <bold>1</bold> (0.75), quercetin <bold>3</bold> (0.002), and naringenin <bold>42</bold> (trace amounts). Isolated quercetin <bold>3</bold> was screened in the same DPPH assay and was found to be most active, with an IC<sub>50</sub> of 4.58 μg/mL, which was comparable to the positive control reference quercetin’s IC<sub>50</sub> of 4.77 μg/mL. Isosalipurposide <bold>1</bold> was the second most active compound, with an IC<sub>50</sub> of 81.9 μg/mL, which was three times more active than naringenin <bold>42</bold> (255.5 μg/mL). The activities of isosalipurposide <bold>1</bold> and quercetin <bold>3</bold> support the potent antioxidant activity exerted by the ethyl acetate extract.</p><p>The aqueous crude extract of flowers collected in Alexandria, Egypt by Al-Huqail et al.
