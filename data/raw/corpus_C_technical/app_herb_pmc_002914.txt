of the massive research on the identity of Biblical plant names is based on linguistics and philology [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref> and references therein]. Włodarczyk [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>] reviewed “how many plants are mentioned in the Bible” and concluded that the list contains 206 plant names, 95 of which “are recognized by all contemporary researchers of the floras of the Bible.” This discrepancy is not at all surprising since most authors of books on plants of the Bible [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref> a except <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>] were not familiar with Hebrew and/or the Holy Land flora. For example, Duke [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>] enumerates at least 176 species as “Biblical Medicinal Plants” (hence BMPs), while the total number of recognized plants in the Bible is about 100 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>]. Needless to say, too many species of his list are not related at all to the flora in the region and were never grown or traded in the ancient Middle East. Jacob [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>] listed 55 plants (most on a species level but some on a genus level) as BMPs, based on a comparison to Ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian literature.</p><p id="Par6">Amar [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>] revised the flora of the Bible, based particularly on old Jewish post-Biblical sources and their succession down the generations.
