However, the specific components responsible for these anti-obesity and anti-diabetic effects require further investigation. While most studies on amaranth’s metabolic benefits have been conducted in vitro or in animal models, human clinical trials are essential to validate these findings [<xref rid="B141-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">141</xref>]. In human studies, daily intake of 18 mL of amaranth oil for three weeks was found to significantly lower total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL, and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) cholesterol levels [<xref rid="B149-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">149</xref>]. Elevated LDL levels above 130 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol below 35 mg/dL, and total blood cholesterol levels above 200 mg/dL are considered indicators of heightened cardiovascular risk. Amaranth seeds, similar to quinoa, contain high-quality polyunsaturated fatty acids, lutein, and tocopherols, which may contribute to cardiovascular health benefits, although further studies are needed to confirm these effects in human populations [<xref rid="B150-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">150</xref>,<xref rid="B151-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">151</xref>].</p><p>This comprehensive health profile underscores the potential of amaranth as a key ingredient for the development of functional foods and nutraceuticals aimed at preventing chronic diseases and improving overall health [<xref rid="B142-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">142</xref>].</p><p>As shown in <xref rid="nutrients-17-01749-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>, the amaranth findings reveal significant anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, and anticancer properties. Bioactive Compounds in <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic></title><sec id="sec8dot1-nutrients-17-01749"><title>8.1. Polyphenols</title><p><italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> spp. are a rich source of polyphenolic compounds, with significant variations in composition, concentration, and bioactivity across different species and plant parts. The application of advanced analytical techniques such as LC-HRMS, UHPLC-ESI-MS/MS, HPLC-DAD-ESI-MS/MS, and <sup>1</sup>H NMR-based metabolomics has allowed for the precise identification, quantification, and characterization of these bioactive molecules.</p><p>The polyphenol content in <italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic> seeds cultivated in Southern Italy was analyzed using LC-HRMS, identifying 14 different polyphenols with ellagic acid (16,271.86 ± 19,798.53 μg/Kg) as the most abundant, followed by apigenin (2749.51 ± 889.95 μg/Kg). These findings revealed that environmental factors influence polyphenol variability [<xref rid="B152-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">152</xref>]. Similarly, an analysis of <italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic> using <sup>1</sup>H NMR and UHPLC-ESI-MS/MS identified flavonoids and phenolic acids. A comparative analysis with other legumes emphasized the unique polyphenolic profile of <italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic>, further supporting its role as a functional food ingredient [<xref rid="B153-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">153</xref>].</p><p>The seed coat of <italic toggle="yes">L. angustifolius</italic> was investigated using HPLC-DAD-ESI-MS/MS, revealing a high polyphenol concentration comparable to or exceeding other common sources of polyphenols. This study reinforces the nutritional and functional relevance of the lupin seed coat in food applications [<xref rid="B154-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">154</xref>]. Additionally, <italic toggle="yes">L. angustifolius</italic> seeds exhibited the highest total polyphenol content (696.21 mg GAE/100 g) among four <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus</italic> spp., followed by <italic toggle="yes">L. albus</italic> (614.13 mg GAE/100 g), <italic toggle="yes">L. luteus</italic> (467.78 mg GAE/100 g), and <italic toggle="yes">L. mutabilis</italic> (367.36 mg GAE/100 g) [<xref rid="B155-nutrients-17-01749" ref-type="bibr">155</xref>].</p><p>In <italic toggle="yes">Lupinus luteus</italic> germinated seeds produced by Plantico (Waganiec, Poland), apigenin and fisetin were identified as the dominant flavonoids.
