Globally, such assessments emphasize integrating ethnobotanical knowledge with livelihood strategies and ecological management to ensure sustainable use of medicinal and multipurpose plants [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec39"><title>Comparison with previous ethnobotanical studies using the Jaccard similarity index</title><p id="Par226">Comparing the MPs of the Karamara forest patches with previous ethnobotanical studies using the Jaccard Similarity Index (JI) highlights both shared and unique aspects of traditional knowledge. The highest similarity was observed with studies from Jigjiga town and Jigjiga district, reflecting strong concordance in species composition among geographically and culturally proximate Somali communities. This overlap likely results from shared ecological conditions, plant availability, and cultural practices, consistent with other studies of ethnic-specific medicinal plant use in Ethiopia [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>].</p><p id="Par227">Moderate similarity with regions such as Guraferda, Yeki, Armachiho, Bita, and Asagirt indicates partial overlap in plant use. These variations may arise from differences in ecology, accessibility, and cultural practices. Similar trends are reported in Kenya and Nigeria, where species overlap is shaped by local flora, cultural preferences, and traditional healthcare practices [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR110">110</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR112">112</xref>].</p><p id="Par228">Lower similarity indices (&lt; 20%) with areas including Quarit, Ticho, Sodo, and Dawuro highlight the distinctiveness of the Karamara flora and the specialized ethnobotanical knowledge of the Somali ethnic group. Globally, similar patterns emerge where ecological heterogeneity, floristic diversity, and cultural traditions generate unique medicinal plant assemblages, as documented in India, Pakistan, China, Europe, and the Americas [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR93">93</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR94">94</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR108">108</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR109">109</xref>].</p><p id="Par229">The Jaccard analysis highlights the influence of geographic proximity, cultural affiliation, and ecological similarity on medicinal plant knowledge. High similarity among neighboring Somali communities supports targeted pharmacological research and conservation planning, while low similarity with distant regions points to opportunities for discovering underutilized or novel species. This approach aligns with global ethnobotanical practices, where similarity indices are valuable for assessing cross-cultural and ecological patterns in medicinal plant use [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR70">70</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR106">106</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec40"><title>Indigenous knowledge transfer and medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups</title><p id="Par230">Independent t-tests revealed significant differences in medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups in the Karamara forest patches.
