Because the leading causes of blindness in Australia are age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), diabetic retinopathy (DR) and glaucoma,<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0013" ref-type="ref">13</xref> the application of OCT to these diseases forms the focus of this manuscript. Through illustrative cases of patients seen at the Centre for Eye Health,<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0014" ref-type="ref">14</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0015" ref-type="ref">15</xref> we describe both the advantages and limitations of its use. We also emphasise the importance of an evidence‐based, multimodal imaging approach to eye care. Finally, we discuss the role of OCT as a diagnostic versus a screening test. Research‐related variations on OCT, not yet widely available in clinical practice, including polarisation‐sensitive OCT, projection‐resolved OCT‐A or multi‐directional OCT, will not be discussed.</p><p>All case images herein were captured under a standardised testing protocol (varying by the eye condition in question) combining: monoscopic and stereoscopic fundus photography (Kowa WX 3D non‐mydriatic retinal camera, Kowa, Tokyo, Japan), Optomap ultra‐widefield and fundus autofluorescence imaging (Optomap Panoramic 200Tx, Optos, Dunfermline, Scotland, UK), OCT (Cirrus HD‐OCT, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, California, USA and/or Spectralis HRA2 + OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) with scan spacing and density at the discretion of the examining clinician, and standard automated perimetry (Humphrey visual field analyzer, Carl Zeiss Meditec). Patient written consent was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by a Biomedical Human Research Ethics Advisory Panel of the University of New South Wales, Australia.</p></sec><sec id="cxo12847-sec-0003"><title>Age‐related macular degeneration</title><sec id="cxo12847-sec-0004"><title>Early to intermediate AMD</title><p>A current clinical classification scale<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0016" ref-type="ref">16</xref> recommends subdividing AMD into three stages: early, intermediate and late. Large drusen (&gt; 125 μm in diameter) and pigmentary abnormalities define the intermediate stage, while macular atrophy and/or neovascularisation marks the conversion to late‐stage disease (Figure <xref rid="cxo12847-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1</xref>A). Using OCT, drusen in intermediate AMD typically appear as nodular elevations of the RPE/Bruch's membrane complex with medium internal reflectivity.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0017" ref-type="ref">17</xref> Pigmentary abnormalities, on the other hand, usually coincide with hyper‐reflective foci in the outer retina and variations in RPE thickness.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0018" ref-type="ref">18</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0019" ref-type="ref">19</xref> The overlying outer nuclear layer and other photoreceptor layers (external limiting membrane and the ellipsoid zone) often exhibit associated attenuation and thinning. Scrutiny of the photoreceptor layers is important and may be considered in conjunction with drusen height, ultrastructure, area and volume as predictive ‘biomarkers’ of disease progression.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0020" ref-type="ref">20</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0021" ref-type="ref">21</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0022" ref-type="ref">22</xref> For example, subretinal drusenoid deposits confer a 2–6‐fold higher risk of progression to late AMD.<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0023" ref-type="ref">23</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0024" ref-type="ref">24</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0025" ref-type="ref">25</xref> Choroidal thickness is also garnering increasing interest and may hold prognostic value for identifying patients who have or are at risk of developing AMD‐related macular atrophy (particularly if the subfoveal choroidal thickness is ≤ 124 μm).<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0026" ref-type="ref">26</xref> Using OCT‐A, non‐neovascular AMD may present with large areas of signal void and reduced flow signal in the choriocapillaris (due to flow velocities below the decorrelation threshold and/or decreased choriocapillaris vessel calibre and density).<xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0027" ref-type="ref">27</xref>, <xref rid="cxo12847-bib-0028" ref-type="ref">28</xref>

Drusen or elevations of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/Bruch's membrane complex, hyper‐reflective foci in the outer retina and subretinal drusenoid deposits are key risk factors for progression, typical of intermediate AMD, and may be identified using OCT data acquired from a single patient attendance.
