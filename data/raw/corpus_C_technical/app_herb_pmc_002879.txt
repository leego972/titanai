For example, leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Casuarina junghuhniana</italic> was used for psychological disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>] and its stem sap for diabetes mellitus [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>]; <italic toggle="yes">Curcuma purpurascens</italic> rhizome was used for liver disease [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>] and for asthma [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>]; and stem and leaves of <italic toggle="yes">Cryptocarya massoy</italic> was reported for fracture-related conditions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref>] as well as coordination disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>]. Collectively, these examples underscore that, alongside a widely shared medicinal plants use, Indonesia’s ethnopharmacological landscape contains culturally patterned plant–indication–part-used associations that likely arise from the interaction of local ecology, knowledge transmission, and culturally specific health priorities.</p></sec><sec id="Sec14"><title>Plants with high versatility across various ailments</title><p id="Par37">our analysis finds that <italic toggle="yes">Zingiber officinale</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Curcuma longa</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Carica papaya</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Morinda citrifolia</italic>,<italic toggle="yes"> Moringa oleifera</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Orthosiphon aristatus</italic> emerge as highly versatile taxa that are repeatedly cited in studies from different islands and used to treat a wide spectrum of complaints, including metabolic, digestive, infectious and general symptoms. Their wide spatial and therapeutic spread suggests that communities perceive these species as reliable, multi-purpose remedies, making them strong candidates for pharmacological prioritisation, safety assessment and nutraceutical development. This pattern of a shared, high-consensus medicinal plants align closely with ethnobotanical observations from other parts of Asia and Africa. In South Africa, an appraisal of antidiabetic plants in KwaZulu-Natal similarly highlights a subset of culturally important species including <italic toggle="yes">Moringa oleifera</italic> and other widely known species that dominate local treatment and are now being investigated phytochemically to bridge indigenous knowledge with modern healthcare [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR138">138</xref>].</p><p id="Par38">Our analysis in most frequently reported ailments mapped to ICPC-2 categories reveals nuanced patterns in medicinal plant use, distinguishing between conditions with clear species dominance and those with heterogeneous treatment options. For gastrointestinal and digestive complaints, surveys from Algeria and India also report that, despite numerous plants being used, a limited number of taxa carry most of the use-reports for diarrhoea and other gut or hepato-renal disorders [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR122">122</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR139">139</xref>]. In contrast, for chronic and highly prevalent conditions such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and cough, the review revealed no single dominant species; leading plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Apium graveolens</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Muntingia calabura</italic> contributed only a small fraction of use-reports and appeared in a minority of relevant studies. Comparable patterns have been described in Mindanao, where region-specific species contribute disproportionately to local identity and may include threatened or endemic taxa requiring urgent conservation attention [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>].</p><p id="Par39">Taken together, this systematic review synthesises ethnobotanical data on medicinal plant use across Indonesia using a standard clinical system (ICPC-2) and appraisal of methodological quality. By harmonising 3,202 use-reports for 791 species from 73 studies, the review moves beyond simple species inventories to identify geographically widespread species, region- and ethnicity-specific plants, and priority plants for ailments.
