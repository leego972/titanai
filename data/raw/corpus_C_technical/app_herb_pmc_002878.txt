Similarly, national ethnobotanical research record that much of the literature remains largely descriptive, with limited use of probabilistic sampling, standardized diagnostic categories or advanced quantitative analysis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR126">126</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR127">127</xref>]. Deliberative methodologies such as Focus Group Discussions (FGD) or Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) were rare, indicating that Indonesian ethnobotany remains largely extractive rather than participatory [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR95">95</xref>].</p><p id="Par34">Our appraisal also reveals substantial weaknesses in taxonomic validation and use of ethnobotanical indices within Indonesian ethnobotanical research. Several studies relied on literature-based identification, citing regional floras such as Flora of Java or pharmacognostic texts like Atlas Tumbuhan Obat [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR58">58</xref>], cross-referencing global databases (e.g., POWO, GBIF) or using image-based tools like PlantNet [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR75">75</xref>]. This level of uncertainty directly undermines the reproducibility of pharmacological and phytochemical research, where precise species identity is essential for toxicity assessment, dose standardisation, and conservation planning [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR128">128</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR129">129</xref>]. Ethnobotanical studies from Morocco likewise stress voucher-based identification yet note that many surveys in still depend heavily on vernacular names and do not consistently report their taxonomic procedures, creating ambiguity when different local names refer to the same species or, conversely, when the same name is used for multiple species [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR130">130</xref>]. The implications of weak taxonomic foundations extend well beyond field surveys. Reviews of African medicinal plants emphasise that misidentification or taxonomic ambiguity at the ethnobotanical stage can propagate errors into phytochemical databases thereby reducing the reliability of candidate selection and wasting scarce research resources [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR131">131</xref>].</p><p id="Par35">The situation is similar for quantitative ethnobotanical indices. The global literature shows that rich qualitative descriptions of ailments and plant uses are rarely matched by systematic mapping to international disease taxonomies or by fully transparent quantitative analysis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR132">132</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR135">135</xref>]. Even when indices such as Use Value or Informant Consensus Factor are calculated, they are not always accompanied by clear definitions, denominators or measures of uncertainty, which constrains comparability across sites [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR125">125</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR136">136</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="Sec13"><title>Geographical distribution and cross ethnicity prominence</title><p id="Par36">Analysis of cross-cultural distribution revealed that 239 species were recorded across multiple provinces and ethnic groups highlights the widespread utilization of medicinal plants such as <italic toggle="yes">Zingiber officinale</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Piper betle</italic> across diverse provincial landscapes. In contrast to these widely shared medicinal plants, a distinct subset of species was found to be tightly linked to specific regions and ethnic groups. These highly localized species exemplify region- and ethnicity-specific ethnomedicinal practices, which may reflect unique therapeutic strategies and biocultural adaptations. For example, among the Dayak Meratus in South Kalimantan, <italic toggle="yes">Tristaniopsis</italic> sp. was reported for mouth/tongue/lip disease using water from the stem [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR74">74</xref>] and separately for hematuria using the bark [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>]. In the same ethnoecological context, roots of <italic toggle="yes">Artocarpus odoratissimus</italic> was used for abdominal pain or cramps [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>] and its leaves for uncomplicated hypertension [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR95">95</xref>].
