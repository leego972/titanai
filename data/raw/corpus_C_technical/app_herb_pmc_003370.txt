We documented new medicinal uses for several plants listed in the regional and local pharmacopoeias as well as for plants not previously reported in an urban environment. In general, there is little information about the types of plants used for medicinal purposes in urban ecosystems in Costa Rica. Although the country has a high endemic diversity of plants, many exotic medicinal plant species were introduced by the Spaniards during the colonization and by Afro-Costa Rican descendants. The present results thus show how the diversity of the medicinal plants used by these garden owners' confirms a socioeconomic gradient and reflects both Costa Rica’s colonial history as well as the current epidemiological profile of the country. 2022</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1"><title>Background</title><p id="Par5">There is a growing attention in ethnobiological literature to what Nabhan has called the ethnobiosphere in urban environments [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>]. Urban home or domestic gardens are important indicators of medicinal plant use in the city [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR3">3</xref>] and are therefore important sites to understand urban health practices and biocultural knowledge transfers [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>]. Medicinal plants form an important component of global health care as they are utilized as a main source of treatment in many developing countries and increasingly also in the global north [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>]. Latin-America forms no exception to this trend, medicinal plants are widely employed in home medicine and phytotherapy in rural areas, often by indigenous and mestizo communities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>], and in general, medicinal treatments form the second most important use category of local floras across the continent [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>].</p><p id="Par6">Urban ethnobotanical studies in Latin-America have so far mainly focused on (i) market studies [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>] documenting the diversity of medicinal plant species sold, (ii) the dynamics of ethnobotanical practices of transnational and internal migrant communities [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>] and to a lesser extent on (iii) urban home gardens.
