Her cabinet was considered one of the most important natural history collections in Europe in the eighteenth century [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>]. The other ones (21%) worked in their countries, in particular Marie Le Masson Le Golft (1749–1826), whose work focused on marine biology; she published many books, including Coup d'Oeil Sur l'État Ancien et Présent du Havre (1778), with notes on fauna and flora of a French harbor [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR88">88</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR89">89</xref>].</p><p id="Par29">Ten of them traveled and/or published works with their husbands (35%). However, some of these women persisted traveling after their husbands’ death, such as Ottile Coudreau (1870–1910), who assumed control of an expedition in the Amazon region, publishing alone the results [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR92">92</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR96">96</xref>]. Eight of these female naturalists (25%) traveled by their own pursuits, such as Ida Pfeiffer (1795–1858) who traveled during 15 years around the world, coming to Brazil in 1846. She carried a letter of recommendation from Humboldt and other naturalists, helping her with her expeditions; she published many books that were translated to several languages [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR72">72</xref>]. Dorothea Maria Gsell (1678–1743), in particular, did not travel with her husband and neither traveled by her own pursuit, but traveled as an assistant of her mother, Maria Sibylla Merian. Later, she became professor of Arts in the Saint Petersburg’s Academy of Art and counselor of arts acquisition of Czar Peter. Also, she became administrator of Czar Peter’s natural history collection [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR62">62</xref>].</p><p id="Par30">Six of them (21%) where traveler writers who made observations about plants in their publications, such as Baroness E. de Langsdorff (1812–1889), who came to Brazil in 1842 with the aim of dealing with the marriage of Prince of Joinville and Princess Francisca, Brazilian Emperor D. Pedro I’s sister; Langsdorff published her observations on landscapes and plants in a journal [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR56">56</xref>]. Teresa von Bayern, Princess of Bayern (1850–1925) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR100">100</xref>] and Maria Graham (1785–1842) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR80">80</xref>] collected plants that were included in <italic toggle="yes">Flora Brasiliensis</italic> of the German naturalist Carl Friedrich Philipp von Martius [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR101">101</xref>].</p><p id="Par31">Most interestingly is to note that the trajectory of every one of these women is really admirable. Adela Breton (1849–1923), for example, was an archeologist, explorer and artist. She registered an archeological site in Mexico, including observations on landscape and vegetation. Her work is recognized as of great importance for Mesoamerican studies. Breton also made many watercolors of plants native to Central America, mainly Mexico [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>]. Jeanne Barret (1740–1897) traveled disguised as a man, being the first woman ever to travel around the world in a scientific expedition. She was the botanist assistant of Philibert Commerson [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR77">77</xref>]. Marianne North (1830–1890) carried out many scientific expeditions aimed at illustrating plants in their natural environment, illustrating more than 700 plant genres. Some were totally unknown and were named after her, such as <italic toggle="yes">Northia seychellana</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Nepenthes northiana</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Crinum nothianum</italic>, the last one was described based on her drawings [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR82">82</xref>, <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR83">83</xref>].</p><p id="Par32">However, something in common is the ethnobotanical data in their works, which still did not receive necessary attention.</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>The case of Maria Sibylla Merian</title><sec id="Sec10"><title>Maria Sibylla’s erasure mechanisms</title><p id="Par33">Maria Sibylla Merian's trajectory draws a lot of attention, although her scientific relevance has been neglected during the last centuries, mainly outside Europe.
