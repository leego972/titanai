<styled-content style="fixed-case" toggle="no">OCTA</styled-content> Findings in Inflammatory Optic Neuropathy</title><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0008"><label>3.1</label><title>Multiple Sclerosis Associated Optic Neuropathy</title><p>Optic neuritis is the initial manifestation of MS in 25% of patients, with 75% of individuals with a diagnosis of MS experiencing at least a single episode of optic neuritis at some point in the natural history of their disease [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0054" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>]. Optic neuritis typically manifests with retro‐orbital pain, reduced visual acuity, reduced colour vision, relative afferent pupil defect, and normal optic disc on fundus examination, but occasionally a hyperaemic disc oedema is seen if the inflammation affects the nerve anterior to the lamina cribosa [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0055" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>]. Following an episode of optic neuritis, which may be subclinical, retrograde axonal degeneration of the ganglion cell of the optic nerve occurs. The optic disc may appear pale clinically, most commonly in the temporal aspect and associated with thinning of the RNFL on OCT cross‐section imaging [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0056" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>]. Gradual recovery of optic nerve function occurs, although residual deficits persist in the majority of individuals [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0056" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>]. It is apparent that parafoveal and peripapillary capillary density positively correlates with inner retinal thickness independent of other ocular variables. It is theorised that microvascular changes occur secondarily to this neuroaxonal degeneration because of reduced ganglion energy demand signal [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0057" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>]. One study found that superficial macular vascular plexus was reduced in the central and superior sectors in individuals with MS when compared to healthy subjects regardless of whether there was a concomitant diagnosis of optic neuritis [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0058" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>]. Another study found that optic disc capillary density and RPC density were reduced in individuals with MS when compared to healthy subjects regardless of whether there is a concomitant diagnosis of optic neuritis [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0059" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>]. While other investigators have found this reduction in peripapillary capillary density to be significantly reduced in those with MS and concomitant optic neuritis comparative to those without a previous diagnosis of optic neuritis [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0060" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>]. The variability may be due to the severity and duration of optic neuritis. In optic neuritis, the myelin sheath surrounding the optic nerve is damaged first. The neuronal loss and capillary dropout depend on the severity and the location in the retrobulbar location of the optic nerve. Regardless, superficial capillary plexus vascular density and RPC vascular density positively correlate to RNFL thickness and RGC count, which in turn correlate with the degree of morbidity and disease duration [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0059" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>]. Furthermore, ONH vascular density and RPC density positively correlated with the severity of optic nerve pathology on VEP and subject disability score in individuals with MS, supporting the use of OCTA as a measure of optic nerve damage and a marker of disease severity [<xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0061" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0062" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>, <xref rid="ceo14544-bib-0063" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="ceo14544-sec-0009"><label>3.2</label><title>Other Inflammatory Optic Neuropathy</title><p>The discovery of biomarkers, Aquaporin‐4 antibody (AQP4‐IgG) and Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein antibody (MOG‐IgG), has resulted in a paradigm shift in the understanding of inflammatory optic neuropathies.
