PART 1: SOCIAL ENGINEERING FUNDAMENTALS
================================================================================

--- CIALDINI'S PRINCIPLES OF INFLUENCE (APPLIED TO ATTACKS) ---

RECIPROCITY: People return favors.
  Attack: "I just helped your colleague with their IT issue. Now I need your password reset."
  Defense: Policies that don't reward unauthorized requests regardless of prior help.

COMMITMENT/CONSISTENCY: People follow through on prior commitments.
  Attack: "You agreed earlier that security is important, so you'll verify your credentials now, right?"
  Foot-in-the-door: Small ask first → larger ask later. First "yes" makes second easier.
  Defense: Recognize commitment escalation patterns. Any escalation to sensitive info = red flag.

SOCIAL PROOF: People follow what others do.
  Attack: "Everyone in your department has already updated their credentials."
  Defense: Verify claims independently. Don't assume "everyone" means policy changed.

AUTHORITY: People obey perceived authority.
  Attack: Impersonate CEO, IT director, auditor, government official.
  Spear phishing: Email appearing from CEO with urgent wire transfer request.
  Defense: Verbal confirmation via known phone number for any sensitive requests.

LIKING: People comply with those they like.
  Attack: Build rapport over time. LinkedIn → fake connection → months of "relationship" building.
  Vishing: Friendly, helpful tone disarms suspicion.
  Defense: Awareness that attackers exploit friendliness.

SCARCITY: Urgency/limited time increases compliance.
  Attack: "Your account will be locked in 30 minutes unless you verify NOW."
  Classic: Fake expiring security alerts, invoice deadlines, urgent executive requests.
  Defense: Urgency is a red flag. Slow down. Verify through separate channel.

--- ATTACK CYCLE ---

1. RECONNAISSANCE:
  OSINT: LinkedIn (org chart, roles, names), company website, job postings (reveals tech stack),
  social media (personal details for pretexting), public records, domain registration, data breaches.
  
  Tools: Maltego, theHarvester, SpiderFoot, recon-ng, LinkedIn Sales Navigator.
  
  What to collect:
  Employee names and roles (for impersonation targets).
  Organizational structure (who reports to whom).
  Technology in use (vendor names, software — for IT impersonation).
  Current events (merger, layoffs, audit, new software rollout — pretexting hooks).
  Email format: firstname.lastname@company.com, f.lastname@company.com, etc.

2. PRETEXTING:
  Build believable cover story. Consistent identity.
  Pretext examples:
  New employee: "Hi, I just joined the IT department. I'm having trouble accessing the system."
  Vendor: "I'm calling from Microsoft/Cisco/your software vendor. We detected an issue with your account."
  IT auditor: "We're performing a mandatory security assessment. I need to verify your access credentials."
  Executive assistant: "I'm calling on behalf of CFO John Smith. He needs an urgent wire transfer processed."

3. EXECUTION:
  Deliver the attack via chosen vector (email, phone, in-person).

4. EXTRACTION:
  Obtain goal: Credentials, money, access, information.

5. EXIT:
  Leave no trace. Maintain cover story if follow-up checks occur.

PART 2: SPEAR PHISHING
================================================================================

--- SPEAR PHISHING ANATOMY ---

Spear phishing: Targeted phishing using personal/organizational context.
Mass phishing: Generic. Low success. High volume.
Spear phishing: Personalized. High success rate. Low volume. Used by APTs and sophisticated criminals.

EMAIL COMPONENTS FOR MAXIMUM EFFECTIVENESS:

FROM address:
  Lookalike domain: company.com → company-it.com, cornpany.com (rn looks like m), company.co.
  Display name spoofing: "IT Security Team <attacker@attacker.com>" — display name trusted, domain ignored.
  Subdomain: security@mail.company.attacker.com (looks legitimate at a glance).
  Legitimate but compromised: Actual legitimate account used to send phishing.

SUBJECT LINE:
  High-open rate subjects (real data from phishing simulations):
  "Password Expiry Notice"
  "Unusual sign-in activity"
  "Shared document: Q4 Financial Results [name].xlsx"
  "Action Required: Verify your account"
  "DocuSign: [CEO Name] signed a document"
  "Your recent order has shipped"
  Personalized: Include target's name, company, manager's name.

BODY:
  Match company's email style (fonts, logo, footer, disclaimer language).
  Reference real people, real projects, real current events.
  One clear action: "Click here to verify" or "Sign document" or "Review attachment."
  Urgency: "Within 24 hours" or "Before end of business today."

PAYLOAD OPTIONS:
  1. Credential harvest: Link → fake login page. Submit = captured credentials.
  2. Malware: Attachment (Word macro, LNK file, ISO with executable).
  3. Business Email Compromise (BEC): No link/attachment. Social engineering only. Wire transfer fraud.

--- GOINGPHISH / GOPHISH CAMPAIGN SETUP ---

GoPhish: Open-source phishing framework.

Setup:
  Sender Profile: Configure SMTP relay. SPF/DKIM aligned for maximum delivery.
  Email template: HTML email matching target's expected communications.
  Landing page: Cloned credential harvest page. JS tracker.
  Group: Target list with email, first name, last name, position.
  Campaign: Combine above. Launch. Track: Sent, Opened, Clicked, Submitted Data.

Tracking pixels:
  1x1 transparent GIF with unique ID. Loaded on email open.
  Records: Email opened, timestamp, IP address, user agent.
  Tells attacker: Target exists, email client used, approximate location.

--- CREDENTIAL HARVESTING PAGES ---

EvilGinx2: Advanced phishing framework. Real-time credential and session token theft.
  Proxies: Real target website. Man-in-the-browser.
  Victim: Sees real website (not clone). Enters real credentials.
  EvilGinx: Captures credentials AND session cookies.
  Bypasses MFA: Gets valid post-MFA session. Replays session. No MFA needed again.
  
  Setup:
  sudo ./evilginx -developer   # Test mode
  phishlets hostname office365 attacker-domain.com
  phishlets enable office365
  lures create office365
  lures get-url 0   # Get phishing URL to send to victim

PART 3: BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE (BEC)
================================================================================

--- BEC ANATOMY ---

BEC: Most financially damaging cybercrime. $2.9B losses in 2023 (FBI IC3).
No malware. Pure social engineering. Hard to detect technically.

BEC CATEGORIES (FBI):

Type 1 — CEO Fraud:
  Attacker impersonates CEO (email domain lookalike or compromised account).
  Emails CFO/finance team: "I need a wire transfer to [vendor]. It's for a confidential acquisition. Don't discuss with anyone."
  Urgency + authority + secrecy = high success.

Type 2 — Vendor Invoice Fraud:
  Compromise vendor's email or spoof it.
  Send to company: "Our banking details have changed. Please update and send payment to new account."
  Company updates vendor payment details to attacker's account.
  Next legitimate invoice: Paid to attacker.

Type 3 — Real Estate Wire Fraud:
  Intercept real estate transaction email chains.
  Moment before closing: "Wire transfer instructions have changed. Use these new bank details."
  Buyer: Wires purchase price to attacker. Irreversible. Devastating.

Type 4 — W-2 Phishing:
  Impersonate CEO/CFO. Request W-2 forms for all employees from HR.
  Use W-2 data for: Tax fraud, identity theft.

--- BEC DEFENSE ---

Technical:
  DMARC p=reject: Prevents domain spoofing. Mandatory for all domains.
  Email authentication: SPF + DKIM + DMARC all properly configured.
  Display name alerts: Banner when email display name matches known exec but domain doesn't match.
  External email labels: "EXTERNAL" banner on all emails from outside domain.

Process:
  Callback verification: Any wire transfer or banking change → callback to known number (not from email).
  Two-person authorization: Any wire over $X requires two approvers.
  Verbal confirmation: CFO personally approves large wire transfers.
  Anti-fraud callback on any banking detail changes.

Training:
  Simulate BEC attacks on finance team.
  Specific training on wire fraud scenarios.
  Positive reinforcement: "Well caught!" when suspicious email reported.

PART 4: VISHING (VOICE PHISHING)
================================================================================

--- VISHING TECHNIQUES ---

CALLER ID SPOOFING:
  Call appears from: Internal extension, IT helpdesk number, executive's number.
  Tools: VoIP services that allow caller ID manipulation (legal for legitimate use).
  Services: Spoofcard, SpoofTel, various VoIP providers.
  Legality: Illegal in US to spoof for fraudulent purposes (Truth in Caller ID Act).

PRETEXTING SCRIPTS:

IT helpdesk pretext:
  "Hi, this is [Name] from the IT security team. We've detected a suspicious login to your account from [made-up location]. To protect your account, I need to verify your identity. Can you confirm your employee ID and the last password you used?"
  
  Social proof element: "I've already spoken to [manager's name from LinkedIn] about this."

Microsoft support scam:
  "Hi, this is Microsoft support. We've detected unusual activity on your Windows license. To prevent it from being suspended, we need to remotely access your computer for 15 minutes."
  Leads to: TeamViewer/AnyDesk install → remote access → malware installation.

IRS/government scam:
  "This is the IRS. You owe $X in back taxes. Pay now or face immediate arrest."
  Urgency + authority + fear → victims pay gift cards or wire transfers.

--- VISHING DEFENSE ---

For employees:
  Never give credentials over phone: IT will NEVER ask for your password by phone.
  Verify caller: Hang up, call back via official number you looked up independently.
  Report suspicious calls: Security team wants to know about these.

For organizations:
  Clear policy: IT never asks for passwords by phone.
  Training with simulated vishing calls.
  Callback verification process for sensitive requests.
  Voice authentication for high-risk transactions (banking).

PART 5: SMISHING (SMS PHISHING)
================================================================================

--- SMS ATTACK LANDSCAPE ---

SMS trust: People open >95% of text messages. Higher than email open rates.
SMS authentication bypass: "Your verification code is..." intercepted.

COMMON SMISHING ATTACKS:
  Package delivery scam: "Your package couldn't be delivered. Track it here: [link]"
  Bank fraud alert: "Suspicious activity on your account. Verify here: [link]"
  Free prize: "You've been selected for $1000 Amazon gift card: [link]"
  Account verification: "Unusual sign-in. Verify your identity: [link]"

SIM SWAPPING:
  Attack: Social engineer mobile carrier into transferring victim's phone number to attacker's SIM.
  Process: Call carrier with OSINT-gathered personal info (DOB, last 4 SSN, address).
  Carrier: Transfers number. All SMS now go to attacker.
  Bypass: SMS-based MFA. Attacker receives all 2FA codes.
  
  Notable SIM swap attacks:
  Jack Dorsey (Twitter CEO): Phone number hijacked. Twitter account compromised.
  Michael Terpin: Sued AT&T. $24M in cryptocurrency stolen via SIM swap.
  
  Defense: Use phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2). Never SMS MFA for high-value accounts.
  Carrier PIN: Set PIN on mobile account. Required for any SIM changes.

PART 6: PHISHING DEFENSE PROGRAMS
================================================================================

--- BUILDING A SECURITY AWARENESS PROGRAM ---

PHISHING SIMULATION PROGRAM:
  Tool: KnowBe4, Proofpoint Security Awareness, Cofense, GoPhish (open source).
  
  Baseline: Send initial unannounced simulation to all employees.
  Measure: Click rate, credential submission rate, report rate.
  Industry average click rate: ~32% before training.
  After good training: <5% target.
  
  Ongoing schedule: Monthly simulations with varying difficulty/pretexts.
  Consequence: Not punishment. Education. Failed simulations → immediate training module.
  Reward: Recognition for reporting. "If you see something, say something."
  
  Metrics to track:
  Click rate: % who clicked phishing link
  Submission rate: % who submitted credentials
  Report rate: % who reported to security (SHOULD go up over time)
  Mean time to report: How quickly does someone report? (Faster = better)

TECHNICAL CONTROLS:
  Email authentication: SPF, DKIM, DMARC (all three). DMARC policy = reject.
  Sandbox: Detonate all links and attachments before delivery.
  URL rewriting: Rewrite links → scan at click time (protection against delayed weaponization).
  Anti-spoof display name: Warn when external email matches exec name.
  DKIM fail banner: Large "EXTERNAL EMAIL — verify before clicking" banner.
  Blocked attachment types: .exe, .bat, .vbs, .ps1 never delivered.
  
  Microsoft 365 Defender — Anti-phishing policies:
  Impersonation protection: Flag emails impersonating specific executives.
  Mailbox intelligence: ML on each user's communication patterns.
  Safe Links: Real-time URL scanning at click time.
  Safe Attachments: Cloud sandbox detonation before delivery.

REPORTING INFRASTRUCTURE:
  One-click report button: Outlook "Report Phishing" button.
  Triage: Automatic IOC extraction from reported emails → feed to SIEM.
  Closure: Close the loop — tell reporter what happened. Builds trust and reporting culture.
