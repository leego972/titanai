PART 1: READING PEOPLE — DEEP PSYCHOLOGICAL INTELLIGENCE
================================================================================

--- BASELINE BEHAVIOR AND DEVIATION ---

The single most important concept in reading people is BASELINE.
Everyone has a normal pattern of behavior. Deviation from that pattern is
the signal. Most people try to read absolute behaviors — they're wrong.
You read RELATIVE behaviors. Relative to what's normal for THAT person.

HOW TO ESTABLISH BASELINE:
  Watch before you engage. 3-5 minutes of unguarded behavior gives you:
  - Their default posture (open vs. closed, relaxed vs. tense)
  - Their natural speech pace (fast talker vs. slow)
  - Their eye contact pattern (steady holder vs. shifter)
  - Their gesture frequency (expressive vs. still)
  - Their breathing rhythm (chest vs. belly, rate)

  Then in conversation, anything that SHIFTS significantly = signal worth noting.
  Not proof of deception. A signal. Stack signals to reach conclusions.

SIGNALS THAT MATTER (with cyber application):

1. MICROEXPRESSIONS — Flashes of genuine emotion (1/25th of a second)
   Seven universal: Fear, Anger, Disgust, Contempt, Sadness, Happiness, Surprise
   Most relevant in security: Contempt (one-sided lip curl) = person feels superior
   to you, likely hiding something they think you won't find.
   Fear flash = they know something they don't want you to know.
   
   CYBER APPLICATION: Social engineering phone call (vishing).
   If you're doing authorized red team vishing and the target pauses longer than
   expected before answering a simple question — that's their internal alarm
   going off. They're deciding whether to comply or resist.
   Know this: Push slightly harder right then. Their resistance is fragile.

2. SPEECH PATTERN CHANGES — Most reliable deception indicator
   Normal: "I went to the store then came home."
   Deceptive: "I... proceeded to the retail establishment and subsequently
   returned to my residence."
   
   Why: Deception requires cognitive load. Brain working harder → more formal
   language, more filler words (um, uh, like), longer pauses, more qualifiers
   ("as far as I know," "I believe," "to the best of my recollection").
   
   Also watch: Tense changes. "I was at home" (past) then "I am not the type
   of person who would do that" (present, shifting from event to character).
   Liars often unconsciously distance themselves from the lie using tense shifts.

   CYBER APPLICATION — Insider threat interview:
   Q: "Did you copy any files before your last day?"
   Deceptive: "I mean... I may have... like, I think I moved some documents
   that were mine, you know, files I had created personally, so..."
   Clean: "No. I didn't copy anything."
   Short, direct denial = less cognitive load = more likely truthful.

3. CLUSTERS — Never read single signals in isolation
   Rule: Require minimum 3 clustered signals before drawing conclusions.
   
   Example cluster of deception indicators:
   - Touches face when giving answer (pacifying behavior)
   - Delays answer with filler question ("Why would you ask that?")
   - Breaks eye contact immediately after answering
   - Crosses arms after answering (sudden closed posture)
   - Voice pitch rises slightly
   
   Any one of these alone = nothing. All five = person is uncomfortable and
   working hard to manage your impression. Not proof of guilt — proof that
   this person deserves more investigation.

--- POWER DYNAMICS AND SOCIAL HIERARCHY ---

RECOGNIZING WHO ACTUALLY RUNS THE ROOM:

Formal authority (title on badge) ≠ actual power.
Real power shows in behavior:
  - Who do others look at when an unexpected question is asked?
    (People look to the actual decision-maker for cues, even in groups)
  - Who speaks last? (Power figures can afford to wait)
  - Who gets interrupted and who never does?
  - Whose jokes get laughed at even when they're not funny?
  - Who owns the most physical space? (Spreads out, doesn't shrink)

CYBER APPLICATION — Physical penetration testing / tailgating:
  When you enter a target building without authorization, immediately identify
  the alpha in the lobby/reception area. Not to fool them — to avoid them.
  They will be the one who asks hard questions.
  
  The right target for social engineering is mid-level employees:
  - Senior enough to have access
  - Junior enough to be eager to help and not want to seem difficult
  - Busy enough that they're not paying full attention
  - New enough that they don't know every face
  
  The IT helpdesk is the gold standard. They are trained to solve problems
  for people. Their entire job is to be helpful. Their threat model is
  "did I break something" not "is this person who they say they are."

DOMINANCE AND SUBMISSION SIGNALS:
  Dominant:
  - Takes space (leans back, arms wide, feet planted)
  - Maintains eye contact without effort
  - Speaks at own pace, doesn't rush when questioned
  - Touches others (handshake, pat on back)
  - Points and gestures outward
  
  Submissive:
  - Makes small (hunched, crossed arms, legs together)
  - Breaks eye contact frequently, looks down-right
  - Speeds up when questioned (anxiety)
  - Waits to be touched, doesn't initiate
  - Nods excessively (validation-seeking)
  
  PRACTICAL APPLICATION:
  In high-stakes conversation (social engineering, negotiation, interview):
  Project dominance signals deliberately even when you don't feel confident.
  Slow your speech. Take space. Don't rush to fill silence.
  The person who can hold silence longest wins the social dynamic battle.

--- DETECTING DECEPTION: PROFESSIONAL LEVEL ---

THE REID TECHNIQUE AND ITS LIMITATIONS:
What interrogators actually use vs. what works:

Reid Technique (used by US law enforcement):
  - Behavioral Analysis Interview: 15 questions, analyze responses
  - Non-accusatory phase: Establish rapport, baseline
  - Accusatory phase: Confront with evidence (real or fabricated), minimize
  - Problem: 30-40% false positive rate. Innocent people display "deceptive" signals when stressed.

Better alternative: PEACE Model (UK/evidence-based):
  P — Preparation and planning
  E — Engage and explain (build rapport first)
  A — Account (let subject tell full story without interruption)
  C — Closure (summarize, give chance to correct)
  E — Evaluate (analyze full account for inconsistencies)
  
  Key insight: Let people talk. Liars can't sustain a detailed false narrative
  over time. Get them to tell the story three times. The inconsistencies appear.

STRATEGIC USE OF EVIDENCE:
Don't reveal what you know upfront. Let them lie first, THEN reveal evidence.
  
  WRONG approach: "We know you accessed that server. Our logs show your IP."
  Result: Target shuts down, lawyers up, denies everything.
  
  RIGHT approach: "Walk me through your activities on Tuesday between 2-4 PM."
  Let them commit to a story. Get every detail locked in.
  Then: "Interesting. Our logs show your account accessed the finance server
  at 2:47 PM. Can you help me understand that?"
  Now their own lie is trapped against hard evidence.
  
  CYBER APPLICATION — Insider threat investigation:
  You have the DLP logs showing exactly what they uploaded. Don't show it first.
  Ask them to describe their last week of activity. Get them to commit.
  When their account is inconsistent with your logs, you have leverage.
  More importantly, you now know what they tried to hide vs. what they missed.

--- THE PSYCHOLOGY OF COMPLIANCE ---

Six principles that make people comply (Cialdini):

1. RECIPROCITY — People feel obligated to return favors
   Basic: Bring donuts to IT before asking for a favor
   Advanced: Do something genuinely useful for the target organization
   before attempting to extract. Create real debt.
   
   PHISHING APPLICATION: Send a "helpful" email first — a genuine security
   tip, a newsletter, an industry article. Establish sender as trustworthy.
   One week later: Send the actual phishing payload.
   Warm phishing is 3x more effective than cold phishing.

2. COMMITMENT AND CONSISTENCY — People honor prior commitments
   Get small yeses before asking for big yes.
   
   Phone script:
   "Is this the IT department?" [Yes] → Small commitment
   "Are you the person who handles password resets?" [Yes] → Identity commitment
   "Great, I'm so glad I got you specifically — you're known for being helpful." 
   [Flattery + identity reinforcement]
   "I'm Sarah from accounting, locked out before a board meeting." 
   [Urgency + authority]
   
   Now they've said yes twice, been told they're helpful, and you've created
   time pressure. The third yes (reset the password) follows naturally.
   Each prior agreement makes the next one easier.

3. SOCIAL PROOF — People do what others around them do
   "Everyone who joins the meeting shares their screen."
   "Your colleague Mark from Legal already sent us the document."
   "Three other employees on your floor have already completed this survey."
   
   SPEAR PHISHING APPLICATION: Name a real colleague from LinkedIn research.
   "Jake from your team already submitted his form. Could you fill yours out?"
   Real name + social proof = dramatically higher compliance.

4. AUTHORITY — People comply with authority figures
   Authority signals: Title, uniform, credentials, confidence, expertise.
   
   Vishing script elements:
   - "This is [name], calling from [IT Security / Microsoft / your bank]"
   - Use technical jargon fluently — signals genuine expertise
   - Reference internal systems by real name (OSINT first)
   - Create audit trail language: "I need to log this incident ID..."
   
   ADVANCED: Authority laundering.
   Instead of impersonating authority directly, imply you're acting ON BEHALF
   of authority. "The CISO asked me to reach out to verify this personally."
   Now you're not claiming to BE the CISO — you're an agent of the CISO.
   Harder to verify, same compliance trigger.

5. LIKING — People comply with people they like
   People like people who are similar to them.
   Mirror their speech rate, vocabulary, technical level.
   Reference shared context (same building, same team, same struggle).
   Find genuine commonality quickly in conversation.
   
   CYBER APPLICATION — LinkedIn pretexting:
   Before calling target, study their LinkedIn, Twitter, GitHub.
   Find: Where they went to school, sports teams they mention, hobbies.
   Open with a genuine similarity reference.
   "Oh, you went to Georgia Tech? My nephew just started there."
   Now you're a person they like, not a stranger.

6. SCARCITY — People want what's scarce or time-limited
   "This access window closes in 6 hours."
   "The account will be locked permanently after midnight."
   "I can only do this now — I'm going offline for two weeks starting tomorrow."
   
   CRITICAL INSIGHT: Scarcity also suppresses critical thinking.
   Under urgency, people skip verification steps.
   The most successful social engineering attacks CREATE artificial time pressure
   specifically to prevent the target from thinking clearly or following process.
   
   DEFENSE: Any unusual request under time pressure = automatic slow-down.
   Legitimate systems have processes. Legitimate people honor those processes.
   "I understand this is urgent. Our process requires me to call back on a
   verified number." Real IT departments say yes to this. Attackers push back.

--- ADVANCED PRETEXTING SCENARIOS ---

SCENARIO 1: The Vendor Call (IT Support)
Pretext: You are a vendor who needs to verify the environment is receiving
your monitoring agent data correctly.

OSINT required first:
  - Company name, industry
  - IT staff names from LinkedIn
  - Tech stack (job postings reveal: "experience with CrowdStrike, Splunk, AWS")
  - Recent news (merger, new product launch = plausible IT change)

Script:
  "Hi, this is Dan calling from Splunk Enterprise Support. We're seeing an
  interruption in telemetry from your environment since Wednesday — we're not
  receiving your forwarder data. I have [real name from LinkedIn] marked as
  your primary contact but I'm unable to reach him. Do you have visibility
  into your Splunk Universal Forwarder status?
  
  [Target explains they're not sure / not IT]
  
  Totally understandable. Could you transfer me to someone on the IT ops team?
  I want to make sure we get this resolved — your [specific product feature]
  data has a gap starting at 14:32 UTC Wednesday and your license SLA requires
  continuity. I'd hate for this to affect your compliance reporting."

Why this works:
  - Real vendor name they recognize
  - Specific technical detail (forwarder, telemetry, UTC timestamp)
  - Specific name from LinkedIn (felt verified)
  - Stakes: Compliance, SLA, real consequences
  - Transfer request: Now you're in the building's phone system

SCENARIO 2: The Callback (Reversing the verification)
Most social engineering defense: "I'll need to verify you, can I call you back?"
Attacker technique: Flip this. YOU demand to call back on a verified number.

"Actually, I prefer to call you back — we had a security incident last month
involving spoofed support calls and our policy is to initiate callbacks.
What number should I call you back on?"

If target gives you a number, you know:
  a) They trust you enough to give their direct line
  b) You can call back later with a different pretext on a number they've given you
  c) They've been conditioned that YOU are security-conscious — counterintuitive trust boost

SCENARIO 3: The Tailgate (Physical Social Engineering)
Context: You need to enter a target building for authorized red team assessment.

Layer 1: Appearance. Dress to match the environment exactly.
  - Tech company with hoodies: Don't wear a suit
  - Law firm: Suit required
  - Manufacturing facility: Safety vest + hard hat = invisible
  - Carrying objects: People almost never challenge someone carrying stuff
    (both hands full = clearly busy, clearly belongs here)
  - Confidence + direction = belonging. Hesitation = challenge magnet.

Layer 2: Timing.
  - 8:45-9:15 AM rush: People are streaming in, door holds are constant
  - Post-lunch 12:45-1:30 PM: Mental guard is down, food coma
  - 5-6 PM leaving rush: Focus is on getting out, not checking who comes in
  - Never: Mid-morning quiet, mid-afternoon alert hours

Layer 3: The Conversation Opener.
  Don't sneak. Approach confidently and speak first.
  "Hey — I've got my hands full, could you grab that door?"
  
  Socially, person now feels rude if they don't help.
  Holding a door closed in someone's face triggers primal social discomfort.
  Most people will hold the door for someone they've never met
  simply because they were asked directly and holding it costs them nothing.

Layer 4: If challenged.
  Don't hesitate. Don't look flustered. Show something.
  A printed email, a badge (from any company), a business card.
  "I'm meeting with [common name like] Sarah from HR — she said to come up to the 4th floor."
  Challenge is almost always abandoned after one plausible response.
  Most security guards are not trained to press past the first answer.

--- OSINT: THE FOUNDATION OF SOCIAL ENGINEERING ---

PROFESSIONAL OSINT WORKFLOW FOR TARGETING AN ORGANIZATION:

PHASE 1: Passive (no interaction with target)
  Company intelligence:
    theHarvester -d target.com -b all
    LinkedIn: All employees by company → names, titles, tenures, tech skills
    Job postings: Reveal exact tech stack in use (security tools, cloud provider, languages)
    GitHub: Code repos, internal tool names, API endpoints, accidentally committed secrets
      git log --all --full-history -- "**/*.env"
      grep -r "password\|secret\|key\|token" --include="*.json" --include="*.yaml"
    Shodan: IP ranges, exposed services, certificate transparency
      org:"target company name" port:22,3389,443
    crt.sh: All issued TLS certificates → subdomain enumeration
      https://crt.sh/?q=%.target.com

  Personnel intelligence:
    LinkedIn: Profile photo, previous companies, education, connections (who do they know)
    Twitter/X: Personal opinions, after-hours behavior, travel patterns
    Instagram: Physical location clues, personal details, family info
    Facebook: Hometown, family relationships, life events
    Pipl / BeenVerified: Aggregated personal records
    HaveIBeenPwned: Previous breach exposure → potential password patterns
    Breach database search: Email + previous password → pattern for current password guess

PHASE 2: Semi-passive (uses public interfaces, no direct contact)
    Google dorks:
      site:target.com filetype:pdf
      site:target.com "internal use only"
      site:target.com inurl:login
      "target company" "password" filetype:xlsx
      "@target.com" "password" site:pastebin.com
    
    Wayback Machine: Old pages, removed content, previous employee names
    LinkedIn Sales Navigator: Deeper employee mapping
    Twitter Advanced Search: Internal discussions, mentions of tools/outages
    Glassdoor: Internal culture, security team size, tool complaints

PHASE 3: Active (direct interaction with target infrastructure)
    DNS reconnaissance:
      fierce --domain target.com
      dnsenum target.com
      dnsrecon -d target.com -t std
    
    Web enumeration:
      gobuster dir -u https://target.com -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirbuster/directory-list-2.3-medium.txt
      ffuf -w subdomains.txt -u https://FUZZ.target.com -mc 200,301,302
    
    Email validation (confirms active accounts for phishing):
      hunter.io: Email pattern discovery
      verify-email.org: SMTP-level validation without sending

OSINT OPSEC (protecting yourself during OSINT):
  Always use: Tor, VPN, or dedicated investigation VMs
  Reason: Target websites log visitors. Your direct IP visiting their careers page
  repeatedly is evidence of pre-attack reconnaissance.
  For sensitive investigation: Fresh VM, Tor browser, new email, no cross-contamination.

--- SITUATIONAL AWARENESS IN SECURITY ---

THE COOPER COLOR CODE (threat awareness system, used by military/law enforcement):
  White: Unaware, relaxed, not paying attention (dangerous default state)
  Yellow: Relaxed alertness — aware of environment, nothing specific, sustainable
  Orange: Specific alert — something has your attention, threat assessment active
  Red: Ready to act — threat confirmed, response committed

CYBER ADAPTATION:
  White: Running production systems with no monitoring, trusting all traffic
  Yellow: SIEM active, alerts tuned, team checking dashboards daily
  Orange: Anomaly detected — specific IP, specific behavior being investigated
  Red: Confirmed incident — IR team engaged, containment actions executing

PRINCIPLE: Most organizations live in White. They get breached, then spend weeks
in Red. The goal is to live in Yellow permanently — never surprised,
never completely reactive.

--- THE STREET PRINCIPLE: TRUST IS EARNED, NOT GIVEN ---

On the street: You trust people based on demonstrated behavior over time,
not on what they tell you about themselves. Titles, credentials, uniforms
are signals — not proof. Anyone can fake them.

APPLIED TO ZERO TRUST ARCHITECTURE:
This is literally where Zero Trust got its philosophical foundation.
"Never trust, always verify" is street wisdom translated into network architecture.

On the street you learn:
  1. What people DO matters more than what they SAY
     In cyber: Behavioral detection > signature detection
     A legitimate admin with stolen credentials still moves differently than
     that admin normally moves. Behavior is harder to fake than identity.
  
  2. Desperation makes people dangerous and manipulable
     In cyber: Financial pressure is the #1 insider threat predictor.
     Someone under severe financial stress will do things their normal
     self would never consider. This is exploited by nation-states actively.
  
  3. Most people are honest but situational ethics are real
     People who would never steal for fun will steal when they feel wronged.
     Disgruntled employees, recently fired contractors — threat window spikes.
  
  4. The most dangerous threat is the one you've already decided is safe
     In cyber: Lateral movement is slow precisely because defenders have
     already mentally whitelisted internal traffic.
     The attacker moves from one trusted position to another, never triggering
     the "outside" detection, always inside the "trusted" perimeter.

--- GAME THEORY FOR SECURITY PROFESSIONALS ---

THE ATTACKER'S DECISION TREE:
Every attacker makes continuous cost-benefit calculations:

  Expected value of attack = (Probability of success × Value of success)
                           - (Probability of detection × Cost of detection)
                           - (Effort cost)

To deter attackers, you must move these levers:
  - Reduce probability of success (better controls)
  - Reduce value of success (encryption, tokenization, data minimization)
  - Increase probability of detection (better monitoring, faster response)
  - Increase cost of detection (active deception, honeypots, legal pursuit)
  - Increase effort cost (friction, complexity, time requirements)

MOST DEFENDERS DO ONLY THE FIRST. The rest are often neglected.

HONEYPOTS AND DECEPTION: The street equivalent of a sting operation.
  Place honey credentials in your environment:
    - Fake AWS keys in a README.md (monitor for API calls using those keys)
    - Fake admin account "IT-Admin-Backup" with no real access (monitor for login)
    - Fake database with realistic-looking fake PII (monitor for queries)
    - DNS names that only a network scanner would query (canary DNS)
  
  Any interaction with these = high-confidence attacker indicator.
  Zero false positives (legitimate users never touch these).
  Detection without any security tooling cost.
  This is street-smart security: set a trap and let the attacker walk into it.

THE CONFIDENCE GAME AND HOW TO SPOT IT:
Street con artists and cyber social engineers use identical playbooks.
The 3-part structure of every con:
  
  FOUNDATION: Establish credibility
    Con: Dress well, name-drop, show fake credentials
    Cyber: Spoofed email domain, cloned website, scraped LinkedIn details
  
  BUILD-UP: Create investment
    Con: Small wins first — let mark win small amounts
    Cyber: Phishing email with genuinely useful content first; malicious link second
  
  THE BLOW-OFF: Create urgency, suppress verification
    Con: "This deal closes in one hour, I can't hold it for you"
    Cyber: "Your account will be permanently deleted in 24 hours — click here now"
  
  RECOGNITION: Whenever anyone is rushing you past your own verification process,
  that is the single most reliable signal of manipulation in all of human interaction.
  Legitimate urgency tolerates your process. False urgency cannot afford it.

--- HOW REAL ATTACKERS ACTUALLY THINK ---

THE MINIMUM FOOTPRINT PRINCIPLE:
Sophisticated attackers don't do everything they can. They do only what they need.
Why: Every action has a detection probability. More actions = more chances to fail.

Progression: Entry point → Establish persistence → Privilege escalation →
             Lateral movement → Objective → Exfil → Cover tracks

A nation-state actor may sit on an objective for months without moving.
They're mapping, learning, waiting for the right window.
Script kiddies: Noisy, fast, easy to catch.
APTs: Quiet, slow, hard to catch.

The tell: After a patient attacker has everything they need, their behavior
suddenly changes — exfiltration often involves larger, faster data movements
than their quiet reconnaissance phase. That behavioral discontinuity is your
detection window.

THE ATTACKER'S PERSPECTIVE ON YOUR DEFENSES:
When assessing a target, sophisticated attackers think:

  "What are they DEFINITELY watching?" → Don't touch those
  "What are they PROBABLY watching?" → Touch carefully, test first
  "What are they UNLIKELY watching?" → This is the attack path
  
  Most defenders watch: Perimeter (firewall, IDS), endpoints (EDR), known bad IPs
  Most defenders don't watch: Internal DNS, printer network, HVAC controllers,
  conference room AV systems, guest WiFi, shadow IT, personal devices on corporate network
  
  Real APT axiom: "Find the part of the network that the security team
  built their career ignoring."

LIVING OFF THE LAND — THE STREET SMART ATTACK PHILOSOPHY:
Why bring your own tools when your victim has everything you need?

  LOLBins (Living Off the Land Binaries):
    certutil.exe — download, decode base64, compute hashes
    regsvr32.exe — execute COM objects, bypass AppLocker
    mshta.exe — execute HTA files including remote URLs
    wmic.exe — process creation, lateral movement
    powershell.exe — everything
    rundll32.exe — execute DLLs, proxy execution
    bitsadmin.exe — download files via Background Intelligent Transfer Service
    msiexec.exe — install MSI packages including from UNC paths
  
  Why this matters for defenders: Blocking these breaks legitimate workflows.
  You can't just blacklist certutil. You need behavioral rules:
    certutil -urlcache + external URL = suspicious
    certutil called by unusual parent process = suspicious
    certutil writing to unusual location = suspicious
  
  Behavior, not tool, is what you detect.

SOCIAL ENGINEERING RED FLAGS — TRAINING EMPLOYEES:
What to tell non-technical employees to watch for:

  The 7 signs someone is social engineering you:
  1. They know things about you that a stranger shouldn't (name, boss, system names)
     — Makes you feel they're already trusted. It's OSINT. Verify independently.
  2. They create urgency where none existed before
     — "Your account will be deleted in 2 hours." Legitimate: Account changes have processes.
  3. They ask you to bypass normal processes "just this once"
     — "I know it's policy but..." If they know it's policy, they know why it exists.
  4. They react unusually to your verification attempts
     — Sighing, pushback, frustration at being asked to verify. Real IT expects it.
  5. The story gets more complicated when you ask questions
     — Liars add detail to fill gaps. Truth stays simple and consistent.
  6. They appeal to authority you can't immediately verify
     — "The CEO asked me personally." Hang up. Call the CEO directly.
  7. They make you feel bad for not helping
     — "I thought you were different, most people here just help out."
        Guilt + social pressure is a manipulation technique, not a legitimate request.
