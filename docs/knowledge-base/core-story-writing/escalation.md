# Escalation

## Purpose
Escalation is the progressive intensification of conflict, stakes, and consequences across a film's runtime.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Escalation means each attempt to solve the problem creates a bigger problem
- True escalation closes options — the protagonist has fewer and fewer choices
- Escalation must affect all story dimensions: plot, character, and emotional
- A film that escalates only externally but not internally feels hollow
- The darkest moment is the peak of escalation before the resolution

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Map escalation as a curve — it should rise from Act 1 to climax with brief valleys
- Flag any sequence where the protagonist's situation doesn't get meaningfully worse
- Distinguish between repetition (same problem repeated) and escalation (bigger version of the problem)

## Scene-level checks
- Is the protagonist worse off at the end of this scene than at the start?
- Does this scene close off an option the protagonist previously had?

## Project-level checks
- Does each act end with higher stakes than the one before?
- Is there a clear escalation curve from inciting incident to climax?
- Does the darkest moment feel genuinely worse than anything earlier?

## Warning signs
- Repetitive failure — protagonist tries the same approach four times without escalation
- External escalation only — bigger stunts but no deeper emotional cost
- Protagonist who gains options instead of losing them in Act 2

## Strong recommendation patterns
- When Act 2 sags, check whether escalation is genuine or just repetitive
- Confirm that each act break represents a genuine escalation in stakes

## Example assistant output
> Your Act 2 has five attempts to rescue Sara but each feels the same difficulty. Escalation means each attempt must be harder because of what the previous one cost. First attempt: he loses his money. Second: his alibi. Third: his partner. Now the final attempt is impossible unless he sacrifices something personal.
