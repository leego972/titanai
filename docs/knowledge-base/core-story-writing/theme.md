# Theme

## Purpose
Theme is the film's central argument about the human condition — the invisible through-line that gives every scene, character, and image deeper meaning.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Theme is not topic — 'war' is a topic, 'war destroys what it claims to protect' is a theme
- Theme is expressed through character decisions, not dialogue speeches
- Every major character should embody a different stance on the theme
- The climax should resolve the theme's central question, not just the plot
- Theme must be present from Act 1, not introduced in Act 3

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Identify the film's theme before advising on character arcs or scene structure
- If the theme is stated directly in dialogue, flag it as on-the-nose
- Check that the antagonist's worldview is a direct challenge to the theme

## Scene-level checks
- Does this scene actively engage with the film's theme?
- Is the theme being shown through action and consequence, or just stated?

## Project-level checks
- Can the theme be stated in one clear sentence?
- Does the protagonist's arc align with the thematic resolution?
- Do all major characters represent different positions on the theme?

## Warning signs
- Theme that changes halfway through the film
- Theme delivered through monologues rather than dramatic action
- Protagonist whose arc contradicts the film's stated theme

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Ask the director: what is this film about beyond its plot? The answer is the theme
- Check every major scene against the theme — scenes that don't engage with it are risks

## Example assistant output
> Your film is about revenge, but your theme seems to be saying revenge is justified — which means your protagonist has no arc. For a theme to resonate, the protagonist needs to believe one thing at the start and discover another by the end.
