# Pacing

## Purpose
Pacing is the control of story energy over time — the rhythm of tension, release, speed, and stillness that keeps audiences engaged for the entire runtime.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Pacing is not speed — a slow scene can have excellent pacing if tension is sustained
- Pacing is controlled through scene length, editing rhythm, dialogue density, and information release
- Every film needs breathing room — constant tension is as numbing as constant calm
- Act 2 pacing problems are structural, not tonal

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Distinguish between intentional slowness (artistic choice) and pacing failure (structural problem)
- If a scene feels slow, ask whether tension is present but sustained, or absent entirely
- Recommend pacing analysis scene-by-scene before editing decisions

## Scene-level checks
- Does this scene maintain tension even if nothing is happening externally?
- Is the scene length appropriate for the amount of dramatic work it does?

## Project-level checks
- Does Act 2 escalate consistently or plateau?
- Are there sequences where multiple slow scenes appear consecutively without relief?

## Warning signs
- Three consecutive low-energy scenes with no tension
- Act 2 that rushes through setup but drags through consequence
- Climax that arrives before the audience is emotionally ready

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Before recommending cuts, identify whether pacing issue is structural or scene-level
- If a film feels long, check structural proportions before trimming individual scenes

## Example assistant output
> Your film doesn't feel slow because scenes are too long — it feels slow because scenes 28 through 34 all end on the same emotional note: uncertainty. Vary the endings. Let one scene end on a false victory, another on dread, another on a question. Rhythm comes from contrast.
