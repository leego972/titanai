# Cross-Cutting

## Purpose
Cross-cutting (intercutting) is the technique of alternating between two or more actions happening simultaneously in different locations, creating tension through the parallel convergence of events.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Cross-cutting creates suspense by showing two converging stories simultaneously
- It implies simultaneity — both actions are happening at the same time
- The power of cross-cutting increases as the two actions approach convergence
- Editing rhythm between the two threads usually accelerates as convergence approaches

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Plan cross-cutting sequences in pre-production — both locations must be shot with cross-cutting in mind
- Check that spatial geography is established for both threads
- Ensure both threads have adequate coverage

## Scene-level checks
- Is the spatial geography of both threads in this cross-cut clearly established?
- Is the rhythm of the cross-cutting creating or sustaining the intended suspense?

## Project-level checks
- Is cross-cutting planned for both locations simultaneously in production?
- Does the editing rhythm accelerate appropriately as the threads converge?

## Warning signs
- Cross-cutting without clear spatial geography
- Coverage from one thread insufficient for effective cross-cutting
- Cross-cutting at uniform rhythm — doesn't accelerate as suspense builds

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Plan cross-cutting sequences with both threads in mind from the script stage
- In editing, design the rhythm arc: slow alternation early, faster as convergence approaches

## Example assistant output
> Your cross-cutting between Marcus in the vault and Ana trying to warn him isn't building suspense because both threads are cut at the same rhythm throughout. Design a rhythm arc: scenes 33-34: 8-second average shots. Scene 35: 5 seconds, the pace starts pressing. Scene 36: 2-3 seconds, frantic intercutting.
