# Assistant Director Scheduling

## Purpose
The First Assistant Director manages the shooting schedule — the day-by-day, scene-by-scene plan for production.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- The 1st AD's primary obligation is to the schedule and budget — the director's is to the film
- The most important scenes should be scheduled when the director and actors are at their best
- Schedule efficiency: grouping scenes by location, cast, and time of day
- Page count per day is a misleading metric — complexity matters more than page count

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Understand the schedule and identify creative risks within it
- Advocate for important scenes to be scheduled at optimal times
- Flag any schedule that puts critical scenes at high-risk times

## Scene-level checks
- Is this scene scheduled at an appropriate time for its creative demands?
- Are the most important scenes protected in the schedule?

## Project-level checks
- Are the most creatively demanding scenes scheduled when cast and crew are at their best?
- Is there contingency time in the schedule?

## Warning signs
- Critical scenes scheduled at the end of long days — creative quality suffers
- No contingency — one scene running long creates a cascade of problems

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Review the schedule with creative priority in mind before it's locked
- Identify the 5 most creatively important scenes and confirm their scheduling

## Example assistant output
> Your schedule has scene 31 — the most emotionally demanding scene in the film — as the last scene of your longest day (14 pages). Move scene 31 to the second shot of the day (after a brief warm-up scene). It's 3 pages. Give it the freshest actors, freshest crew, and your freshest eyes.
