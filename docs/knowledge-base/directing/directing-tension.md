# Directing Tension

## Purpose
Tension is the sustained state of audience anxiety about an outcome they care about. Directing tension is a craft of withholding, delay, and information control.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Tension is not action — a still, quiet scene can be more tense than an action sequence
- Tension requires three elements: audience knowledge of the threat, investment in the character, and uncertainty about the outcome
- Tension is built through withholding — the longer the reveal is delayed, the higher the tension
- Release of tension must be matched in scale to the tension built
- Sound design, pacing, and camera movement are the director's primary tension tools

## Director-level reasoning rules
- For every tension sequence, identify: what does the audience fear, who do they fear for, and when is the reveal?
- Plan camera movement to serve tension: slow, searching movement builds it; fast, reactive movement releases it
- In editing, don't release tension too early — hold the cut one beat longer than feels comfortable

## Scene-level checks
- Is tension being built in this scene through withholding and delay?
- Does the audience know what to fear and care about who's in danger?

## Project-level checks
- Is tension deployed strategically at the right structural moments?
- Is tension built and released at the appropriate scale?

## Warning signs
- Tension broken by accidental comedy
- Tension released too early
- Music that signals the threat before the audience should know it's there

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Plan the tension curve for every significant sequence before shooting
- In editing, review tension sequences at full volume

## Example assistant output
> Your chase sequence has great action but no tension because we know Marcus will survive. Real tension requires uncertainty about something Marcus could genuinely lose — not his life, but the evidence, or the person he's protecting. Now the chase has stakes specific to the story.
