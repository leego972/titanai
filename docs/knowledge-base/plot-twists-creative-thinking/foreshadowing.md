# Foreshadowing

## Purpose
Foreshadowing is the technique of placing early story elements that hint at later developments — creating retroactive inevitability and rewarding attentive audiences.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Foreshadowing makes the story feel like destiny — events were always going to happen this way
- It must be subtle enough to miss on first viewing but obvious in retrospect
- Types: visual (a recurring image), dialogue (a throwaway line), thematic (a parallel situation), and action (a behaviour pattern)
- Foreshadowing and setup-and-payoff overlap but are distinct: setup provides necessary information; foreshadowing creates emotional resonance
- The best foreshadowing works as part of the normal story before its significance is revealed

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Track all foreshadowing elements — they must pay off or be removed
- Check that foreshadowing is subtle enough to be missed first time
- Test foreshadowing by watching the film twice — it should be visible and satisfying on the second viewing
- Distinguish foreshadowing from telegraphing — the difference is subtlety

## Scene-level checks
- Is this foreshadowing element subtle enough to be missed on first viewing?
- Does this foreshadowing element integrate naturally into the story, not stick out as a plant?

## Project-level checks
- Are all foreshadowing elements tracked and paid off?
- Is there foreshadowing for all major third-act events?
- Is foreshadowing balanced — present but not so heavy it telegraphs the outcome?

## Warning signs
- Foreshadowing so obvious it telegraphs the twist — ruins the surprise
- Foreshadowing planted but never paid off — creates false expectations
- Over-reliance on dialogue foreshadowing — 'little did he know' — too obvious and uncinematic

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Create a foreshadowing map: every element planted and its payoff
- Prioritise visual foreshadowing over dialogue — images stay in memory differently than words

## Example assistant output
> The recurring motif of Marcus touching his scar in moments of doubt is excellent foreshadowing — subtle, visual, character-specific. You use it four times in Act 1 and Act 2. In Act 3, when he makes his defining choice, he should touch the scar and stop himself — breaking the pattern. The audience who noticed will feel the character's growth without a word of dialogue.
