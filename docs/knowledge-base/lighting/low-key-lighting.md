# Low-Key Lighting

## Purpose
Low-key lighting features deep shadows, high contrast, and limited illumination. It communicates threat, mystery, moral ambiguity, and the unknown.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Low-key: high contrast, deep shadows, low overall illumination, selective illumination
- Communicates: threat, mystery, moral ambiguity, secrets, danger
- Low-key doesn't mean underexposed — the lit areas should be correctly exposed
- Low-key requires precision: where the light lands and where it doesn't is equally important

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate low-key lighting for precision and dramatic purpose
- Check that low-key scenes are not simply underexposed
- Brief the gaffer: what is lit in a low-key scene is a specific creative decision

## Scene-level checks
- Is the low-key lighting in this scene creating drama through specific shadow placement?
- Is the scene correctly exposed in the lit areas?

## Project-level checks
- Is low-key lighting escalating appropriately through Act 2 as tension rises?
- Are low-key setups planned with precision?

## Warning signs
- Underexposed scenes passed off as low-key — flat, dark, and unreadable
- Low-key without a plan — dark for aesthetic reasons
- Low-key scenes that lose all visible detail

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Plan low-key setups by deciding what stays in shadow, then light only what must be seen
- Brief the colorist that low-key footage needs careful grading

## Example assistant output
> Your scene 34 is genuinely very dark. The problem is I can't see Elena's eyes. In a low-key setup, you can lose the body, you can lose the background, but you must hold the eyes — that's where the emotion lives. Add a tiny eye light: a small directional source that catches just the eyes. One square inch of light. The scene stays dark but we can see what she's feeling.
