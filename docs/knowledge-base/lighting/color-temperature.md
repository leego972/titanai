# Color Temperature

## Purpose
Color temperature measures the warmth or coolness of a light source in Kelvin. It is one of the most powerful tools in cinematography and lighting design.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Color temperature in Kelvin: candlelight (~1800K), tungsten (~3200K), daylight (~5600K)
- Warm (low Kelvin) = domestic, safe, historical, internal, comfortable
- Cool (high Kelvin) = exterior, clinical, modern, exposed, alienated
- Mixing color temperatures creates visual complexity
- White balancing eliminates color temperature — sometimes the temperature itself is the story

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate color temperature design for narrative meaning
- Brief the DP: do not white-balance out deliberate color temperature choices
- For scenes of domestic safety, warm sources; for scenes of exposure or threat, cool sources

## Scene-level checks
- Is the color temperature in this scene communicating the intended emotional register?
- Does the color temperature create or reinforce the scene's narrative meaning?

## Project-level checks
- Is color temperature designed as part of the film's visual language?
- Do color temperature shifts signal story or character changes?

## Warning signs
- Color temperature inconsistency within a scene
- Accidental warm or cool light that contradicts the scene's emotional register
- White-balancing out deliberate color temperature choices

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Establish a color temperature palette for the film: what temperature means what
- Review dailies for color temperature consistency

## Example assistant output
> Your domestic safety scenes (Elena's home in Act 1) are lit warm and tungsten — perfect. But your interrogation scenes are also warm because you shot them in the same building. The interrogation should be cool, clinical, exposed. Get 1/2 CTB gel on your key light for interrogation scenes.
