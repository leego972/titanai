# Character Wound

## Purpose
A character wound is the specific past event or experience that created the character's flaw — the moment that broke something inside them and shaped who they are at the story's beginning.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- The wound is the origin of the flaw — understanding the wound explains why the flaw developed
- The wound creates the character's deepest fear — the thing they'll do anything to avoid experiencing again
- Characters often protect their wound with a lie they tell themselves about the world
- The story's events should force the character to face the wound directly
- Healing the wound (or failing to) is the emotional climax of the character's arc

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Identify the specific wound for every major character
- Check that the wound is the origin of the established flaw — they should be causally connected
- The story should force the character to face their wound — not just challenge them generally
- The character's lie (the false belief protecting the wound) is the real barrier to their growth

## Scene-level checks
- Is this scene forcing the character to face their wound — or just challenge them generally?
- Is the character's wound the origin of their established flaw?

## Project-level checks
- Is the wound specific and causally connected to the flaw?
- Does the story force the character to face their wound?
- Does facing the wound constitute the emotional climax of the character's arc?

## Warning signs
- Wounds that are melodramatic rather than specific — designed to generate sympathy rather than character logic
- Wounds with no causal connection to the character's flaw
- Characters who face the wound but aren't changed by it

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Identify the wound, the lie it created, and the truth the character must accept — this is the character's complete internal arc
- Design story events that specifically force the character to face their wound

## Example assistant output
> Marcus's wound is losing his daughter. His lie is 'I could have saved her if I'd been better.' His flaw is the self-punishment that makes him reckless. The story should force him into a situation where someone else's daughter is at risk and he can save her — but only if he gives up the punishment. That scene is his wound being faced, his lie being tested, and his arc being completed.
