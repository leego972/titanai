# Character Flaw

## Purpose
A character flaw is the specific internal weakness that prevents a character from getting what they need — the thing they must overcome (or succumb to) for the arc to complete. It is the engine of character development.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- A character flaw is specific — not 'he's proud' but 'he believes vulnerability equals weakness, which prevents him from accepting help'
- The flaw should be a strength taken to excess — the hero's greatest strength and greatest flaw are often the same thing
- The flaw creates the character's internal obstacle — it's the enemy inside
- The story events must specifically challenge and test the flaw
- In a positive arc, the flaw is overcome; in a negative arc, it destroys the character

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Identify the specific flaw for every major character before advising on their arc
- Check that the story's events specifically test the flaw — not just challenge the character generally
- The flaw must be visible in behaviour from the beginning
- Test the flaw: can it be stated specifically enough that we can identify when the character is exhibiting it?

## Scene-level checks
- Is this character's flaw visible in their behaviour in this scene?
- Is this scene's conflict specifically testing the character's flaw?

## Project-level checks
- Are all major characters' flaws specific and visible from the beginning?
- Do the story's events specifically test each character's flaw?
- Is the flaw overcome (or succumbed to) in the climax?

## Warning signs
- Flaws so general they justify any character behaviour
- Flaws introduced late rather than visible from the beginning
- Story events that challenge the character but don't specifically test the flaw

## Strong recommendation patterns
- State each character's flaw as specifically as possible — test if you can identify it scene by scene
- Brief actors on the flaw as the engine of their arc

## Example assistant output
> Elena's flaw is stated as 'she doesn't trust people' but that's too general. Specify it: 'Elena can't accept help because she believes accepting it means admitting she was wrong to leave.' Now we can track exactly when the flaw appears and design specific story events that test that specific belief.
