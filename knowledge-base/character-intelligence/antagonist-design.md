# Antagonist Design

## Purpose
The antagonist is the force opposing the protagonist — the obstacle to their want that also, ideally, represents everything the protagonist must overcome internally. Great antagonism is a mirror, not just an obstacle.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- The antagonist opposes the protagonist's want — they are the external embodiment of the protagonist's internal obstacle
- A great antagonist believes they're the hero of their own story
- The antagonist should be as compelling as the protagonist — if they're not, the film loses half its dramatic engine
- The antagonist's goal should be understandable — even if their methods aren't
- The antagonist's worldview should directly challenge the film's theme

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate the antagonist for their worldview, motivation, and thematic function
- Check that the antagonist is compelling enough to generate genuine opposition
- The antagonist should challenge the protagonist's belief system, not just their plans
- A weak antagonist makes for a weak climax — the protagonist's victory is only as meaningful as the obstacle they overcome

## Scene-level checks
- Is this antagonist's worldview clearly established — do we understand why they believe what they believe?
- Is this antagonist compelling enough to generate genuine dramatic tension?

## Project-level checks
- Is the antagonist as fully developed as the protagonist?
- Does the antagonist's worldview directly challenge the film's theme?
- Is the antagonist's goal understandable even if their methods aren't?

## Warning signs
- Antagonists who are evil for evil's sake — no understandable worldview
- Antagonists less compelling than the protagonist — unbalanced drama
- Antagonists whose defeat is too easy — protagonists win without cost

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Give the antagonist a scene that makes the audience sympathise with their worldview before they condemn their actions
- Test the antagonist: if you told the story from their perspective, would they be the hero?

## Example assistant output
> Your antagonist is frightening and effective but we never understand why he believes the end justifies the means. Give him one scene where his logic is made completely clear — not just credible, but actually persuasive. The audience should momentarily think he might be right. That moment of moral uncertainty is what makes him genuinely threatening.
