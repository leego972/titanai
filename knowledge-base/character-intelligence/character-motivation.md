# Character Motivation

## Purpose
Motivation is the specific want driving a character's actions at every moment — the 'why' behind every decision. Without clear motivation, characters are puppets of plot. The 1B Brain must ensure every major character has clear, specific motivation at every point in the story.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Motivation operates at multiple levels: immediate want (this scene), overall goal (this film), and deepest need (what they actually need but may not know)
- The gap between what a character wants and what they need is often the source of the character's arc
- Motivation must be specific — 'to survive' is too vague to generate interesting decisions
- Character decisions that don't arise from motivation are plot puppetry
- Motivation can change — but change must be caused by a specific event

## Director-level reasoning rules
- For every scene, identify the specific motivation for every major character
- Check that character decisions follow logically from their established motivation
- Flag any decision that doesn't arise from the character's established motivation
- Motivation must be specific — replace 'to win' with 'to prove their father wrong'

## Scene-level checks
- What is this character's specific motivation in this scene — what do they want right now?
- Does this character's decision arise from their established motivation?

## Project-level checks
- Are all major characters' motivations clear and specific?
- Do character decisions consistently arise from established motivation?
- Does motivation change logically in response to story events?

## Warning signs
- Characters who make decisions for plot reasons rather than character reasons
- Vague motivation — 'to do the right thing' — that can justify any decision
- Motivation that changes without a clear causing event

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Write a one-sentence motivation statement for every character before production
- Brief actors: their character's motivation is the engine of every scene

## Example assistant output
> Your antagonist's motivation is 'greed.' That's a category, not a motivation. What specifically does he want the money for? What does having enough money mean to him? 'He wants to buy back the factory his father lost' is specific enough to generate interesting decisions. Vague motivation produces generic villainy.
