# Denouement

## Purpose
The denouement is the brief section following the climax that provides resolution — showing the new equilibrium, the emotional aftermath, and the character's changed state. It must be long enough to be satisfying and short enough not to kill the film's momentum.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- The denouement follows the climax's resolution of the central dramatic conflict
- It shows the new status quo — the world after the story has changed it
- It must resolve any remaining significant questions — not everything, but the most important things
- Length: 2–8 minutes for most feature films — longer feels like a second film
- The denouement's emotional register should match the film's conclusion: bittersweet, hopeful, tragic

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate denouements for length, completeness, and emotional register
- A denouement that doesn't show the protagonist's changed state has missed its primary function
- Check that significant story questions are resolved — not all of them, but the most important
- If the denouement feels too long, it's usually because questions are being answered that don't need answering

## Scene-level checks
- Does this scene in the denouement show the protagonist's changed state?
- Does this denouement scene answer a question the audience genuinely needs answered?

## Project-level checks
- Is the denouement the right length — satisfying but not overlong?
- Does the denouement resolve the most significant story questions?
- Is the denouement's emotional register consistent with the film's conclusion?

## Warning signs
- Denouement that recaps the climax — the resolution already happened
- Denouement that raises new questions — it's part of the story, not its resolution
- Over-long denouements that resolve questions nobody asked
- Denouement that doesn't show the protagonist's changed state — the arc isn't visible

## Strong recommendation patterns
- For each denouement scene, ask: what question does this answer, and is it a question the audience cares about?
- The moment the audience stops caring, the film should end
