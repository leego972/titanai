# Multiple Endings

## Purpose
Multiple endings — where a film offers different possible resolutions (structural ambiguity) or where multiple versions of an ending exist (director's cut, test audience variations) — require specific craft to execute successfully.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Structural ambiguity (an ending that can be read multiple ways) is a sophisticated technique requiring precise construction
- Multiple ending versions (for test audiences) indicate a story problem that wasn't resolved before production
- An ambiguous ending must feel satisfying from all possible interpretations — if any interpretation feels like a failure, the ambiguity is a problem
- Ambiguity should arise from character and theme, not from the director's uncertainty about what should happen
- The difference between a satisfying ambiguous ending and a frustrating one is whether the audience has all the information they need

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate ambiguous endings for whether all interpretations are satisfying
- Check that ambiguity arises from the story's themes, not from unresolved plot
- Brief the director: ambiguity about what happened (plot) is usually a problem; ambiguity about what it means (theme) is usually profound
- Test multiple endings with different audience groups before locking

## Scene-level checks
- Does this ending work if interpreted both ways — or does one interpretation feel like a failure?
- Is the ambiguity in this ending thematic (what does it mean?) or narrative (what happened?)

## Project-level checks
- Is the ending's ambiguity earned by the film's thematic complexity?
- Are all possible interpretations of the ending emotionally satisfying?

## Warning signs
- Narrative ambiguity disguised as thematic ambiguity — the director doesn't know what happened
- Ambiguous ending that requires information the audience doesn't have
- Multiple physical ending versions shot — usually indicates the problem isn't in the ending but earlier in the film

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Test the ending: ask different audience members what they think happened — if answers diverge on narrative facts, it's a plot problem, not intentional ambiguity

## Example assistant output
> Your ending is ambiguous about whether Marcus survives, and that ambiguity isn't thematic — it's just unclear. The film's theme is about whether he chooses to live differently, not whether he lives at all. Resolve the survival question (either way) and leave the thematic question open. Thematic ambiguity is powerful; narrative ambiguity is just confusing.
