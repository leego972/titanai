# Subtext

## Purpose
Subtext is what characters mean but don't say — the emotional truth beneath the surface of dialogue and action. Subtext is what separates great film writing from functional film writing.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Subtext exists when characters can't or won't say what they really mean
- The best dialogue scenes are about something completely different from their surface topic
- Subtext is communicated through: word choice, pauses, what's avoided, physical behaviour, and contradiction between words and action
- When characters say exactly what they mean, they're speaking in exposition, not subtext
- Subtext requires the audience to actively participate — they complete the meaning

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate dialogue for subtext — is there a deeper conversation beneath the surface?
- Flag any scene where characters say exactly what they mean — this is an exposition problem
- Brief actors: subtext is the real performance. The lines are cover.
- Check that subtext is consistent with the character's established psychology

## Scene-level checks
- Is there a subtext conversation happening beneath the surface of this scene's dialogue?
- Are the characters communicating their real feelings through indirect language and behaviour?

## Project-level checks
- Does the film have a consistent subtext layer throughout?
- Are key emotional truths communicated through subtext rather than direct statement?
- Is the audience trusted to read the subtext — or is it over-explained?

## Warning signs
- Scenes where characters state exactly what they feel — no subtext, just exposition
- Characters who articulate their own psychology perfectly — real people don't have this clarity
- Scenes where the subtext is so subtle it's invisible — director knows it, audience doesn't

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Ask for each scene: what can't the characters say, and why can't they say it? That's the subtext.
- Brief actors on the subtext separately from the dialogue — they're playing two scenes simultaneously

## Example assistant output
> Your argument scene between Elena and her father is about the old house. But the real argument is about whether she blames him for her mother's death. The house is the safe version. Direct your actors on the real argument and let the surface argument run on its own — the collision between the two layers is where the scene lives.
