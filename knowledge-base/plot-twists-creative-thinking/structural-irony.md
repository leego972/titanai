# Structural Irony

## Purpose
Structural irony is the gap between what the audience knows and what the characters know, or between what the story appears to be about and what it's actually about. It creates layers of meaning that reward repeat viewing.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Dramatic irony: the audience knows something a character doesn't — creates tension, comedy, or tragedy
- Situational irony: the outcome is the opposite of what was expected — creates meaning through contrast
- Thematic irony: the story appears to argue one thing but actually argues its opposite
- Structural irony is the deepest layer of cinematic storytelling — it requires the story to work on two levels simultaneously
- The best structural irony makes the film richer on every viewing, not just the first

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Identify all forms of irony in the film and evaluate whether they're being used intentionally
- Check that dramatic irony is supported by the audience having the right information at the right time
- Thematic irony requires extraordinary structural discipline — the story must simultaneously argue two positions
- Use structural irony as a quality test — a film with deep irony has deep architecture

## Scene-level checks
- Is this scene operating ironically — and is the irony visible to the audience?
- Is the audience in possession of the information they need for the intended irony to land?

## Project-level checks
- Are the film's major ironies intentional and structurally supported?
- Does the film work on multiple levels — the surface story and the ironic story?

## Warning signs
- Irony that requires the audience to know things they haven't been given
- Irony so subtle it's invisible — the director knows it, the audience doesn't
- Thematic irony so strong it contradicts the intended message

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Identify the film's ironic structure and ensure it's visible on screen, not just in the director's head

## Example assistant output
> Your film is structurally ironic: Marcus is hunting a corruption he is unknowingly participating in. That irony is your film's richest layer but it only lands if the audience suspects it before the reveal. Add two moments in Act 2 where the audience sees Marcus do something morally identical to what he's investigating — without the film commenting on it. The audience will feel the irony before they can name it.
