# Misdirection

## Purpose
Misdirection is the deliberate technique of directing the audience's attention and expectations toward an incorrect interpretation, setting up a more powerful revelation.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Misdirection works by making the wrong answer feel more obvious than the right one
- It requires giving the audience real information — just selectively presented
- The best misdirection uses the audience's genre knowledge against them
- Misdirection must be fair — retroactively, the audience should feel they had the information
- There are two types: misdirected attention (look here, not there) and misdirected interpretation (this means X, but it means Y)

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate misdirection for fairness — does it withhold information or mislead interpretation?
- Withholding information to deceive is a plot hole; presenting information that means something different is fair misdirection
- Check that misdirection doesn't require characters to behave inconsistently
- The payoff of misdirection should feel better than the anticipated version

## Scene-level checks
- Is this misdirection fair — does the audience have the real information, just reading it incorrectly?
- Is this misdirection supported by the character's consistent psychology?

## Project-level checks
- Is there a clear misdirection strategy for the film's major reveals?
- Does the misdirection make the reveal more powerful than if the audience had guessed correctly?

## Warning signs
- Misdirection that requires the audience to be given false information
- Misdirection that requires a character to act implausibly
- Misdirection so obvious it telegraphs the twist it's hiding

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Design misdirection from the reveal backward — what would make the audience miss this clue?
- Test misdirection with fresh readers who don't know the twist

## Example assistant output
> The misdirection in your film works too well — the audience is so convinced of the red herring that the real twist feels left-field. Add one more ambiguous clue for the real answer. The best misdirections make 40% of the audience suspect the truth before it's revealed — that 40% is your word-of-mouth.
