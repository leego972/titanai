# Music Direction

## Purpose
Music direction is the creative brief given to the composer — the specific emotional, thematic, and sonic requirements for the film's score.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- Music direction is not 'make it feel emotional' — it's 'make it feel like loss without sentimentality'
- Reference tracks are useful starting points but the composer must transcend them
- Music direction should address: instrumentation, tone, thematic material, and what to avoid
- The best music directions include what the music should NOT do

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Help directors articulate specific music direction before the composer is briefed
- Check that music direction goes beyond 'emotional' to specific emotional registers
- Identify what the score should explicitly avoid — generic, manipulative, on-the-nose

## Scene-level checks
- Is this scene's music direction specific enough to brief a composer?
- Does the music direction communicate what NOT to do as well as what to do?

## Project-level checks
- Is music direction developed before the composer begins?
- Is the music direction consistent with the film's visual and narrative language?

## Warning signs
- Music direction so vague it produces generic results: 'make it feel epic'
- Music direction given after the score is written — too late to influence the work

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Write a music direction document before briefing any composer
- Include three specific emotional registers, two instrumentation directions, and two things to avoid

## Example assistant output
> Your music direction to the composer was 'orchestral, emotional, like Hans Zimmer.' Try instead: 'Solo piano as the primary voice — intimate, hesitant, never fully resolving. Strings enter only in scenes of genuine human connection. No brass. No swells. The score should feel like it's afraid to be heard.' That's specific enough to produce music that could only belong to this film.
