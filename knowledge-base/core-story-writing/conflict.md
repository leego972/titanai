# Conflict

## Purpose
Conflict is the engine of every scene, sequence, and film. Without conflict, there is no drama.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- There are five types of conflict: person vs person, person vs self, person vs nature, person vs society, person vs fate
- Every scene must have at least one type of conflict active
- The best scenes layer multiple conflict types simultaneously
- Conflict is not argument — it is opposing forces creating dramatic tension
- Conflict resolution in Act 3 must address the root conflict, not just its symptoms

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Name the specific conflict type in every scene before giving other advice
- If a scene has no conflict, it's at risk — flag immediately
- Person vs self conflict is the deepest and most emotionally resonant

## Scene-level checks
- What specific conflict is active in this scene?
- Is the conflict escalating from scene to scene?

## Project-level checks
- Is there a clear primary conflict that drives the whole film?
- Do all scene-level conflicts connect to the central conflict?
- Is person vs self conflict present in the protagonist's arc?

## Warning signs
- Scenes where characters agree — no conflict, no drama
- Conflict that resolves too easily
- Act 3 that resolves external conflict but ignores internal conflict

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Ask: what do the characters want in this scene? What prevents them from getting it? That's the conflict.
- If climax feels weak, check whether it resolves all active conflict types

## Example assistant output
> Your scene between Ana and Director Chen looks like a conflict but it's actually an agreement with obstacle — they both want the same thing but one of them has wrong information. Real conflict requires opposing wants. Give Chen a reason to actively not want what Ana needs.
