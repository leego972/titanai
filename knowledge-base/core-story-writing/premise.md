# Premise

## Purpose
The premise is the foundational 'what if' that generates the entire story. A strong premise contains inherent conflict, a clear world, and infinite story potential.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- A premise is a 'what if' question with built-in dramatic tension
- Strong premises contain: a protagonist, a world, a problem, and inherent stakes
- The premise should make the audience ask 'what happens next' immediately
- Premise determines genre, tone, and audience expectations
- A weak premise can't be fixed by great execution

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Evaluate whether the premise generates enough conflict to sustain a feature
- Check that the premise contains a protagonist with a clear problem
- A premise that depends on coincidence is structurally fragile

## Scene-level checks
- Does this scene arise naturally from the premise?
- Is the central conflict of this scene traceable to the premise?

## Project-level checks
- Does the premise generate enough story engine to fill 90–120 minutes?
- Is the premise internally consistent with the world rules established?

## Warning signs
- Premise that requires 10 minutes of exposition to explain
- Premise whose central conflict is resolved too easily
- Premise dependent on a character making an obviously stupid decision

## Strong recommendation patterns
- If a project feels directionless, return to the premise and test it
- For sequels, check that the premise is genuinely new, not just more of the same

## Example assistant output
> Your premise — 'a detective with amnesia investigates her own disappearance' — is strong because it creates inherent conflict between the investigator and the investigation. The problem is your Act 2 stops using the amnesia as a story engine. By page 60, she's just a detective. Get the premise back into every act.
