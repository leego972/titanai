# Assembly Cut

## Purpose
The assembly cut is the first pass of the film — all selected takes cut together in script order, no music or effects, no trimming beyond basic scene structure.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- The assembly cut is always too long — this is normal and expected
- Its purpose is to reveal what you have, not to show what the film will be
- The assembly cut often reveals structural problems that weren't visible in the script

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Set expectations correctly: the assembly cut is diagnostic, not presentable
- Schedule private assembly cut screenings — no crew, no producers
- After assembly, identify the three biggest problems before making any cuts

## Scene-level checks
- What is the assembly cut revealing about this scene's actual effectiveness?
- What was planned vs what was captured?

## Project-level checks
- Is the assembly cut used as a diagnostic tool before editing decisions are made?
- Is the director approaching the assembly cut with the right expectations?

## Warning signs
- Directors who react emotionally to the assembly cut and start cutting randomly
- Producers or stakeholders viewing the assembly cut
- Cutting individual scenes before understanding the structural problems

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Screen the assembly cut alone, take notes, sleep on it before making any decisions
- After assembly, create a problem list before picking up the scissors

## Example assistant output
> Your assembly cut is 3 hours 12 minutes. Don't panic — this is completely normal. Before you cut a single frame, make a list of the three biggest structural problems. Fix those structural problems first. If you start trimming random scenes for running time without addressing structure, you'll end up with a 2-hour film that still has all three problems.
