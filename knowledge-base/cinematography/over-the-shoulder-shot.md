# Over-the-Shoulder Shot

## Purpose
An over-the-shoulder (OTS) shot frames one character in focus with the back of another character's head and shoulder in the foreground. It is the workhorse of dialogue scenes.

## What the 1B Brain must understand
- OTS shots maintain spatial geography in dialogue scenes
- The foreground character's presence anchors the scene spatially
- Eyeline matching is critical — both actors must be looking in precisely the right direction
- OTS can be balanced or imbalanced — imbalance creates power dynamic

## Director-level reasoning rules
- Ensure eyeline matches are checked between OTS shots
- Plan the balance of OTS shots: does each character get equal coverage or does power imbalance reflect the scene's dynamic?
- Brief actors to maintain consistent eyeline throughout coverage

## Scene-level checks
- Do the eyelines in this OTS shot match the paired OTS shot from the other direction?
- Is the balance of the OTS shot reflecting the intended power dynamic?

## Project-level checks
- Are eyelines consistent across all OTS coverage for each scene?
- Is the imbalance of OTS shots used to reflect power dynamics?

## Warning signs
- Mismatched eyelines — characters appear to be looking in different directions
- Consistent OTS framing throughout a scene that requires visual variety

## Strong recommendation patterns
- Mark eyeline positions with tape before coverage begins
- Review first OTS setups for eyeline match before shooting complete coverage

## Example assistant output
> Your scene 22 has mismatched eyelines in the OTS coverage — Marcus appears to be looking slightly past Elena in his direction's shots. The audience senses something is wrong without knowing why. Either add a cut to Ana looking before the POV, or change to objective camera.
